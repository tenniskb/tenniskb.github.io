# Tier 2 — Topic depth gaps (2026-08-24)

These are topics the repo already mentions, but the books carry the
structured detail (annual plans, formal curricula, named methodologies,
historical data) that the repo doesn't have. Each Tier 2 gap is a
*deepening*, not a new topic.

## 1. German Tennis Federation (BTV/VDT) periodized annual plan

**Books in library on this topic:** 1
**Source:** `Tennis_course_Vol2_Lessons_and_Training.pdf`
**Suggested repo location:** `reference-library/training-programs/German-Tennis-Federation-Annual-Plan.md`

### What the book says

The BTV/VDT tennis course, Vol. 2 (Lessons and Training), lays out a
structured yearly training plan with:

- Macrocycle → mesocycle → microcycle breakdown
- Specific on-court / off-court ratio percentages
- Load-progression rules across the year
- Tournament-period vs preparation-period differentiation

### Why the repo doesn't have this

The repo has a "Periodization" article (3 books in the library overlap),
but it covers the conceptual macro/meso/micro framework without a concrete
worked example. The BTV/VDT plan is a ready-made worked example.

## 3. Vic Braden's 50-50-50 club rule / laugh-and-win mentality

**Books in library on this topic:** 1
**Source:** `2. Vic Braden's Laugh and Win at Doubles_complete_vi.md`
**Suggested repo location:** new section `reference-library/coauthored-books/Vic-Braden-Laugh-and-Win.md`

### What the book says

Braden's 50-50-50 rule: 50 % of the time you should be laughing, 50 %
of the time you should be learning, and 50 % of the time you should be
playing — the percentages don't add to 100 because the categories overlap.

Braden's doubles philosophy: doubles is a *social* game; the team that
enjoys each other's company wins more than the team with the better
groundstrokes. Practical patterns include the "I-formation," the
"Australian," and the "back-poach."

### Why the repo doesn't have this

The repo has doubles formations (11–101 books in the library overlap)
but not Braden's specific fun-based philosophy. Worth a one-pager.

## 4. Oscar Wegner's "Simplify the stroke" / "Becker method"

**Books in library on this topic:** 1
**Source:** `Play_Better_Tennis_in_Two_Hours_ Simplify - Oscar Wegner.pdf`
**Suggested repo location:** new section `reference-library/coauthored-books/Oscar-Wegner-Simplify.md`

### What the book says

Wegner's central thesis: *"forehand = high-C"* — a simplified
biomechanics model that the everyday player can use without learning the
full kinetic chain. Boris Becker's rise to world #1 is cited as the
testament.

The "Becker method" is Wegner's term for the same approach: low-effort,
high-C forehand mechanics, designed to be playable by adults who started
tennis late.

### Why the repo doesn't have this

The repo's biomechanics articles are technical (kinetic chain, joint
angles, etc.). Wegner's simplification is a useful counterweight for the
adult-onset player. Worth a one-pager.

## 5. Tao of Tennis / Soft Science of Tennis / Zen Tennis

### What the books say

Three books approach tennis mental training from Eastern-philosophy

  choking.

mindfulness / acceptance-based approaches), but doesn't carry these
specific Eastern-philosophy framings.

## 6. Daniel Coyle's Talent Code — deep-practice rules

**Books in library on this topic:** 1 (referenced elsewhere)
**Source:** `The_Talent_Code.pdf`; supplemented with web research on Coyle's framework
**Suggested repo location:** new article `practice/Deep-Practice-Rules.md`

### What the book says (extended)

Coyle's deep-practice framework rests on three (not four) canonical rules:

1. **Rule 1 — Chunk It.** (a) Absorb the whole task; (b) break into the smallest possible pieces; (c) play with time — slow down to attend to errors, then speed up.
2. **Rule 2 — Repeat It.** Attentive repetition at the **edge of your ability**. 3–5 hours/day is the human ceiling for deep practice; world-class skill ≈ **10,000 hours** (≈3 hr/day × 10 yr) of deep practice.
3. **Rule 3 — Learn to Feel It.** Detect errors in real time ("feel" them); metacognition at the edge of capability.

### Myelin science (the substrate of skill)

- Every precise thought/movement/feeling is an electrical signal travelling along a chain of neurons.
- **Myelin is universal** — the mechanism is identical regardless of skill domain.
- **Myelin only wraps, never unwraps** — once a circuit is insulated it stays insulated (barring age/disease).
- **Myelin grows in waves that peak before age 30** and decline thereafter — a key reason early deliberate practice is so impactful.
- Signal metaphor: an unmyelinated circuit is "dial-up"; a myelinated one is "broadband" — bandwidth = speed + accuracy + timing.

### Direct quotes

> "Deep practice is built on a paradox: struggling in certain targeted ways — operating at the edges of your ability, where you make mistakes — makes you smarter." — Coyle, p. 18

> "It's not how fast you can do it; it's how slow you can do it correctly." — Coyle, on chunking

### Why the repo doesn't have this

The repo has `Myelination` (3 books in the library overlap on
sensorimotor learning / myelination), but doesn't carry the *three
deep-practice rules* as a structured framework. Coyle's rules are the
practical companion to the myelination science.

## 7. Chasing Points (Gregory Howe)

**Books in library on this topic:** 1
**Source:** `Chasing_Points.pdf`
**Suggested repo location:** supplement `tactics/Pro-Tour-Tactical-Patterns.md`

### What the book says

A full-season diary from a mid-ranked ATP tour player. Concrete tactical
patterns observed week-to-week:

- When to attack vs defend on second-serve returns
- How to read the opponent's body language between points
- Why the third shot of the rally is the most tactically loaded
- How to manage momentum swings across a three-set match

### Why the repo doesn't have this

The repo has tactics articles but lacks the *narrative-grounded* approach.
Howe's diary is the kind of source that supplies the "what does this look
like in week 14 of the tour" texture that tactical frameworks lack.

## 8. Greg Rusedski's 240 km/h serve (1998)

**Books in library on this topic:** 1 (cited in aerodynamics paper)
**Source:** `Review_of_tennis_ball_aerodynamics.pdf` (footnote)
**Suggested repo location:** supplement existing `serve/Eight-Stage Serve Model`

### What the book says

Rusedski's 1998 serve (recorded at 240 km/h, or ~149 mph) is cited as a
historical data point for "where serve speed has plateaued." Top-10 ATP
servers in 2024-2026 rarely exceed 230 km/h sustained; the 240 km/h mark
remains the high-water reference for nearly three decades.

### Why the repo doesn't have this

The serve article has the *mechanics* (8-stage model) but not the
*historical speed data*. A 100-word insert closes the gap.
