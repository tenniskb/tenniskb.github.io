# Chapter 4: Blitz Chess on Concrete
## Why the First Four Shots Decide the Match

---

I get asked this a lot by parents watching their kid grind out beautiful, twenty-shot rallies from the center of the court: "Isn't that what tennis is supposed to look like?"

Here's the thing — it isn't. Not at the level that actually wins matches.

A tennis analyst named Craig O'Shannessy, who later became Novak Djokovic's own tactical strategist, went back and studied over half a million points from Grand Slam tournaments. Wimbledon, the US Open, Roland Garros, the Australian Open — all of it. And what he found should change how every player at every level trains.

Nearly **seventy percent** of all points in professional tennis end within the first four shots. Not four minutes. Four shots. Another twenty percent wrap up by shot eight. That leaves only about **one point in ten** that turns into the long, grinding baseline battle everyone loves to imagine when they picture "real tennis."

Break those first four shots down and you get a simple sequence: the serve, the return, the server's first groundstroke after the serve — what coaches call **Serve+1** — and the returner's first groundstroke after that, or **Return+1**.

Now think about how most players actually spend their practice time. Cooperative crosscourt rallies from the middle of the court, back and forth, comfortable pace, twenty balls deep. That's rehearsal for the one scenario out of ten that barely happens in a real match. Meanwhile the four shots that decide most points get almost no dedicated practice at all.

If you want to win more matches, you don't need to get better at grinding. You need to become dangerous in the opening exchange. That's what this chapter is about.

---

### Serve+1: Setting Up Your Third Shot Before You Even Toss the Ball

Here's a mindset shift that changes everything: the serve was never really about the ace. At the pro level, servers rarely expect to end the point on the serve itself. What they're actually doing is **setting up shot three**.

Watch Carlos Alcaraz or, in his prime, Roger Federer, serve wide out to the deuce court. The goal isn't to blow the returner off the court — it's to pull them a few meters wide of the singles line, so wide that whatever comes back is a weak, floating ball into the middle of the court. The instant that return leaves the returner's racket, the server is already running around their backhand, hunting their favorite weapon: the inside-out forehand.

That third shot — Serve+1 — is where the real damage gets done. And there are three ways to finish it off:

**The open-court laser.** The returner just got dragged wide and hasn't recovered yet. You simply hit into the open space they vacated. Clean, direct, brutally effective.

**The wrong-foot trap.** This one's sneakier and, frankly, more satisfying to execute. The returner is sprinting back toward the center of the court to recover position — their whole body weight is moving in one direction. Instead of hitting into the open court, you hit it *behind* them, into the space they just left. Reversing direction at full sprint takes real physical effort, and most players simply can't do it in time. You'll see their balance collapse completely.

**The dipping short angle.** Instead of power, you use heavy topspin to drop the ball short and wide into the service box, dragging your opponent forward and off the court entirely — the opposite problem from the wrong-foot trap, but just as disruptive.

The lesson for your own game: before you ever toss the ball up, know where you want your third shot to land. The serve is the setup. Shot three is the finish.

### Return+1: Why the Middle of the Court is Your Best Friend

So if the server is trying to set a trap on shot three, what should you be doing on shot two — the return itself?

I see amateur players make the same costly mistake constantly: facing a fast first serve, they try to rip a low-percentage winner down the line. Most of the time it either sails wide or clips the highest part of the net. It's an ambitious shot that almost never pays off.

The players who understand tennis at a deeper level do something almost boring by comparison — and it wins them far more points. They aim roughly eighty percent of their returns off a big first serve deep down the **middle** of the court, into that corridor between the service line hash marks.

It sounds unglamorous. It's devastatingly effective, for three reasons.

First, it takes away the server's angles. A ball you send crosscourt or down the line gives your opponent a wide range of directions to redirect it — real estate they can use to hurt you. A ball sent straight back down the middle collapses that range dramatically. The server simply doesn't have anywhere dangerous to send it.

Second, it jams their setup. A deep ball aimed right at the server's body prevents them from stepping around into that big, open-stance forehand they were hoping to load up on Serve+1. Instead, they're stuck backpedaling, forced to hit a neutral, defensive shot instead of the weapon they planned.

Third, it's simply the safest shot on the court. You're clearing the net at its lowest point, dead center, and you have the entire length of the court to land it in. Fewer unforced errors, more pressure on your opponent. When you're not sure what to do with a tough return, the middle is almost never the wrong answer.

### Wardlaw's Directionals: the Simplest Rules in Tennis

A collegiate coach named Paul Wardlaw put together one of the cleanest, most useful frameworks in the sport — a set of rules about which direction to hit the ball based on which direction it's coming from. Once you internalize these, a huge percentage of your unforced errors simply disappear.

**Rule one: a crosscourt ball should go back crosscourt.** When a ball arrives at you on a diagonal, it's already traveling across your body in that direction. Redirecting it down the line means fighting that natural momentum, striking it earlier, and changing its path sharply — a low-percentage move under any kind of pressure. Sending it back crosscourt, on the other hand, means you're swinging with the ball's natural line of travel, over the lowest part of the net, into the longest diagonal on the court. It's the path of least resistance, and it's usually your best option.

**Rule two: a down-the-line ball can go anywhere.** When the ball comes straight at you, you have options. You can drive it right back down the line, or redirect it crosscourt — either way, you're in control, because the ball isn't fighting your swing path.

**Rule three: never change direction on a ball that's crossed behind your body.** If you're late to a ball and it's already passed behind your lead hip by the time you make contact, trying to go down the line is close to a guaranteed error — usually a shank off the frame. In that spot, just roll it back crosscourt with some topspin, reset, and live to fight the next ball.

Three rules. Learn them, trust them, and a whole category of unforced errors just goes away.

### Not All Points Are Created Equal

Tennis has a strange scoring system — you don't just win the most points, you have to win the right points, in the right sequence, to take a game. And that means some points matter enormously more than others.

Here's a number worth sitting with: winning a single point at 30-30 swings your probability of winning that game from roughly fifty percent to over eighty percent. That's the leverage packed into one point. Compare that to a point at 40-0, which barely moves the needle at all — you were already heavily favored to win the game regardless.

And this is exactly where amateur players get it backwards. Under the pressure of 30-30 or break point at 30-40, most players instinctively pull back. They go passive, start pushing the ball, and hope their opponent misses first. It's a completely understandable impulse — and it's the wrong one. Playing passively on break points has been shown to tank your conversion rate significantly.

Watch the truly great clutch competitors — Nadal, Serena, Djokovic — and you'll notice they do the opposite under pressure. On the biggest points, they lean into their favorite pattern and execute it with total conviction. Not recklessly — but without hesitation. That's the habit worth building in your own game: when the point matters most, trust your best shot and go get it, rather than shrinking and hoping.

### Winning Ugly: Brad Gilbert's Art of Rhythm Sabotage

Brad Gilbert, a former top-five player in the world, wrote a book called *Winning Ugly* that's become something of a bible for players who know they're not the most physically gifted person on the court. His central idea is simple, and it's one of my favorite pieces of tennis wisdom:

*If your opponent loves rhythm, don't give them rhythm. Give them ugly.*

Picture a big, powerful ballstriker — someone who crushes flat, fast balls at a comfortable waist-height rhythm all day long. If you match their pace, you're playing right into the rhythm that makes them dangerous. You're doing their job for them.

Instead, break it up:

**Throw in the moonball.** A high, loopy, heavy topspin ball that clears the net by feet, not inches, and pushes your power-hitting opponent well behind the baseline, out of their comfortable strike zone.

**Slice it low.** A biting underspin ball that skids through low, forcing a tall player to bend deep on every single shot — physically uncomfortable and rhythm-destroying in a way pace alone never is.

**Mess with the tempo.** Take your full time between points. Vary your service toss and rhythm. Sneak in a drop shot when they're expecting pace. None of these things are flashy. All of them chip away at an opponent who's built their entire game around a rhythm you refuse to give them.

You don't have to out-hit someone to beat them. Sometimes the winning move is simply refusing to play their game.

### Building a Player Over Ten Years, Not Ten Weeks

I want to zoom out for a moment, because everything above is about winning points — but developing a player is a much longer game. Real, lasting tennis ability isn't built in a single off-season. It's closer to a ten-year process.

Broadly, that development moves through five stages:

Roughly **ages six to nine**, the priority isn't tennis technique at all — it's general athleticism. Multi-sport activity, agility, balance, and above all, fun. Kids who specialize too early and too narrowly often burn out or develop lopsided, injury-prone movement patterns.

Roughly **ages nine to twelve**, the fundamentals of stroke mechanics start to take shape — how the body links together to generate spin and power, the foundational patterns that everything later gets built on.

Roughly **ages twelve to fifteen**, kids hit their growth spurt — what's technically called Peak Height Velocity. This is a stage that trips up a lot of well-meaning coaches. As limbs lengthen rapidly, sometimes inches in just a few months, a kid's whole sense of their own body changes. Movements that were fluid six months ago suddenly look clumsy. I want to be really clear about this, because I've seen it misjudged constantly: this awkwardness is not lost talent. It's biology. A good coach recognizes growth-spurt awkwardness for what it is, lightens the racket if needed, and keeps the focus on core stability and body awareness until things settle back down.

Roughly **ages fifteen to eighteen**, that's when the tactical material in this chapter — the first-strike, 0-to-4-shot thinking — really becomes the focus, alongside structured power training and building real mental toughness under pressure.

And **eighteen and beyond** is about fine-tuning everything for tour-level competition — the small margins, the recovery science, the last layer of polish that separates a good player from a great one.

If you're coaching or raising a young player, the single most important thing to take from this is patience with the process — especially around that growth-spurt stage. Awkward hands at fourteen do not mean lost talent. They mean a body that's still under construction.

### The Coach's Eye: Common Tactical Mistakes and How to Fix Them

| What Goes Wrong | What It Costs You | How to Fix It |
|---|---|---|
| **The Grinder Trap** — rallying without a plan | You burn energy on long rallies while quietly losing the battle in the first four shots, where most points are actually decided | Pick your Serve+1 target *before* you toss the ball. Every single time. |
| **Chasing down-the-line winners off crosscourt balls** | Several needless unforced errors handed away every set | Trust Wardlaw's rule: crosscourt stays crosscourt until you get a genuinely short ball to attack |
| **Pushing on break points** | A real drop in how often you actually convert your chances | Attack the second serve, step inside the baseline, go after your best pattern with conviction |
| **Feeding pace to a power hitter** | They settle into their comfortable rhythm and start playing their best tennis | Break their rhythm — moonballs, low slices, sudden changes of pace |

### On-Court Drills: Training the First Four Shots

**Drill 1 — The 4-Shot Kill Game.** Play tiebreak-style games to ten points, with one rule bolted on: if a rally goes past four shots without a winner or a forced error, both players score zero on that point. It sounds harsh, but it works fast — it rewires your brain to actually hunt for aggressive Serve+1 and Return+1 patterns instead of drifting into comfortable, passive rallying.

**Drill 2 — The Middle-Third Target Box.** Mark out a two-by-two-meter box right on the center hash of your opponent's baseline. Then receive twenty first serves, aiming to land at least fourteen of your twenty returns inside that box. It builds exactly the return depth and consistency you need to shut down a server's Shot-3 attack — and it does it under the kind of repetition pressure that actually sticks.
