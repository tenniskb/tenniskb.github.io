# Chapter 16: The Synchronized Mind
## Doubles Formations, Poaching, and Playing as One

---

I've coached a lot of singles players who step onto a doubles court and immediately look lost. They rally fine, they move fine, but the partnership itself doesn't work — two individuals standing on the same side of the net instead of one team. Here's the thing I want you to understand before anything else in this chapter: doubles isn't singles with a partner. It's a completely different sport that happens to use the same court and the same ball. The skill that separates good doubles teams from great ones isn't racquet skill at all — it's synchronization. Two bodies moving like they're tied together, reading each other without talking, closing off the same space at the same moment. That's what this chapter is about.

### The Golden Rule: Get to the Net, Together

If I could only teach you one thing about doubles, it would be this: the team that gets both players inside the service line, moving forward together, wins the overwhelming majority of points. The numbers back this up in a big way — teams with both players advanced past the service line win around 72% of points. Drop one player back at the baseline and that number falls off a cliff to under 50%. Leave both of you stuck at the baseline and you're down around 26% — you're basically losing before the point starts.

So why doesn't everyone just rush the net every time? Fear, mostly. Getting passed or lobbed feels embarrassing, and staying back feels safe. But safe is exactly the problem. Doubles is won in the air, at the net, where you can angle balls away and finish points in one shot. Every service game, every return, every rally ball you hit should have one question behind it: *does this get us closer to the net together?*

**Think of it like a rope tied around both your waists.** Not a real rope — a mental one, about ten feet long, that keeps you connected to your partner no matter what's happening on court. When your partner gets pulled wide to cover the alley, that rope should be yanking you toward the center T to plug the hole they just left. When your partner sees a short ball and steps in to attack it, you should already be stepping in too, without looking, without being told. The moment that rope goes slack — the moment you drift more than ten or twelve feet apart — you've opened a gap right down the middle, and good opponents will find it every single time.

### When the Returner is Killing You: the I-Formation and the Australian

Every team eventually runs into a returner who just crushes their crosscourt return — dips it low, hits it hard, and your net player can't get a racquet on it no matter where they stand. Standard doubles positioning doesn't have a great answer for this. That's where two specialized formations come in.

**The I-Formation** is built around disguise. Instead of standing in their normal spot, your net player crouches down low right on the center service line, head below the height of the net tape, blocking the returner's usual sightline to the server. Before the toss goes up, the two of you agree on a plan with a silent signal behind the net player's back — maybe a finger pointing means "I'm poaching to the deuce side," a closed fist means "I'm going to the ad side," and an open palm means "I'm faking, stay put." The instant the server makes contact, the net player explodes out of that crouch along the planned line, cutting the return off well above the net for a putaway before the returner even knows what happened.

The value here is almost entirely psychological. You're not winning the point with better strokes — you're winning it by taking away the returner's visual reference points and making them guess. A returner who's used to seeing the same two dots on the court every time suddenly has no idea where either of you will end up, and guessing under pressure produces mis-hits.

**The Australian Formation** works differently. Here, the server and the net player stand on the *same* half of the court instead of opposite halves. This closes off the returner's favorite crosscourt lane entirely and forces them to go down the line instead — over the highest part of the net, into the smallest part of the court, right where your alley coverage is waiting. If a returner's forehand crosscourt is beating you, the Australian formation is often the cleanest fix you can make without changing a single stroke.

### Poaching the Right Way: Go Forward, Not Sideways

Most club players who try to poach fail for the same reason — they run sideways, parallel to the net, chasing the ball along its flight path. That feels natural, but it's exactly backwards. Running sideways keeps you the same distance from the net the whole time, which means you're intercepting the ball late and low, often below the tape, where you have no angle and no power.

Elite poachers do something different: they cut forward, at roughly a 45-degree angle toward the net strap, the moment the server makes contact. That single change — angling forward instead of drifting sideways — does two things for you. First, it closes the gap to the ball dramatically faster, because you're now attacking the ball's path instead of chasing it. Second, and more importantly, it lets you meet the ball much higher — often over a foot above the tape, right near the top of its arc — instead of scrambling for it down around your shins. From up there, you're not blocking a volley back into play; you're punching it downward at the opposing net player's feet, and there's very little they can do about that.

If you remember nothing else about poaching, remember this: forward beats sideways, every time.

### Fixing the Middle: Whose Ball is It?

Ask any doubles pair what costs them the most free points and most will eventually admit it's the same thing — a ball comes screaming down the middle, both players glance at each other, both hesitate, and the ball lands untouched between them. This isn't a skill problem. It's a communication failure, and it's completely fixable with one rule.

**Whoever's forehand faces the middle owns every ball that comes down the middle. Period.** No debate, no hesitation, no politely leaving it for your partner. If your forehand is the one pointed at the center strap, that's your ball, and you should be calling "mine" out loud, early and often, so there's never a moment of doubt. This is exactly why left-handed and right-handed partners who both play with their forehand in the middle — like the Bryan brothers did for years — create such a difficult wall to break down. There's no seam for opponents to exploit, because there's never any confusion about who's taking the ball.

Alongside this, good teams also stagger their positioning slightly rather than standing in a flat line. The player on the crosscourt side plays about a step deeper, while the player closer to the line side presses tighter to the net. That slight "V" shape naturally funnels balls toward your most dangerous player at the net instead of leaving both of you equally exposed.

### Common Doubles Breakdowns and How to Fix Them

| What Goes Wrong | Why It Happens | The Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You keep getting lobbed | Standing right on top of the net with your knees locked straight, no time to react | Hold your ground about eight to ten feet back from the net instead of hugging it, and stay ready to turn and track a smash |
| Balls die untouched down the middle | No agreed-upon rule for who takes center balls | Forehand-in-the-middle takes it, every time — call it loudly and early |
| Your poach keeps dying in the net | Running sideways and intercepting the ball low, below tape height | Cut forward at an angle instead of drifting sideways, and take the ball as high as you can |
| Your partner's body language is dragging the team down | Eye-rolls and sighs after missed volleys quietly poison the partnership | Build a simple ritual — a fist bump or racquet tap after every single point, win or lose — and stick to it without fail |

### On-Court Drills

**The Four-Player Firefight.** Put all four players at the net, everyone standing inside their own service box. The coach feeds one fast ball into the middle of the court and the four of you go at it in a continuous, no-bounce reflex battle — pure hands, pure reaction. First team to fifteen points wins. This drill will expose weak volley technique and slow hands faster than almost anything else you can do in practice.

**The I-Formation Gauntlet.** Set up a server and net player against a live returning team and run twenty straight points out of the I-formation. The net player must flash a real signal before every single serve — mix it up, roughly ten poaches, five fakes, and five stays, so the pattern never becomes predictable even to your own returner. The goal is to win at least fourteen of those twenty points through disguise and first-strike interception at net. If you're not hitting that number, the problem is usually timing the explosion out of the crouch, not the disguise itself — you're leaving too early or too late relative to contact.

### Closing the Set: the Ten-Point Tiebreak

When a third set comes down to a ten-point match tiebreak, most of the tactical nuance of a full match gets compressed into a handful of possessions, and small edges matter enormously.

The single biggest lever is the early mini-break. If you can win the first return point and then hold your own two service points, you've built a 3-0 lead — and teams that reach 3-0 in a ten-point breaker go on to win it roughly three times out of four. That means your focus in the opening points shouldn't be on flash, it should be on discipline: land a high first-serve percentage, avoid any double faults, and go after the body and the T rather than gambling on lines you might not have on a given day.

It also pays to be aggressive early with your formations. Deploying the I-formation on your first couple of return points, before opponents have had a chance to adjust their patterns, tends to pay off more than saving your tricks for later. And when the breaker tightens up at 8-8 or 9-8, this is exactly when passive teams start playing not to lose — waiting for a mistake instead of making something happen. Championship teams do the opposite. They call the poach, they take the net, and they go win the point on their own terms rather than hoping the other side hands it to them.

Doubles rewards courage more than any other part of tennis. Learn to trust your partner, close the net together, and take the middle without hesitation, and you'll win more of these tight moments than you lose.
