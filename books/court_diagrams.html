1. The "Wear Down the Backhand, Then Finish" Pattern

Brad Gilbert • Winning Ugly
Read Baseline Tactics
Core Strategic Analysis: Brad Gilbert stresses that you do not need to hit a thunderous winner straight from the baseline. Keep pressing the opponent onto the backhand side with safe, deep, high-topspin balls (1). Once their stamina and sense of balance are worn down, the opponent will surely reply with a short, weak ball (2) — that is the golden moment to step into the court and attack the wide-open space (3).

2. The Australian Formation & the Poaching Art of Stealing Points

Pat Blaskower • The Art of Doubles
Read The Art of Doubles
Core Strategic Analysis: The Australian Formation thoroughly neutralises the opponent's familiar cross-court return habit. By having the net player stand on the same half as the server, the cross-court angle is completely sealed off. The returner is forced to risk a down-the-line shot over the highest part of the net, creating the chance for the net player to read it and poach the point decisively.

3. The "Serve + 1" Attacking Pattern

Brent Abel • Pattern Play Tennis
Read Pattern Play Tennis
Core Strategic Analysis: In modern tennis, points are not finished by an ace but decided by the shot immediately after the serve (Serve + 1). The wide serve (1) drags the opponent far off the edge of the court. As soon as the opponent scrambles to return the ball over the net, you have already moved into the court and finish with a forehand into the vast space in the opposite corner (2).

4. The Net-Crossing Point & Shot Direction (Directionals)

Paul Wardlaw • Tactics in Tennis
Read Tennis Tactics
Core Strategic Analysis: The Wardlaw Directionals rule states: when the ball comes cross-court from the opponent, the safest direction with the highest success rate is always to return it cross-court (crossing the lowest part of the net and the longest stretch of court). You should only change direction down the line when you are in perfect balance and the ball has already crossed the centre of the net into your half.

5. Serving down the T narrows the return-angle cone

Talbert & Old · The Art of Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: The T serve is the cheapest tactical weapon in tennis: you do not need maximum speed, only precision. By putting the ball next to the centre line you erase the entire cross-court lane the opponent is used to, and at the same time you create for yourself a short, central ball to attack on the next shot.

6. Wide serve and the gap it opens

Talbert & Old · The Art of Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Talbert and Old stress that the value of a wide serve lies not in winning the point outright but in breaking the opponent's defensive position. Once the opponent is dragged outside the sideline the whole geometry of the court tilts your way: the return loses its power and the opposite half becomes a wide-open target.

7. The danger of the short serve

Talbert & Old · The Art of Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: In singles a short serve is one of the costliest tactical errors there is. A short ball gives the opponent time, height and angle — the three things a deep serve is supposed to take away. Talbert calls it a 'tactical disaster': you open the door for the opponent to attack with your own hands.

8. The body serve in doubles

George Lott · How to Play Winning Doubles
Tactical analysis: George Lott ranks the body serve among the 'three killer targets' in doubles. Unlike the wide serve or the T serve, it does not aim to win the point directly but to break the structure of the return: the opponent cannot rotate the shoulders, so the ball always comes back short and high — exactly what the net player is waiting for.

9. The I-Formation and the poach

Françoise Dürr · Doubles Strategy
Tactical analysis: The I-Formation inverts every cue the returner relies on. Instead of standing offset to cover one lane, the two players line up down the middle and force the opponent to decide while blind. Françoise Dürr stresses that the strength of the formation lies in the hesitation it plants in the opponent's mind.

10. The cross-court return — the safest lane

Françoise Dürr · Doubles Strategy (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Dürr explains why the cross-court return is almost always right: it gives you the most court to land in, takes the ball over the lowest part of the net in the middle, and never travels through the net player's volley zone. The down-the-line return, by contrast, is the shortest and most direct path to the opponent's racket.

11. The return at the server's feet

George Lott · How to Play Winning Doubles
Tactical analysis: This is the most highly rated return in the classic doubles literature. A ball at the feet turns the first volley — normally the serving team's strongest weapon — into a defensive shot. You do not need to hit a winner; you only need to take away the opponent's comfortable stance.

12. The defensive return lob

Talbert & Old · The Art of Doubles (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Talbert files the defensive lob among the 'escape shots' that every doubles player must own. The conditions are strict: the ball must be high enough to clear the net player's reach and deep enough to deny them a comfortable overhead. A short lob is a gift.

13. Chip and charge

Talbert & Old · The Art of Doubles (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: Chip and charge turns the return from a defensive shot into an opening attack. The condition is depth: the chip must land deep enough that the opponent cannot attack before you reach the net. Talbert calls it the fastest way to flip the momentum of a service game.

14. The kick second serve buys time to close the net

Serious Tennis · The Serving Arsenal
Tactical analysis: The second serve is usually written off as a shot that merely 'gets the ball in'. Modern tactics read it the other way round: a kick second serve carries a far bigger safety margin than a flat first serve and pushes the opponent back behind the baseline — precisely the two conditions a serve-and-volleyer needs.

15. The high finishing volley and the low neutralising volley

Modern Volley Handbook 2026 (Part II)
Tactical analysis: The 2026 volley handbook separates the two volleys by CONTACT HEIGHT rather than by power. A high volley is a finisher because the weight travels downward and the racket face closes; a low volley forces the face open to lift the ball over the net, so it can only ever be a neutraliser played deep. Confusing the two is the most common source of error for players new to the net.

16. The static wall — locked joints, no backswing

Modern Volley Handbook 2026 (Part I)
Tactical analysis: The idea of the 'static wall' explains why the modern volley has almost no backswing lag: the racket travels about 30 cm and all the power comes from rotating the shoulders rather than from the arm. Taking the racket back not only costs time, it tears open the court behind the net player — the hatched risk zone in the diagram.

17. The reflex defensive volley — a minimal block

Modern Volley Handbook 2026 (Part III)
Tactical analysis: The defensive volley is a pure reflex test: there is no time to rotate the shoulders, so the whole shot collapses into a minimal block — centre of gravity low, knees bent, racket face open. The goal is not to win the point but to send the ball deep enough to buy time to retreat into the correct defensive position.

18. The punishing overhead after a lob

Modern Volley Handbook 2026 (Part III)
Tactical analysis: A lob is only genuinely dangerous when the net player reacts late. Retreat with a crossover step and take the ball at its highest point, and the lob turns into an overhead — the highest-percentage finishing shot in tennis. The safe target is the empty left half, because the opponent is still standing in the corner they lobbed from.

19. The bisector position at the net

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.9)
Tactical analysis: Allen Fox reduces net positioning to pure geometry: the net player must stand on the bisector of the angle formed by the two possible passes. On the bisector both passes cover the same distance — you are never forced to guess. Step off it by even half a metre and you open a high-percentage lane for the opponent.

20. Inverted geometry: at the net you volley down the line

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.8)
Tactical analysis: This is one of the most interesting paradoxes of court geometry. From the baseline, cross-court gives you more court, a lower net and a longer margin — so it is the high-percentage shot. But once you are at the net, down the line becomes the correct choice: the shortest path, the net height no longer matters, and above all it does not open the cross-court angle for the opponent to counter into.

21. The correct approach shot and following it in

Pattern Play Tennis (approach patterns)
Tactical analysis: Pattern Play treats the approach as a PATTERN rather than a single shot: a deep down-the-line approach, a move forward behind the ball, and a first volley that establishes control. The three must fit together. A short approach, or one hit into the middle (the risk zone), is punished immediately with a passing shot.

22. The tandem formation — both players take the net

Doubles Tactics & Formations (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Tandem is the strongest attacking shape in doubles: two players at the net split the court laterally and leave no lane uncovered. The psychological pressure on the returner is enormous because every reply has to pass through a volley zone. In exchange, tandem demands perfectly synchronised movement — if one player drifts out of position the whole system collapses and the court behind them is exposed at once.

23. Cover the middle or cover the line — the net player's order of priority

Doubles Tactics & Formations (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: This is the law of percentages applied to the net player. A ball through the middle always has more court than a down-the-line ball, so it is the opponent's natural choice. The net player must therefore prioritise the middle and accept the open down-the-line lane — a lane the opponent has to hit almost perfectly to exploit. Getting the priority order wrong means making your mistake on the very shot the opponent finds easiest.

24. Two options from one position: the punch volley or the drop volley

Skills & Drills (Ch.10 — The art of coming in)
Tactical analysis: The art of the net is that one and the same volley can be a finisher or a surprise. The deep punch is the default because it is safe and still applies pressure; the drop only makes sense when the opponent has retreated deep and cannot change direction. The technical key: the punch volley generates power from the shoulders, the drop volley absorbs it with soft hands.

25. The cross-court rally — the pillar of every tactic

Pattern Play Tennis (Baseline patterns)
Tactical analysis: Pattern Play treats the cross-court rally not as "hitting back and forth to be safe" but as a deliberate PILLAR: it gives you the longest margin, the lowest net in the middle and the most court for the ball to land in. Every attacking pattern is built on this rally foundation — you hold the diagonal until the opponent leaves the ball short, and only then change direction.

26. When you are allowed to change direction down the line

Pattern Play Tennis (Changing direction down the line)
Tactical analysis: Changing direction down the line is the most dangerous shot in tennis because it crosses the highest part of the net and leaves only a narrow strip of court to land in. Pattern Play sets one clear condition for being allowed to change direction: the ball must be short and you must have stepped inside the court. Then the shot becomes a finishing blow instead of a self-inflicted error.

27. Recovering to the bisector after every shot

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: A shot is not over when the ball leaves the strings — it is over when you have recovered. Allen Fox calls the correct recovery position the "bisector": the point between the opponent's two possible return angles, where both directions are an equal distance from you. Standing off the bisector means you have to guess, and guessing always loses to probability.

28. The serve-plus-one pattern

Pattern Play Tennis (Serve-Plus-One)
Tactical analysis: Serve-plus-one is the most basic and most effective attacking pattern in modern tennis: the serve is not meant to win the point outright but to SET UP the second shot. Where the serve lands decides where the +1 goes. If you serve wide, the +1 goes into the open half; if you serve down the T, the +1 goes out wide. The two shots must be planned together, not improvised.

29. A return with a planned pattern

Pattern Play Tennis (The patterned return)
Tactical analysis: A high-level return is not a reflex but a PRE-SET PATTERN: you decide where the return will go before the opponent tosses the ball. The deep cross-court return is the default choice because it is geometrically safest and it pushes the opponent out of the serving position — where they are strongest. Your second shot is already planned to follow that return.

30. Reading and breaking the opponent's habitual pattern

Pattern Play Tennis (Breaking the pattern)
Tactical analysis: A pattern is both a weapon and a weakness. When an opponent repeats a pattern often enough it becomes free information for you: you know where the ball is going BEFORE it leaves their strings. That is when you move a beat early and change direction into the lane they have just vacated. Conversely, you must always check whether your own pattern has been read.

31. Depth as a weapon — pinning the opponent behind the baseline

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Allen Fox calls depth a "double weapon": it both shortens the opponent's reaction time and extends yours. A deep ball forces the opponent to hit from behind the baseline, where they cannot generate power or angle — so the return is always shorter. A short ball dropping into the middle (the hatched zone) does the opposite: it invites the opponent to step in and attack.

32. Running around the backhand — inside-out and inside-in

Skills & Drills (Ch.2 — Inside-Out & Inside-In)
Tactical analysis: The run-around forehand turns a weakness into a weapon: instead of hitting a backhand from the left corner, you step around and attack with the forehand. The two options involve a clear trade-off — inside-out travels cross-court over the lowest part of the net, so it is safer; inside-in goes down the line, shorter but over the highest part of the net with only a narrow strip of court to land in.

33. Cross-court or down the line — a geometry comparison from the baseline

Technique & Tactics (Geometric play)
Tactical analysis: This is the fundamental geometry of tennis. A singles court is 8.23 m wide and 23.77 m long, so the diagonal is about 1.4 m longer than the down-the-line path — meaning a bigger safety margin. On top of that, the net is 15 cm lower in the middle, where every cross-court ball passes. Cross-court is therefore the default shot; down the line should only be used to change direction once you already have an advantage.

34. The grinding rally — depth and variation of spin

Technique & Tactics (The grinding rally)
Tactical analysis: A grinding rally is not hitting back and forth until someone makes a mistake — that is the game of a player with no weapons. The right way is to keep depth as the foundation, then VARY THE SPIN to break the rhythm: a short, dipping, low slice forces the opponent to lower their centre of gravity and hit up from below net height. A change of spin is more unsettling than a change of pace.

35. The attacking lob over the net player's head

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.12)
Tactical analysis: The lob is mistakenly seen as a defensive shot. Against a net player who is tight to the net, the topspin lob is in fact the most effective attacking shot because it attacks their one weakness: the space behind them. Two conditions are mandatory — the trajectory must be higher than their reach, and the landing point must be deep enough that they cannot get back in time to smash.

36. The defensive lob in doubles and the Pat and Mike footwork

Smart Doubles Tennis (Ch.7)
Tactical analysis: The lob is the most important defensive weapon in doubles, but also the most punishable shot if it lacks depth: a short lob in the middle of the court is a gift for the other net player's smash. Skip Singleton stresses the "Pat and Mike" footwork when retreating to cover a lob — the two players move back in a V shape, one covering the sideline and one covering the middle.

37. The down-the-line passing shot

Skills & Drills (Ch.11 — Passing Shot)
Tactical analysis: The down-the-line pass is the speed option: the shortest path, so the ball reaches the net player soonest and their reaction time is minimal. The price is a small margin — the ball must clear the highest part of the net at the sides and land in a narrow lane. That is why it should only be used once the net player has moved clearly to one side.

38. The cross-court passing shot

Skills & Drills (Ch.11 — Passing Shot)
Tactical analysis: The two passing shots are two trade-offs. Cross-court gives the biggest margin and crosses the lowest part of the net in the middle — the highest success rate, but the net player has more time to chase. Down the line gives less reaction time but a smaller margin and a higher net. The practical rule: cross-court by default, down the line only once the net player has moved clearly to one side.

39. The drop shot when the opponent is standing too deep

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.12)
Tactical analysis: The drop shot is a conditional shot: it only works when the opponent is behind the baseline. The second condition is disguise — the preparation must look identical to a normal shot, otherwise the opponent reads it and moves early. The third condition is the technique of absorbing pace: the ball must land just behind the service line and bounce low, not float gently into the middle of the court.

40. The backhand slice — the art of changing the tempo

Skills & Drills (Ch.5 — Backhand Slice)
Tactical analysis: The backhand slice is the cheapest change-of-rhythm shot in tennis: it needs no power, just an open racket face and a downward cut under the ball. Its value lies in the HEIGHT of the bounce — or rather, in its lowness: a dipping ball forces the opponent to lower their centre of gravity and hit up, breaking the topspin rhythm they had going. The slice is also the best preparation shot for coming to the net.

41. Attacking the second serve

Serious Tennis (Attacking-return tactics)
Tactical analysis: The second serve is the weakest shot in an entire game, because it must be both safe and difficult enough not to be hit for a winner. Serious Tennis sees it as the main attacking opportunity: step inside the baseline, take the ball early at a high contact point, and attack in the direction the opponent has just left. This change of position forces the opponent to hit a harder second serve — and therefore to double-fault more often.

42. Neutralising the serve-and-volley player

Talbert · Tennis Tactics (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Serve-and-volley is a strong tactic, but it has one inherent weakness: a player moving towards the net cannot hit a low volley properly. Talbert therefore ranks the return at the feet as the number-one priority — short, low and landing right in their advancing stride. A high lob (the hatched zone) is a poor choice: it gives them a high volley to finish with.

43. The half-volley — the reflex pick-up off the bounce at close quarters

Technique & Tactics (The Half-Volley)
Tactical analysis: The half-volley is the harshest test of balance and reflex in tennis: you must hit the ball at the very instant it bounces, while still moving forward. The technique comes down to three things — bend the knees deep so your eyes are level with the ball, open the racket face to lift the ball over the net, and never swing. The only goal is to send the ball back deep, not to win the point.

44. Facing the thunderbolt server — the block return

Tennis Beyond the Cannonball Serve (Part 2)
Tactical analysis: Against a thunderbolt server the return is not an attacking shot but a SURVIVAL shot. At 200 km/h you have about 0.4 seconds — not enough to turn your shoulders. So the return must be compressed into a block: racket compact in front of the chest, racket face fixed, just redirecting. The only goal is to return deep enough to get the ball back in play — every short return is punished immediately on the next shot.

45. The Australian formation — the net player stands in the middle to close the centre

Doubles Tactics & Formations (The Australian formation)
Tactical analysis: The Australian formation is a small positional change that produces a big change in probability: the net player shifts to the middle of the court, closing the centre lane — where the most dangerous cross-court return travels. The opponent is pushed into two equally bad options: return down the line (the ball the net player is waiting for) or return into the middle (where the net player is standing). In exchange, the serving team's right half is temporarily exposed.

46. Tandem scissors — both players crossing at once

Doubles Tactics & Formations (Tandem Scissors)
Tactical analysis: Tandem scissors is the hardest variation of net formations: both players move diagonally at once, cutting past each other and swapping sides completely. Its strength is that the opponent never knows which lane is closed — because the answer keeps changing. The risk matches: if the two players get out of sync, half the court opens up while they are moving.

47. The poach — spotting the golden moment

Smart Doubles Tennis (Ch.8)
Tactical analysis: Skip Singleton sees the poach as the skill that separates a good doubles player from a great one — because the net player can win the point without touching the ball in the early exchanges. The secret lies in READING the opponent's swing: the golden moment is when they have committed to a direction and cannot change their mind. A mandatory condition is that the partner must move across immediately — otherwise you trade one closed lane for two open ones.

48. Synchronised movement — the two players move as one block

Smart Doubles Tennis (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: The principle of tandem movement says the two players at the net must move as ONE BLOCK: the distance between them stays constant, and only the position of the whole block changes. When one player shifts while the other stands still, a lane opens up immediately — and at a high level that lane exists for just one beat, which is enough to lose the point.

49. The three-shot sequence through the transition zone

Smart Doubles Tennis (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: The transition zone (between the service line and the net) is the most dangerous place on the court — but also where the battle is decided. Singleton describes a textbook three-shot sequence for crossing it: the deep approach, handling the low return at your feet, then the first volley. The golden rule that comes with it: be still at contact — hitting while running is the most common error in this area.

50. The net player lobbed — the coordinated retreat

Doubles Tactics & Formations (When lobbed)
Tactical analysis: A lob over the net player is the harshest test of a pair's understanding. The most common mistake is both players chasing the same ball — leaving the entire other half open for the next smash. The coordinating rule: the lobbed player chases the ball and replies with a deep lob; the other retreats at once to cover the opposite half, forming a V.

51. High-percentage tennis — hitting into the biggest open space

Tennis Beyond the Cannonball Serve (High-percentage geometry)
Tactical analysis: High-percentage tennis is not about playing safe — it is about choosing the shot with the highest TOTAL probability, counting both your chance of landing it and the opponent's chance of getting it back. The shot into the biggest open space satisfies both: the widest margin for you, the longest run for the opponent. That is why the first shot in the sequence need not win the point — it only needs to create the space.

52. Beating the pusher — patience, then attack

Think To Win · Allen Fox (Ch.13)
Tactical analysis: The pusher is frustrating because they take away what every attacking player needs: rhythm and pace to borrow. The common mistake is trying to hit harder than necessary and making your own errors. The right way is to accept a long rally, generate your own pace with your legs and body, keep depth — and patiently wait for the short ball. A pusher always has to leave the ball short at least once in every long rally.

53. Scouting the opponent, then exploiting the weakness found

Smart Doubles Tennis (Ch.9 — Scouting)
Tactical analysis: Scouting is not just for coaches — it is a live competitive skill. In the first few games you observe: where the opponent returns, which shot breaks down, what they like to hit under pressure. Once the weakness has surfaced, the rule is to exploit it until they prove they have fixed it — not just once or twice. It is relentlessly repeating the same direction that creates real pressure.

54. Five shots that build a winning point

Tennis Beyond the Cannonball Serve (Five shots)
Tactical analysis: A winning point at a high level rarely comes from one cannonball shot — it comes from a SEQUENCE of shots, each doing exactly one job. The serve creates a favourable return; the favourable return creates the chance to play the approach; the approach creates the point-ending volley. When you look at a point this way, you no longer need the perfect shot — you only need each shot to fulfil its function.

55. Time warfare — the six steps in the decision window

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 3 defines every shot as a window of time made up of six steps: recognition, anticipation, movement, preparation, execution and recovery. Applying pressure does not mean hitting harder — it means taking away the opponent's time at one of those six steps. A ball into the far corner attacks the "movement" step directly: the longest distance eats up the time that would have gone to the other steps. That is why the optimal shot is often not the fastest one, but the one that makes the opponent run the furthest.

56. The time window — four factors that set its size

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 3 identifies four factors that determine the size of the time window: ball speed, length of journey, bounce height and the receiver's position. This turns "creating time" and "taking away time" into two controllable levers. A slow, high, short ball inadvertently throws all four open at once — which is why it is treated as a serious error at a high level, whether or not it lands in.

57. Hitting on the rise — compressing time

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 3 calls on-the-rise the shot that changes the time equation. Technically it is harder — the ball has not reached the top of the bounce, so the margin is smaller and it demands a more compact swing. But spatially, the benefit is double: you shorten the opponent's time while claiming the court position for yourself. This is why attacking players willingly accept the technical risk in exchange for the time advantage.

58. The four dimensions of spatial pressure — width, depth, height, angle

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 5 describes every shot through four independent variables: width, depth, height and angle. They are not four mutually exclusive choices — every ball has a value on all four axes. Understanding this changes how you assess a shot: instead of asking "is this shot powerful?", we ask "which dimension is this shot buying, and which is it paying with?" A wide ball buys space but pushes you out of the central position; a high ball buys safety margin but sells back time.

59. Width + Depth — double pressure driving the opponent into the farthest corner

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 5 calls width + depth the most effective way to apply pressure. Used separately, each dimension has a counter-cost: hitting wide all the time also pushes you out of the central position. But when the two combine, the opponent is driven to the farthest corner behind the baseline — a place where no player can attack, and the best remaining option is a neutral return to recover. This is why width and depth must come in sequence, not stand alone.

60. Return position — adjusting to the server's style

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.11)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 11 elevates the return position to the level of a tactical decision: it determines the time you have to react, the kind of return you can play, and the message you send to the server. The three goals, in priority order, are to get the ball in, neutralise the serve, and only then attack — this order matters because most return errors come from jumping straight to the third goal. The right position is the tool for reliably achieving the first two.

61. Net position — the T zone, 2-3 m from the net

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.12)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 12 treats coming to the net as the strongest spatial action in tennis: from the net you cover most of the court, compress the opponent's time to under a second, and force them to play through a moving wall. But that advantage depends entirely on distance. Stand too close and even a short lob clears you; stand too far and the opponent has too many angles. The T zone — about 2-3 m behind the net — is the point where the distance to the forehand and backhand volley is equal, and a lob must be very high and long to be effective.

62. Expanding defensive space — the high, deep ball as a reset

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.13)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 13 defines strategic defence as the art of expanding space under pressure, rather than contracting. When driven into a corner, a short, medium ball opens up the next attacking chance for the opponent; a high, deep ball instead creates a "spatial reset": the long flight time buys 1-2 seconds of recovery, and the depth pushes the opponent back behind the baseline. An effective defensive ball therefore needs 3-5 m of net clearance with topspin to bring the ball down deep — not just to "land in".

63. One-handed and two-handed backhand — reach traded for stability

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.9)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 9 analyses the backhand as a spatial system with its own function, not a "weak wing" to be protected. Two hands buy temporal compression — the ability to hit later while still generating power, and therefore to disguise direction longer — along with strength in pressured situations, at the cost of shorter reach. One hand buys reach, the variety of the slice and the ability to create angles, at the cost of stability against a high ball into the body. No choice is free: each school buys one spatial dimension and pays in another.

64. Height manipulation — heavy topspin, then a surprise flat, low ball

Spatial Dynamics in Tennis (Ch.18)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 18 lists height manipulation as one of the core patterns of modern tennis. The mechanism is not hitting harder, but breaking the opponent's preparation: two or three heavy topspin balls train them into opening their stance and waiting high, then one flat, low ball forces them to drop their centre of gravity instantly. This is a textbook illustration of the book's overarching principle — the goal is not the prettiest shot, but the shot that puts the opponent in the hardest position.

65. Deuce and Ad court assignment — who stands on which side?

The Art of Doubles (Ch.1)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 1 opens with a portrait of a balanced pair built on four core qualities: the ability to anticipate, disciplined consistency, a supportive temperament and adaptability. But qualities alone are not enough — a doubles team must also answer the first organisational question: who stands on the Deuce court, and who on the Ad court? The answer cannot be arbitrary, because the two halves produce two completely different families of balls. The Deuce half absorbs most of the cross-court exchanges and the long returns; the Ad half is where the pivotal points happen and where the spun serve pulls the receiver off the court. The assignment must therefore be based on complementary strengths — who covers the sideline better, who stays calm at the decisive moments. The diagram shows the two halves mirrored across the centre line, with the two teams facing opposite ways: your Deuce court lies on the same side as the opponents' Ad court.

66. The standard attacking position — the net strap & the convex-mirror principle

The Art of Doubles (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 3 describes the ideal attacking position as two coordinated roles: the cross-courter and the finisher. The cross-courter keeps a distance of about 8-10 steps behind the finisher and aligns their body with the net strap — that is the reference position. The finisher advances closer to the net and positions by the "convex mirror" principle: always standing at the centre of the opponent's highest-probability reflection angles, like a convex mirror reflecting the whole space in front. The key point many recreational players get wrong: seeing two partners standing far apart in a staggered arrangement, they assume the middle is open. The reality is the opposite — by the geometry of angles, a ball into the middle lands exactly in the finisher's zone. The staggered distance does not create a hole; it is precisely what locks down the middle.

67. The defensive position — retreating deep to fill the gap and change state

The Art of Doubles (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 3 sets the defensive position in complete contrast to the attacking one. When forced off the net, the two players must not stand level with each other at the baseline — they must retreat deep and stagger to lock the middle, exactly the middle-locking principle of the attacking position but reversed. Alongside that is the ability to change state: a doubles team must know when to retreat and when to lob over the opponents' heads to reverse the momentum. This chapter stresses that defence is not a passive state of waiting but an organised structure: retreat deep to gain time, keep the ball safely over the net, and wait for the right short ball to spring back to the net. A pair is only strong when it masters both states — and knows exactly when to switch between them.

68. The golden rule “Deep for deep, short for short”

The Art of Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 4 calls elite doubles "percentage tennis" — an arena not for reckless show shots but for the choices with the biggest safety margin over the net. The foundational rule is "deep for deep, short for short": when you are deep at the baseline and the opponent has just hit a deep ball, the mandatory goal is to return it deep. Trying a drop shot or a down-the-line winner from a deep position is a double mistake — low success rate and it opens a counter-attacking angle. Conversely, only when the ball drops short do you advance to the net to finish or drive it down at the opponents' feet. The diagram places the two situations side by side on the same court to show that your position decides what you are allowed to do — not the player's momentary inspiration.

69. High-percentage targets — the opponents' shoelaces & the V lane

The Art of Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 4 defines high-percentage targets as landing points that combine a big safety margin over the net with the highest statistical success rate. When the opponents have taken the net, two such targets stand out. The first is the net player's shoelaces: a ball dropping right at their feet forces them to drop the racket in an instant, turning the reply volley into a highly risky defensive shot. The second is the V-shaped sideline lane — the wide diagonal boring into the space between the net player and the baseline player, where no pair can cover in time. The key point is that neither target demands maximum speed or a line-hugging trajectory: they win through position and patience, exactly the percentage-tennis spirit this chapter champions.

70. The geometry of closing the net — squeezing every return angle

The Art of Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 4 presents closing the net as a purely geometric problem: every step towards the net narrows the cone of angles the opponents can use. Standing far from the net, they have a whole wide fan to play through; closing to the net, that fan shrinks into a tight cone, and most balls fall into the volley zone. This is why modern doubles treats taking the net as the central tactical goal: not to hit harder, but to take away the opponents' space. The diagram shows the inverse relationship between distance to the net and the openness of the return angle — the closer you get, the fewer options the opponents have, and every remaining option leads into your racket.

71. The textbook poach — driving in at 45° instead of running sideways

The Art of Doubles (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 5 calls the poach the most devastating weapon a net player has in doubles, but it also points out the most common mistake that trips up recreational players: they run sideways parallel to the net. Running sideways puts the contact point below the net, turning the volley into a defensive shot that easily finds the net. A textbook poach requires driving diagonally forward at 45° towards the opposite net post: the forward movement allows contact at the highest point above the net, using the whole body's momentum to drive the ball down into the court. Timing is also life-or-death — start only when the opponent's racket head has dropped into contact, no earlier, so as not to reveal your intention and not to be passed through the space you left.

72. Lobbed over the head — switching positions with the call “Switch!”

The Art of Doubles (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 6 sees the lob return as one of the most annoying tactics an opposing doubles team can use: it immediately neutralises an aggressive net player and forces the server to turn and chase in a defensive position. There are two layers to the counter. The preventive layer: the net player must not stand too close to the net when the partner is about to serve — keep a sensible distance, stay on the balls of the feet and be ready to bounce back a step. The reactive layer once the ball is over your head: the first survival rule is that the net player must NEVER run straight backwards — it is dangerous and leaves them completely blind to the play. Instead, the player behind calls out “Switch!” and retreats diagonally to cover, while the net player quickly moves to the opposite side to complete the court switch. Clear communication is what turns two separate individuals into an organised defensive system.

73. The Australian formation — sealing off the favourite cross-court lane

The Art of Doubles (Ch.8)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 8 sees flexibility as the most powerful tactical weapon when the original plan is read. The Australian formation is a textbook example: instead of splitting the two players into two halves as usual, the serving team deliberately stands to one side to seal off the returner's familiar cross-court lane. The receiver instantly loses their favourite ball and must choose one of two bad options: go down the line — harder but more open — or hit straight into the net player's interception zone. This is proof of the book's overarching principle: doubles tactics are not about playing better than the opponent, but about taking away the options they are best at. Conservatism and rigidity are the disease of mediocre pairs; changing the formation is the life raft when the rally stalls.

74. The I-formation — the net player crouching on the centre service line

The Art of Doubles (Ch.8)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 8 introduces the I-formation as the pinnacle of tactical flexibility: the net player crouches right on the centre service line, directly in front of the server, so the two form a vertical line. This stance utterly confuses the returner, who cannot predict which way the two opponents will split. After the serve, the serving team splits in opposite directions, stretching the court the opponents must defend to the maximum. This is the chapter's final lesson: flexibility demands absolute trust in yourself and your partner. A class doubles team, instead of getting frustrated as the opponents keep salvaging easy put-aways, calmly changes the trajectory of the ball, slows the tempo or changes the formation to disrupt the opponents' calculations — and the I-formation is the most powerful formation-changing weapon in that arsenal.

75. The four rotating roles within a doubles point

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 3 likens a doubles match to a play in which you take turns in four different roles. Each role comes with its own responsibilities and its own worries: the server must put the ball in play then advance at once; the server's partner must guard the down-the-line lane and be ready to poach; the receiver must return accurately from the least advantageous position; and the receiver's partner must choose between holding the net or retreating to cover. What separates a good doubles player from an ordinary one is not the technique of each shot but the ability to instantly recognise which role they are in and act according to its logic. The diagram marks the four standard positions of the four roles in a typical serving point, along with the two opening shots that shape the whole rally.

76. The "play the ball" principle — advance and take the net first

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.2)
Tactical analysis: The first and most important principle of modern doubles is proactivity: play the ball instead of letting the ball play you. In doubles, initiative is almost synonymous with net position, because a player at the net can attack two-thirds of the court, while a player at the baseline can only respond along narrow angles. The diagram shows the whole logic of this principle in three shots: a deep pressure shot to seize the initiative, a short return from opponents pushed deep, and a point-ending volley from the net. The key point is that both partners advance together — going to the net alone exposes a gap, while advancing as a pair seals every lane.

77. Controlling the middle lane

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.2)
Tactical analysis: One of the four foundational principles of modern doubles is controlling the middle of the court. In singles, the middle lane is the safest place because the net is lowest there and the court is longest; in doubles, that very lane becomes the most dangerous blind spot for the defending team. When two net players each guard their own side, the boundary between them is a thin strip no one wants to own. A good doubles player deliberately targets that thin strip: a ball into the middle makes both hesitate half a step, and half a step of hesitation is enough to produce a short, high, uncontrolled return. The diagram marks the middle lane in red — target number one whenever you have a favourable ball.

78. The three-quarter-pace spin serve

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 4 argues that in doubles the flat-out serve is not the most effective weapon — the three-quarter-pace spin serve is. The reason lies in the fact that doubles rewards position rather than pure speed: the server needs time to advance to the net and needs a predictable return. A spin serve at three-quarter power creates both conditions — the ball travels slower for you, but kicks higher and heavier for the opponent, forcing them to handle it at shoulder height rather than hip height. The weak, loopy return becomes a gift for your first volley. This is the serve of the club-playing majority, not the preserve of the professionals.

79. The backhand slice return

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 5 calls the return the delicate shot of doubles. Unlike singles, the receiver in doubles stands on a wider expanse of court but has fewer targets: one half is guarded by the opponents' net player, so any tentative return within their reach gets put away. The solution is not to hit harder but to hit lower. The backhand slice produces a low, backspin ball dropping right at the feet of the server advancing to the net — forcing a volley from below in an off-balance position. This is a calculated defensive return: it does not win the point directly, but it strips the opponents of their biggest weapon, the first volley.

80. Stepping into the volley — hitting the ball in front

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 6 asserts that the volley is the heart of a varied doubles game: it is the weapon of both attack and defence, of surprise and of calculation. But the volley in doubles differs from the volley in singles in that it almost always comes with a footstep. Amateur players tend to stand still, wait for the ball and only then swing — producing a short, powerless volley struck at hip height or behind them. Good volleyers do the opposite: they step forward before the ball arrives, transfer their body weight into the shot and make contact in front of them. It is that footstep that creates the depth and accuracy the arm cannot produce on its own. The diagram marks the forward step and the ideal contact point of the ball.

81. The first volley compensates for a weak serve

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 6 offers one of the most liberating insights for club players: in doubles you do not need a powerful serve to win. The classic example is Ken Rosewall — the great Australian whose serve was relatively weak, but whose first volley had extraordinary accuracy and touch. In singles a weak serve is a weakness ruthlessly exploited; in doubles it is almost meaningless, because the first volley will do the rest of the work. The tactical logic is clear: instead of investing in serve speed, invest in the first volley — the shot that decides most of the points in modern doubles.

82. The lob and the overhead — a mandatory weapon pair

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.7)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 7 compares a doubles player weak in both the lob and the overhead to someone entering a chess game without rooks and half the pawns: naturally weaker in attack and therefore more vulnerable in defence. In singles you can play a creditable match without these two shots; in doubles you cannot. The lob is the weapon that breaks a net-controlling team — it forces the opponents to turn and run, handing the initiative back to you. The overhead is the most lethal shot in all doubles statistics, because the ball comes down at an angle no shot can defend. The two shots are inseparable: a good lob is always answered by another lob, and every lob is an overhead opportunity.

83. Building a balanced pair — forehand and backhand complementing each other

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.8)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 8 rejects the notion that two partners must be alike to click. In reality, two players of the same template often rub each other the wrong way on court no less than two very different ones. The real key lies in the balance of skills and the harmony of the emotional relationship. That balance begins with a very concrete decision: who stands on which side. Two right-handers are usually most effective when the player with the better forehand stands on the right (Deuce court) and the player with the best backhand stands on the left (Ad court). For a left-hander, standing in the advantage court (Ad) creates more problems for the opponents, because their backhand is almost hidden from the server's view. The diagram shows this arrangement along with the two characteristic ball families of each half.

84. Beating the other pair — exploiting weaknesses and gaps

Modern Doubles Tennis (Ch.11)
Tactical analysis: Chapter 11 defines doubles as a battle of wits: one collective strategy against another, with the smarter side usually winning. The ability to analyse the opponents' strengths and weaknesses and exploit that information during play matters no less than executing the basic shots correctly. The best way to get to know the opponents is to note in your mind how they play when you face them, then consciously assess their strengths and weaknesses as a team. Observe each player's weaknesses in baseline shots, volleys, lobs and overheads; analyse the first and second serve; note where the net player positions relative to the net and the down-the-line lane; and watch how the two move, cover and communicate. The diagram illustrates the classic strategy: relentlessly squeeze the weaker player until their off-line return opens a gap to finish into.

85. Placing the left-hander in the advantage (Ad) court

Smart Doubles (Developing into a team)
Tactical analysis: Before drawing up any tactical plan, a new pair must answer the first organisational question: who stands on which side. Singleton argues that two right-handers usually find it most effective when the player with the better forehand stands on the right and the player with the best backhand stands on the left. But for a left-hander the advice is different: they create more problems for the opposing team in the advantage court (Ad), because their backhand turns into the middle of the court and is almost hidden from the server's view — the server struggles to find a weakness to target. The important point is that there is no standard formation for every pair: each team must be analysed individually to find each player's strengths and weaknesses, and from there decide where each should stand for the good of the team.

86. Who takes the middle ball? — the forehand priority rule

Smart Doubles (The advantage of the middle)
Tactical analysis: The middle of the court is the biggest source of advantage — and the biggest source of error — for any pair. A ball into the middle poses an instant question: who takes it? If both charge in, a wide space opens up behind them; if both yield, the ball drops between them. Singleton proposes a simple priority rule that eliminates hesitation entirely: the middle ball belongs to the player whose forehand faces into the middle. The rule has a clear technical basis — the forehand in the middle is the strongest, most natural shot, while the other player would have to use the backhand or turn their body to hit it. When the rule is agreed before the match, both players react instinctively and there is no moment of hesitation left.

87. Dealing with the lob — retreating to cover and switching positions

Smart Doubles (Handling the lob)
Tactical analysis: The lob is the most precise test of a pair's coordination. When the ball arcs over the net player's head, the two must react as one: retreat together to the baseline along parallel diagonals, and switch halves on the way back. The most common mistake is for one to retreat while the other stays at the net — leaving a vast gap in the middle that the opponents need only one cross-court shot to exploit. Notably, the lob is not a purely defensive shot: once both are back in position, you can reply with an even deeper lob, forcing the opponents off the net and handing the initiative back to you. The diagram shows both the ball and the two switching retreat paths of your team.

88. The net poacher — cutting off the ball

Smart Doubles (The net poacher)
Tactical analysis: The poach is the signature weapon of the net player: leave your position, cross the middle to cut off a cross-court return before it passes. What makes a good poach is not the speed of the shot but the timing of the decision. A good poacher decides to cross before the opponent makes contact — waiting until you see the ball's direction is already too late. The poach brings two benefits at once: it wins the point directly, and it plants doubt in the receiver's mind, making them return down the line weakly on later points. In exchange, it exposes the space behind the poacher, so it should only be used when the serve has created real pressure.

89. Pre-match communication — agreeing on serve targets

Winning Doubles (Ch.2)
Tactical analysis: Stan Smith opens his book with a simple observation: the biggest advantage of doubles over singles is that you do not have to fight alone. But that advantage only becomes real when the two of you truly know what each other is thinking. In practice, it takes a great deal of communication to know what your partner will do in each situation — and that communication must happen before the match, during the match and after it. Meaningful exchanges must revolve around the tactics for the match, based on your two strengths and the opponents' weaknesses. The signal behind the back before each serve is the most condensed form of communication: it tells the server whether the net player will hold position or poach, and gives both a shared script to execute together. The diagram illustrates a complete plan: the serve target, the opponents' expected reaction, and the pre-called poach.

90. The net player's starting position

Winning Doubles (Ch.3)
Tactical analysis: Great teams can hit spectacular shots, but they win most of their matches by playing the right shot at the right time — in other words, they play the percentages. And the most important percentage factor in doubles is the starting position. Stan Smith points out that the net player should start about halfway between the centre line and the outer sideline. Standing out near the sideline makes it easier to take the down-the-line ball, but leaves almost the entire middle lane open; clustering in the middle covers the middle lane but leaves the down-the-line lane open. The position in between is the optimal balance between the two risks. This is the kind of decision a good player makes once and then repeats unconsciously — not glamorous, but it is exactly what produces the winners that look like luck.

91. The serve + the net player's feint

Winning Doubles (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: A very high proportion of points are decided in the first two touches of the ball: the serve and the return. That is why Stan Smith devotes a whole chapter to maximising the serve — but in a way few people think of. The serve does not need high speed to be a big weapon; what matters is that it goes where the receiver is least prepared. And in doubles, the person who creates that uncertainty is the server's partner. By pretending to cross then stopping, the net player forces the receiver to split their attention between two threats at once, and a distracted receiver cannot return with the balance required. The diagram shows the synergy between the two: an accurate serve plus the net player's feint produces a short return that either of them can finish.

92. The forceful return — getting the ball back in play

Winning Doubles (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: The return is the second most important shot in doubles, and Stan Smith approaches it with a very specific mindset: you should not expect to win the games your opponents serve. So approach those games with a somewhat daring attitude, accepting some risk to create a shift. But first you must get the ball back. Data collected across hundreds of points shows the receiver has an almost equal chance of winning a point, provided they have successfully returned the serve — even with high defensive lobs. The third and most basic goal of the receiver is to get the ball back by any means possible. When you put more balls back, you force the server to hit more volleys than they would like — and more volleys mean more chances to err.

93. The control volley — directing the ball at the opponents' feet

Winning Doubles (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: Stan Smith opens his chapter on volleys with a simple observation that changes the way many people play: in doubles, players need more feel and finesse than in singles. The reason is that there are two opponents and they can be anywhere on the court. The goal of the volley in doubles is not to hit harder than the opponents but to keep them always playing the ball from below their feet, or to force them into trying to pass you from behind the baseline. Both situations lead to the same result: you get more chances to finish from the net. Feel is needed to control the ball — absorbing pace from the opponents' hard shots and directing the ball at their feet. The diagram shows a typical control volley: slow, low, precise, and needing no power at all.

94. Purposeful practice — goal-based drills

Winning Doubles (Ch.7)
Tactical analysis: The key to improving your doubles performance is to keep working on your weaknesses, both as an individual and as a pair. Stan Smith points out that many players do not assess their initial strengths and weaknesses, then get frustrated at not improving — they make the same mistakes match after match. The problem is not that they cannot hit those shots, but that they need smart, focused practice. The best way to start is to review the areas where you and your partner struggled in matches, then design a practice session around exactly those areas. The diagram illustrates the structure of such a drill: begin with a steady cross-court rally to build feel, then add the pressure and finishing element. Practice without a goal only repeats old habits.

95. The baseline disaster zone in doubles

Tennis Tactics (Ch.7)
Tactical analysis: Talbert's chapter 7 gives one of the most persuasive numbers in the entire doubles literature: while great singles players are identified by their ability to strike deep, powerful shots from the baseline, the hallmark of the great doubles players is exactly the opposite — they abandon the baseline and move to the net as early as possible, whatever the court speed. The reason lies in the number: only 10 to 20 percent of doubles points are won from the backcourt. It is a disaster zone, where every shot is defensive and lacks angle. And the shots needed to escape that losing zone are entirely different from the shots that pay off in singles — which is why tactical discussion of baseline play for singles and for doubles must be presented separately. 96. Four ways to get to the net

Tennis Tactics (Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: Talbert's chapter 6 begins with a very tight definition: net play in singles should be conceived as two consecutive shots — a forcing approach shot that clears the way, and a follow-up shot played from the net position. The common weakness of many players is the tendency to charge the net despite having played a weak approach; if they then lose the point, the fault should be attributed to the non-forcing approach rather than to their volleying ability. There are exactly four kinds of shot that can open up a chance to take the net, and this diagram draws all four routes from the same starting point. 97. Deep serve then cross-court volley

Tennis Tactics (Ch.4)
Tactical analysis: Talbert stresses that serve-and-volley is not a single action but a pair of shots in a causal relationship. For a serve to be forcing enough to give you the chance to play a damaging first volley, it must be hit deep on a fast court. Against most opponents, the deep serve should be aimed at the outside angle to open the court for a cross-court volley, or at the backhand corner to draw a weaker return. The diagram illustrates the first option: the wide serve drags the receiver outside the sideline, leaving the opposite half completely empty, and the cross-court volley goes straight into that space. 98. The return — just get the ball back

Tennis Tactics (Ch.5)
Tactical analysis: Talbert devotes chapter 5 to the return and opens with a very specific piece of mental advice: when returning, you must not expect to win the games your opponent serves. So approach those games with a somewhat daring attitude and accept some risk in order to force a change. But first comes the most basic goal: get the ball back by any means possible, even with high defensive lobs. You cannot win a point without keeping the ball in play, and often even the weakest, most pathetic return can make a greedy or over-eager opponent err. There is one truth that must be driven into the returner's mind: data gathered across hundreds of points shows the returner has an almost equal chance of winning a point, provided they have returned the serve successfully.

99. The overhead smash — the number-one damage weapon

100. The volley — the second-highest damage weapon

Tennis Tactics (Ch.3 & Ch.6)
Tactical analysis: The diagram closes the collection with the shot Talbert calls the heart of versatile doubles play. The statistical table in chapter 3 shows the volley has a damage rating of 9.5 in doubles — behind only the overhead smash — and the frequency table shows it accounts for 26.5 percent of shots but delivers as much as 41 percent of point-winning shots. Without it, or with only a weak or hesitant version of it, you will not be able to play doubles to the full potential of the game, or of yourself.
