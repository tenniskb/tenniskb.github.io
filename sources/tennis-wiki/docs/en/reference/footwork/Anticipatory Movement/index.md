# Anticipatory Movement — Technical Reference

Anticipatory movement is starting your footwork or your body positioning before the ball actually arrives — triggered by reading your opponent's preparation, not by reacting to the ball's flight. It's the most direct physical expression of anticipatory play there is. Done right, it converts your entire body weight into forward momentum right at contact, turning what would otherwise be a passive stroke into a genuine weapon.

## The Core Principle

Step into the ball's line as it's approaching — don't wait for the ball to arrive and then step. That pre-stepping is what converts your body weight into forward momentum at contact, instead of you shifting weight reactively after the ball's already on top of you. The difference is genuinely kinetic: a player who moves into the ball contributes their mass to the shot. A player who waits for the ball and then responds is absorbing it instead of using it.

This shows up at multiple scales in your game. On the return, it's adjusting your positioning and depth after reading the toss, before the serve's even struck. On your split-step, it's pre-loading your direction toward what you've read, before you land. In rallies, it's stepping into the ball's line during your opponent's swing — not after their shot's trajectory is already confirmed and it's too late to gain anything from the read.

## Where It Goes Wrong

The default failure is passive waiting — you wait for the ball to arrive, then step. That eliminates any momentum contribution from your body and leaves you with weaker, more tentative contact than you're actually capable of. The opposite failure is premature commitment — moving too early, before your cue-reading has actually confirmed the direction, which gets you wrong-footed. The fix for that one isn't reverting to waiting — it's sharpening your cue-reading accuracy so your early movement is actually informed rather than guessed.
