# The Backhand Bermuda Triangle — Technical Reference

I want to name a specific spacing and timing blind spot that swallows more backhands than any single technical flaw: the Bermuda Triangle, where players get jammed or lose their structure because the ball's gotten too close to the body before they were ready for it. Your backhand requires your hitting shoulder to sit closer to the net than your forehand does, which makes your spatial awareness and early preparation far less forgiving here than on your forehand side.

## How You End Up In It

If the ball gets too close to your body on a backhand, you simply can't extend your arms or use proper rotation — there's no room left. This happens specifically when you misjudge the ball's depth or fail to execute an early unit turn. Surviving it means establishing contact well out in front of your body, keeping real geometric distance between your torso and your actual strike zone.

## Where It Goes Wrong

Late preparation — waiting until the ball bounces to turn your shoulders — produces a rushed swing, jammed elbows, and forces you into defensive slices you didn't actually want to hit. Poor spacing — letting yourself drift too close to the ball's path — collapses your arms against your body and eliminates all your leverage. And pulling off early — opening your head and shoulders before contact — costs you control, produces mishits, and weakens your power transfer even when you technically had room to hit a clean shot.

## The Cure

Extreme early preparation. Your unit turn has to happen the moment the ball leaves your opponent's racket — not when it bounces on your side. A drill worth running: the contact freeze. Stop your swing exactly at the moment of contact and check two things — is the ball sufficiently out in front of you, and are your arms comfortably extended. If either answer is no, you were in the triangle before you even swung.
