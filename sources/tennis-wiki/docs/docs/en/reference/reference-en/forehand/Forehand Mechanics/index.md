# Forehand Mechanics — Technical Reference

Here's the blueprint I want you to build your forehand around: a rotational, kinetic-chain-driven stroke that pulls together ground force, the stretch-shortening cycle, a precise contact point, and an elongated hitting zone, all to produce pace, topspin, and real directional control at once.

## Preparation and Contact

The instant the ball leaves your opponent's racket, your shoulders and hips should turn as one unit — that's your coil. This ensures your racket is fully back before the ball bounces. Waiting for the bounce to start preparing guarantees you'll be late against any real pace.

Your contact point always sits in front of your body — slightly to the side, and well ahead of your lead hip. What I'd call the elongated hitting zone matters here: your racket should stay on its intended path as long as possible rather than brushing through quickly. That's what maximizes both your accuracy and your margin for timing errors.

## Choosing Your Stance

| Stance | Power Source | Recovery Speed | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open | Rotational | Fastest | Wide balls, high pace, topspin |
| Semi-open/Neutral | Linear | Moderate | Inside-out shots, moderate pace |
| Closed | Arm/shoulder extension | Slowest | Attacking short balls, extra reach |

Your open stance loads your outside leg, which lets you push straight back to center with no wasted steps.

## Wrist Mechanics and Spin

For topspin — the windshield-wiper action — your racket approaches the ball from below on a low-to-high path. Your wrist lifts across the back of the ball, and your forearm internally rotates through the finish, generating real spin without sacrificing linear power. Modern pros routinely produce 3,000-plus RPM with this motion.

For a flat drive, you want what's called the laid-back wrist — extended at contact — which locks that link of your kinetic chain and lets ground force transfer directly into the ball instead of leaking out through your wrist.

Producing a genuinely heavy ball combines two things at once: a linear drive — body weight moving through the ball on a neutral or semi-open stance for horizontal penetration — and an angular snap, an explosive wrist action plus internal shoulder rotation at contact for high spin rates. A cue worth trying: imagine hitting through three tennis balls stacked one behind the other — it extends your hitting zone and captures more of the available energy.

## Choosing Your Grip

| Grip | Hand Placement | Primary Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Continental | Heel of palm on bevel 2 | Slice, volleys, serves |
| Eastern | Palm on bevel 3 | Classic flat hitting, versatile |
| Semi-Western | Palm toward bevel 4 | Modern standard — power plus heavy topspin |
| Western | Palm fully under, bevel 5 | Maximum topspin, ideal for high-bouncing balls |

Semi-Western has become the dominant modern choice because it naturally closes your racket face at contact while still letting you generate extreme topspin. If your flat shot keeps going wide, check your grip first — Continental on a drive produces too open a face.

## Your Follow-Through Isn't Decorative

A full, relaxed follow-through is your kinetic chain's braking system. Cut it short and you're pushing enormous leftover energy straight into your elbow and rotator cuff tendons — tennis elbow frequently starts exactly here.

## Where It Goes Wrong

A shot flying long usually traces back to wrist mechanics or insufficient topspin — check your low-to-high path and make sure you're not using Continental on a drive. A shot into the net usually means your contact point's too far back — hit it further in front, and start your unit turn earlier. No power at all usually means an arm-only stroke, legs that stopped contributing — start from the ground and feel your legs drive before your arm even swings. And late contact almost always traces back to a delayed preparation — your shoulder turn needs to start the instant you see the ball leave your opponent's racket, not when it bounces on your side.
