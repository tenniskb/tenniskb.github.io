# The One-Handed Backhand — The Full Deep Dive

*The Silk, The Cannon, and The Quiet Eye*

---

## Chapter 1 — Should You Even Learn This Shot?

I get asked this a lot by players in their 50s: "Should I switch to a one-hander?"

Here's my honest answer in 2026: probably not for power. Definitely yes for slice. And maybe — *maybe* — for topspin, but only if your shoulder says yes and your ego stays quiet.

This deep dive is written for three kinds of players:

1. **You already hit a one-hander** and want to clean it up without wrecking your shoulder in the process.
2. **You want the slice** — the elegant, low-load version of the one-hander that honestly, most players over 50 should be using far more often than they do.
3. **You're a coach or a student** who wants the pro-level mechanics translated into something a 3.5 player can actually use — what to steal from Dimitrov, what to leave alone from Wawrinka, and why Federer's "quiet eye" might be the single cheapest improvement available to you.

### Three Pros, Three Different Answers to the Same Question

I like to study three pros when I teach this stroke, because each one solves the same problem differently, with different trade-offs. At your level, you're not trying to become any of them. You're picking the pieces that are safe for your body.

- **Dimitrov** swings with a fluid C-loop — power comes from hip rotation and the elastic snap of that loop. Medium shoulder load. The takeaway for you: let the racket head fall like silk instead of yanking it back.
- **Wawrinka** is explosive and linear — torso unwind plus a deep left-elbow pull. High shoulder load, arguably the highest of the three. The takeaway for you: steal his linear weight transfer, skip the elbow pull entirely.
- **Federer** is compact and efficient — precision, a quiet eye, and an almost-straight arm at contact. Low-to-medium load. The takeaway for you: quiet eye, heel-to-toe footwork, and an arm that's *almost* straight, not fully locked out.

I think of the one-hander as the most internally coordinated stroke in the game — closer to a tai chi push-hands exchange than to anything else in tennis. On a two-hander, your off hand adds a second engine of stability. On a one-hander, that second engine is gone. You have to build the engine on the inside — your core rotates, your lower back opens, your chest fires the arm through. That's why a good one-hander looks effortless when it's right. It's almost entirely core, not arm.

**The single biggest trap at your level:** trying to copy a pro's *extreme* version of the takeback — Dimitrov's deep chin-on-shoulder turn, Wawrinka's deep elbow pull, Federer's dead-straight arm. Each of those took the pros ten thousand-plus hours and a body conditioned since childhood to handle it. Your job is to take the minimum effective version of each cue and skip the extremes.

## Chapter 2 — The Grip and the Setup

### The Grip: Continental, No Exceptions

Your grip is Continental. Full stop. The V between your thumb and index finger sits on bevel #2 — the same bevel you already use for your volley, your slice, and your overhead.

Why not Eastern or Semi-Western? Because on a one-hander, you don't have a second hand bracing the racket face. Anything more open than Continental leaves the face open at contact and the ball sails long. Continental keeps the face just slightly closed, which is exactly what you need for a single-arm strike.

**A quick check:** hold the racket like you're shaking its hand. Now rotate your hand slightly (counter-clockwise if you're right-handed) until the V lands on the top-left bevel. That's it. Your thumb pad should rest along the back bevel.

Another way to check it: imagine you're hammering a nail into the court in front of you. The "hammer face" — your strings — should point toward the side fence, not up at the sky. If it's pointing skyward, your grip has drifted toward Eastern. Fix it before you swing.

### The Setup: Your Top Hand Points, It Doesn't Grip

Here's the biggest visual difference between a one-hander and a two-hander. On a two-hander, both hands come up together into a diamond shape at chest height. On a one-hander, your top hand never touches the racket during the load. Instead, it has one job: **it points at the ball.**

Through the turn, your left hand (for a right-hander) does three things:

1. **Helps start the turn.** The elbow tucks toward your right hip as your shoulders rotate — the racket gets carried back by the turn of your shoulders, not by your arm pulling it.
2. **Stays up, pointing at the ball.** Your index finger tracks the incoming ball all the way through the turn and into the start of your swing. This is quietly training your eyes to stay locked on the ball.
3. **Releases as you swing forward.** Once your right arm starts its forward path, the left hand stays up (or drops for balance on a low ball) — it does not stay glued to the racket through the swing.

A good way to feel this: stand sideways, right shoulder toward the net. Bring the racket up and back with your right arm bent about 90 degrees — think of an "L" shape from your shoulder down to the racket head. Your left arm mirrors that bend, hand at chin height, pointing at an imaginary incoming ball, elbow tucked close to your body. If you freeze there, you should look a bit like a goalkeeper set to stop a penalty kick.

### Rhythm: Hop First, Then Turn

Same rule as your forehand: split-step first, turn second. A small hop as your opponent strikes the ball, feet landing shoulder-width apart, and only then does your shoulder turn begin.

This matters even more on the one-hander than the two. A late turn on a two-hander can still produce a decent shot because the second hand bails you out. A late turn on a one-hander usually means a shank, or a slice that dives straight into the net.

Here's the sequence I want you thinking: see the ball leave your opponent's strings, hop, turn your shoulders (chin toward your right shoulder, racket up in that L-shape, left hand pointing), then settle your eyes on the spot where you plan to make contact.

**Drill — Wall Mirror Setup.** Stand a meter from a wall or mirror. Run through the turn ten times: hop, turn, L-shape, left hand pointing, hold for two seconds. Check yourself each time — is your chin toward your right shoulder, racket head up, left hand pointing, left elbow tucked in? Three minutes a day is plenty. You're not trying to think your way into this shape in a match — you're trying to make your body do it without thinking.

## Chapter 3 — What Changes Mechanically on One Arm

The same four-layer framework from your forehand applies here — the swing, the setup, the rotational axis, and the feel — but the load shifts. On a two-hander, two arms share the work. On a one-hander, it all lands on your right shoulder and the right side of your core. That's exactly why the rotational and feel pieces matter more here, not less.

### The Swing: Keep the "L" Until Contact

The bent-arm "L" you build on your forehand takeback shows up here too — the angle between your forearm and the racket shaft should stay bent almost all the way to contact. Most 3.5 players straighten that arm too early on the one-hander. It's the same mistake as "arm-swinging" on the forehand, and it produces the same result: a late, weak, injury-prone shot.

The 70/30 rhythm still applies, maybe more so here. Seventy percent of your swing is slow — the drop, the loading, the racket head falling into position. Thirty percent is fast — the burst from contact through the finish.

You've got two safe takeback shapes to choose from:

- **The C-loop (Dimitrov style):** the racket head drops in a big, smooth "C" below the ball, with a deeper unit turn. Looks beautiful when it's right. The risk at your age is that deep chin-on-shoulder turn loading up your neck.
- **The slot (Federer style):** more compact — the racket drops to about waist-to-chest height, slightly behind your body. Less shoulder turn, less neck rotation, easier timing. **This is the one I want as your default.**

The slot asks less of your shoulder and your thoracic spine to get to the same contact point. That's the whole Federer philosophy in one sentence: same result, less travel, less risk. At 50 and up, that's the difference between playing this shot twice a week for years and playing it twice before your shoulder tells you to stop.

**The drop itself** should feel like falling, not pulling. From that L-shape load, just let the racket head fall on its edge into the slot — don't actively pull it down. If you pull it, your shoulder is doing active work every single rep, and that's exactly the kind of repeated load that leads to impingement. Let gravity do the work instead: think of the racket head as a piece of silk falling through the air. You don't pull silk down. You just stop holding it up.

Watch Dimitrov's C-loop in slow motion sometime — there's no jerk at the bottom of the drop. The racket head reaches its low point, pauses for a split second, then fires forward. That pause is the loading, the same way a whip gets drawn back before it cracks.

### The Rotation: Let Your Hips Lead, Not Your Neck

Think of your spine as a door hinge, not a barrel spinning in place. On a one-hander, your rotation is naturally bigger than on a two-hander, because there's no second arm limiting how far you turn. The risk is over-rotating through your upper spine and neck instead of your hips.

The order matters: hips rotate first, chest follows, shoulders follow the chest, arm follows the shoulders. Each piece starts moving only after the piece below it has already started. Ground up, always.

For a right-hander, your left side becomes your rooted, stable side on this shot, and your right side is the one free to move. Your right foot acts like a kickstand — it steps in while your left leg rotates and your chest opens up.

There's a sensation I want you to feel in your lower back on the takeback — an opening, an expansion through your lower back as your hips load into the turn. Most players at your level keep their lower back locked stiff and try to generate all their rotation up in the neck and shoulders instead. Letting that lower back open on the way back, then close as you swing through, is what actually creates the separation between hips and shoulders that generates real racket speed — without needing your neck to do anything dramatic.

### The Feel: Quiet Eyes Win Matches

Here's a Federer habit that costs you nothing to copy and is worth more than almost anything else on this list: after contact, keep your eyes on the contact point for about two-tenths of a second. Your head barely moves.

Why it works — those extra 200 milliseconds give your brain more time to process exactly what just happened: where you made contact, how the ball came off the strings, whether your timing was early or late. That feedback loop is what makes your next shot better. It's free. It just requires patience you're not used to giving yourself.

**In practice** (never in a match), try this: after every shot you hit well, freeze your follow-through and count "one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi" before you move again. That two-second freeze locks the feeling of the correct position into your body, not just your memory of what it looked like.

**Drill — Quiet Eye Feed.** Have a friend feed you ten balls. Your only job is to hit, freeze your follow-through, count to two, then turn to track the next ball. After ten reps, switch it up — hit and immediately turn your head to track the next ball right away. You'll feel the difference immediately. The first version feels slower. It also produces cleaner contact, almost every time.

## Chapter 4 — Three Pros, What to Steal and What to Leave

This is where most players at your level go wrong. They watch a pro on TV, pick a favorite, and try to copy the most extreme version of that player's stroke. Below is the translation — what's actually worth taking from each one, and what belongs on the shelf.

### Dimitrov — The Fluid Artist

His signature: the racket head goes up high, sometimes above his head, then drops through a large C shape below the ball. His takeback is deep — chin practically on his shoulder — and his contact happens well out in front with an almost fully extended arm. Power comes from hip rotation, a shoulder whip, and the elastic snap of that C-loop. On forced shots he'll drop his center of gravity dramatically, right knee nearly brushing the court, using his legs as springs.

**Take:** the silk-drop cue — let the racket head fall, then accelerate through the ball. This is the single easiest pro move to copy, and it reduces shoulder load while adding racket speed. Also take the pause at the bottom of the drop — don't rush through it, that pause is the loading.

**Leave:** the deep chin-on-shoulder turn (that's real neck load at your age — turn your shoulders, but keep your chin closer to neutral) and the very deep knee bend on every shot (save that for the ball that's pulled you wide; a normal athletic stance is enough on a neutral ball).

Try this feel: hold the racket out in front of you, face up, right hand only. Let the racket head drop — don't pull it, just release the tension holding it up. Watch it fall on its edge in an arc. That's the C-loop, at the wrist. Loose wrist, racket falls on its own weight, your chest catches it at the bottom and sends it forward.

### Wawrinka — The Explosive Powerhouse

His signature: aggressive early prep, a deep pull-back of the left elbow behind his back, and a linear weight transfer with his front foot angled around 45 degrees to the net. Power comes from the torso unwinding — that deep elbow pull stores energy that releases as his hips and shoulders fire in sequence. It's the shot that made "Stanimal" a nickname, and you can hear it — a heavy, distinctive contact sound that's the product of a full kinetic chain firing at once.

**The warning first:** that deep left-elbow pull is, in my opinion, the single most shoulder-stressful move covered in this whole guide. Wawrinka is six-foot-three, and he's been hitting this way since he was twelve — his shoulders and thoracic spine have been conditioned for that load for decades. At 50-plus, that same move puts your shoulder into extreme external rotation and abduction, strains your rotator cuff, tightens your upper back, and loads your lower back. Skip it. Fully.

**Take instead:** the linear weight transfer — front foot stepping in at an angle, weight moving forward, back foot pushing off. That's totally safe and very useful; it moves your body *into* the shot instead of just rotating in place. Also take the early preparation — turn early, hit late, since a one-hander has a smaller margin for error than a two-hander and you need to be set before the ball arrives. A moderate front-foot angle (20-30 degrees is plenty, you don't need the full 45) helps your hips open enough to let your chest rotate freely.

### Federer — The Effortless Default

His signature: a compact takeback into what's called the slot position, an almost straight arm at contact, a heel-to-toe step into the court, and remarkable head stability after the ball leaves his strings. His racket speed at contact is actually lower than Dimitrov's or Wawrinka's — but his contact point is more consistent, his placement sharper, and his efficiency is what lets him sustain this shot for a five-hour match.

**Take almost all of it.** The compact slot takeback is the minimum effective version of this stroke — less travel, less shoulder load, easier timing, and it's the version I want as your everyday default. The heel-to-toe step is the safest footwork you can use — small, controlled, gradual loading through the knee, no lunging. The quiet eye, as covered above, is the cheapest upgrade available to you. And the overall mindset — do less, not more, and let placement do the work instead of raw racket speed — is worth copying in full.

**One adjustment:** skip the fully straight arm. That took Federer twenty-five-plus years of conditioning. Use 15 to 20 degrees of elbow flex instead — you give up a small amount of leverage and gain a much bigger amount of shoulder safety. Easy trade.

**Drill — The Federer Slot.** Stand at the baseline with no ball. Take the racket back into the slot: head below where the ball would be, face slightly closed, right arm bent around 90 degrees, left hand pointing. Hold it for two seconds and check yourself. Then swing forward slowly, letting your elbow settle into that 15-20 degree flex as you reach the contact zone — not straight, not deeply bent. Finish over your right shoulder, freeze, and count to two.

## Chapter 5 — The Slice: Your Shoulder's Best Friend

If there's one version of the one-hander every player over 50 should own, regardless of shoulder history, it's the slice. Same grip as your volley, a high-to-low motion, and the underspin buys you time, disrupts rhythm, and works beautifully on defense, low balls, approach shots, and returns of a big serve.

### How It's Different from the Topspin Version

The slice shares your grip with the topspin one-hander but almost nothing else about the motion. Where the topspin takeback brings the racket head up before dropping into the slot or the C-loop, the slice takeback goes **high** — above your shoulder. Where topspin swings low-to-high, the slice swings high-to-low. Contact happens around waist height on the way down, with the face open like a hood — 30 to 45 degrees — instead of closed. Your wrist stays locked through the whole thing; there's no pronation like on the topspin side. And where topspin asks your shoulder to absorb the whole kinetic chain, the slice barely asks anything of it — the swing is short and mostly downward, powered by your weight shifting forward and gravity, not by your arm.

That's really the key insight: **on the slice, power comes from gravity and your weight transfer, not your arm.** The high takeback stores potential energy; the swing releases it. Your arm is just delivering the racket, not generating the force. That's exactly why it's so much gentler on the shoulder than the topspin version, where the shoulder is doing most of the rotational work in a fraction of a second.

I like to describe the whole motion as a door: the takeback is the door swinging open — racket head goes up high, your arm opens, your body starts to turn. The swing is the door swinging closed — racket comes down and across your body, face staying open, contact happening somewhere in the middle of that arc. And as contact happens, you're walking through the door — your weight shifts forward, your front foot plants, your shoulder rotates through. The result is a ball with underspin and a touch of sidespin that floats forward slightly, then stays low after it bounces.

Or think of a windshield wiper — high point at one end, low finish at the other, the face angled the whole way through, contact happening as it passes through the middle.

### When to Use It

- **Under pressure, on defense:** just get it back deep. Buy yourself time to recover position.
- **As an approach shot:** slice it low as you come forward — a low ball forces your opponent into an awkward slice or lob of their own.
- **Returning a big serve:** the underspin absorbs pace and the ball lands short. This is your friend on any serve you can't get on top of.
- **Breaking up rhythm:** slide a slice in after a stretch of topspin rallying. The change of spin throws off your opponent's timing more than you'd expect.

**Drill — Slice Wall Rally.** Stand about 4 meters from a wall. Rally slice only, slow pace, and aim for 20 balls in a row that bounce below waist height on your side. Listen for the sound — a clean slice makes a soft "fwoosh." A flatter, harder "thwack" means you caught it flat, not with underspin. Adjust and try again.

One more note: hit this shot with your right hand only — no left hand on the racket, unlike the two-handed slice. Most players over 50 find the free left hand actually helps their balance through the shot.

## Chapter 6 — Drills, Common Mistakes, Shoulder Care, and Your Cheat Sheet

### The Seven Drills to Work Into Your Week

1. **Wall Mirror Setup** — daily, 3 minutes. Ten reps of the unit turn against a wall or mirror, holding for 2 seconds each time. Check your chin, racket head, pointing hand, and tucked elbow.
2. **Silk Drop** — daily, 5 minutes. Take the racket up and back, then just let it fall into the slot position rather than pulling it. Pause at the bottom, then swing through slowly. Twenty reps.
3. **Quiet Eye + Two-Second Pause** — weekly, on court. Ten fed balls, freezing your follow-through and counting to two after every hit.
4. **Slot + Heel-Toe Shadow** — daily, 5 minutes. Shadow swings at the baseline focusing on the compact slot takeback and a heel-first step into the shot. Twenty reps.
5. **Slice Wall Rally** — 3 times a week, 10 minutes. Twenty consecutive slices landing below waist height off the wall.
6. **C-Loop vs. Slot Comparison** — weekly, 10 minutes. Ten balls with each takeback, back to back, noticing the trade-off between racket speed and shoulder load. Aim to be hitting the slot roughly 80% of the time.
7. **Lower-Back Opening Check** — daily, 3 minutes. Takeback with both hands on the racket, consciously feeling your lower back open as your right hip rotates back and your left hip stays forward. You're practicing the sensation, not a big visible motion.

### Seven Mistakes I See Constantly at Your Level

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong grip | Eastern or Semi-Western — face open at contact | Continental, V on bevel #2 |
| Pulling the takeback | Racket yanked back instead of dropped | The silk-drop cue — let gravity work |
| Deep elbow pull | Left elbow way behind the back, shoulder overloaded | Slot position, elbow stays close to the body |
| Fully straight arm at contact | Locked elbow, shoulder soreness afterward | 15-20 degrees of elbow flex |
| Shallow unit turn | Smaller or later than your forehand turn | "Hop, then turn" — check it in a mirror |
| Arm-swinging | Chest stays still, arm does all the work | "Chest turns, arm follows" |
| No quiet eye | Head snaps around right after contact | Practice the two-second count |

### Shoulder Care, Specifically for You

- **Shoulder impingement risk:** comes mostly from repeated one-arm topspin load. If this is a concern, lean on the slice for anything that isn't a clean, easy ball, and use your two-hander for power situations.
- **Rotator cuff strain:** usually traced back to a deep elbow pull or an overly wide takeback. The slot position and heel-to-toe footwork are your protection here.
- **Neck and upper-back tightness:** comes from a deep chin-on-shoulder turn. Let your shoulders turn fully, but keep your head closer to neutral.
- **Lower back pain:** usually means your lower back isn't opening on the takeback — it's staying stiff and passing the load somewhere it shouldn't. The lower-back check drill above addresses this directly.
- **Good news on the elbow:** the one-hander is actually gentler than the two-hander on your non-dominant arm, since it's not gripping the racket at all during the swing.

If you're managing an existing shoulder issue — especially anything involving impingement or a prior surgery — lean almost entirely on the slice, use your two-hander for power, and treat topspin one-handers as an occasional shot, not a staple.

**The one rule I'd want you to remember above everything else in this guide:** the slice is a shot you can hit every single day. The topspin one-hander is a once-a-week luxury at most. And the deep elbow pull isn't for you at all. Ask your shoulder before you ask your ego.
