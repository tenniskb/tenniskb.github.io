# The Backhand — The Mirror, The Whip, and The Slice

## Chapter 1 — The Big Idea: Your Backhand Is a Mirror

Watch any good player hit a backhand and you'll notice something: the setup looks almost identical to the forehand. Same unit turn, same chest rotation, same contact point out in front. The only thing that's flipped is the direction.

That's the mental shift I want you to make. Most players at your level treat the backhand like it's a completely different stroke. They swing with the arm instead of turning the chest. They look *at* the ball instead of through it. They slow down and get tentative because the whole thing "feels weird."

It shouldn't feel like a different sport. The same four-layer system that governs your forehand — the swing, the setup, the rotational axis, and the feel — applies here just as fully. Flip the direction, and everything else holds.

Here's a way to feel it: stand in front of a mirror and hit a forehand. Now mirror that same motion — same turn, same weight shift, same finish, just with the racket on the other side. Your body doesn't actually know the words "forehand" or "backhand." It only knows one principle: chest turns, arm follows, ball goes. Which side you're on is irrelevant to that principle.

### Two Real Differences

There are exactly two things that genuinely change between the sides, and they're worth knowing precisely.

**First — your dominant hand still drives, but now it has a partner.** On a two-handed backhand, your right hand (if you're right-handed) is still the engine generating the shot. But your left hand is now on the racket too, acting as a brace rather than a driver. It stabilizes; your right hand accelerates. That's the reverse of what feels natural at first, so it takes some getting used to.

**Second — your finish is over the opposite shoulder.** Your forehand finishes over your right shoulder. Your two-handed backhand finishes over your left. This single detail is the clearest visual tell for whether you're hitting the shot correctly.

I like to think of forehand and backhand as two mirrored chapters of the same book — flip to the right and you get "right-shoulder finish," flip to the left and you get "left-shoulder finish." Same book, mirrored chapter.

**The mistake I see constantly:** players finishing their backhand over the *same* shoulder as their forehand — the right one. That produces a weak, wristy shot with no real power behind it, and it's the single easiest thing to diagnose from the sideline. If your backhand keeps finishing over your right shoulder, your chest simply isn't turning enough. Go back and reset the unit turn.

## Chapter 2 — The Two-Handed Backhand: Your Everyday Shot

For a player at your level and age, the two-handed backhand is the right choice. It's stable, it generates real power, and it's gentle on the shoulder. The kinetic chain is the same one you already know from your forehand — we're just going to apply it specifically to this side.

### The Grips

Your two hands do different jobs, so they use different grips.

Your **dominant hand** (right, for a right-hander) uses the same grip as your forehand — Semi-Western or Eastern, with the V between thumb and index sitting on bevel #4 or #3.

Your **non-dominant hand** uses Continental — bevel #2, the same grip as your volley. That hand is there purely for stability, not for adding topspin.

Why Continental on the left hand specifically? It keeps the racket face just slightly closed at contact, which is exactly what you want. If you used Eastern or Semi-Western on that hand instead, the face would sit too open at contact and the ball would sail long.

The single most common mistake I see at your level is using the same grip on both hands — usually Semi-Western on both. That locks the face open at contact, saps your power, and is probably the number one reason recreational backhands float long past the baseline.

### The Unit Turn

Your backhand unit turn is your forehand unit turn, mirrored — shoulders rotate to the right instead of the left, chin rests on your right shoulder, both hands come together up at chest height with your left hand on the throat of the racket.

From that chest-high position, both hands drop together as your shoulders turn, and the racket head stays up and slightly behind you — forming a shape a bit like the letter D against your body.

Here's a useful check: stand in front of a mirror and do a forehand unit turn — chin on left shoulder, racket head up. Now do a backhand turn — chin on right shoulder, racket head up. The two should look like mirror images of each other. If your backhand turn looks shallower, slower, or less committed than your forehand turn, that's exactly where your problem starts.

### The Rotation: Chest Turns, Both Shoulders Move

Here's the layer most players miss entirely. A two-handed backhand isn't really a two-arm swing — it's a chest rotation that happens to have two hands on the racket. The chest turns, both shoulders move together as a unit, and your arms are mostly just along for the ride.

On this side, the roles flip from your forehand: your left side becomes the rooted, weighted axis, and your right side is the one free to move. After a good contact, both your shoulders should end up square to the net, chest fully open. If your right shoulder is still pointing off to the side after you've hit the ball, your chest simply didn't turn far enough.

### The Swing: Same "L," Mirrored Drop

That bent-arm shape you use on your forehand takeback shows up here too — the angle between your right forearm and the racket shaft should stay bent almost all the way to contact. Straightening it too early is the same mistake here as it is on your forehand, and it's just as common.

The same 70/30 rhythm applies: seventy percent of the swing slow and loaded, thirty percent fast through contact. And the drop is mirrored, too — where your forehand racket drops behind your back below the wrist, your backhand racket drops to your left side, below the wrist. Same underlying principle either way: the racket head falls on its own weight. You're not pulling it down with your arm.

### Contact and Finish

Contact happens at full arm extension, out in front of your lead foot, at roughly waist-to-chest height. As the racket releases through the ball, your right forearm rolls over slightly to add topspin.

The finish is the tell: racket ending up high over your left shoulder, head pointing up and back. If it's ending over your right shoulder instead, your chest simply isn't turning through the shot. Total follow-through distance is shorter than your forehand — around a meter and a half — and your two hands should stay together the entire way through.

One habit I want to steer you away from: trying to add pace by "snapping" the wrist at contact. It feels natural, but it's the wrong source of power on this shot — the two-handed backhand gets almost all its power from chest rotation, not the wrist. A wristy backhand is also a common path to tennis elbow in the brace-side (non-dominant) arm.

**Drill — The D-Loop Hold.** Stand in front of a mirror and do a backhand unit turn: both hands together at chest height, both shoulders turned right, racket head up. Hold that shape for two full seconds. Check yourself — chin on the right shoulder? Racket head up? Both hands together? Elbows down? Ten reps a day is enough for your body to start treating this setup with the same commitment as your forehand.

**Drill — The Left-Shoulder Finish Check.** Have a friend watch you hit ten backhands. Their only job is telling you which shoulder the racket finishes over. If eight or more finish over your left shoulder, your chest is turning correctly. If most finish over the right, you're arm-swinging — your chest isn't doing its job. This is honestly the single best diagnostic for this stroke.

## Chapter 3 — The One-Handed Backhand: What to Know, What to Borrow

The one-handed backhand might be the most beautiful stroke in tennis. Wawrinka, Federer, Dimitrov, Gasquet, Thiem — they all hit it differently, and every one of them hits it beautifully.

But it's also the most shoulder-stressful stroke in the game. It demands precise timing, excellent footwork, and a shoulder capable of absorbing repeated one-sided load.

For most players at your stage, the two-handed backhand remains the rational everyday choice. This chapter is here for two groups: players who already hit a one-hander and want to sharpen it, and players who want to add the slice one-hander specifically — which is a lot friendlier on the shoulder than its topspin cousin.

### Three Masters, Three Approaches

**Dimitrov's fluid C-loop:** a deep unit turn, racket head dropping below the ball in a large C shape, contact out in front with an extended arm. Power comes from hip rotation and the elastic snap of that loop. It looks like silk.

**Wawrinka's explosive linear style:** aggressive early prep, left elbow pulled deep behind the back, a linear weight transfer with the front foot angled around 45 degrees to the net. Power comes from the torso unwinding, not the arm. It sounds like a cannon going off.

**Federer's efficient, compact version:** a compact takeback into the slot position (racket head below the ball), an almost straight arm at contact, a heel-to-toe step into the shot, and remarkable head stability — eyes locked on the contact point for roughly 0.2 seconds after impact. Power here comes from precision, not size.

Honestly, none of these is exactly right for you to copy wholesale — each pro has put in fifteen-plus years of specific training that your body hasn't. What's actually worth taking:

- **Dimitrov's C-loop drop** — the feeling of letting the racket head fall rather than pulling it back. Easy to copy, genuinely useful.
- **Wawrinka's deep elbow pull** — this is his rotational engine, but it's a hard, risky copy at your age. I'd leave it alone.
- **Federer's quiet eye and heel-to-toe footwork** — pure efficiency gains. These protect your shoulder and your legs while still improving your shot.

### The Grip

Continental, no exceptions. Anything more open — Eastern or Semi-Western — forces the face open at contact and sends the ball long. The V between your thumb and index sits on bevel #2, same as your volley grip.

### The Footwork

Your right foot (for a right-hander) steps in heel-first, then rolls onto the toe as you load into the shot. This heel-to-toe pattern gives you a stable base and a small upward push through contact. It's the safest footwork option at your age precisely because it's small and controlled — no lunging, no over-reaching, no sudden stops. Your knee absorbs the load gradually instead of all at once.

### The Quiet Eye

After contact, keep your eyes on the contact point for about two-tenths of a second — your head barely moves. That short window of stillness gives your brain extra time to process exactly what just happened at contact, which sharpens your next shot. It costs nothing and it's arguably the fastest upgrade available to you. In practice, try holding your head still for a slow "one-Mississippi" count after every shot before you turn to track the next ball.

**If your shoulder has any real history** — impingement, rotator cuff strain, chronic tightness — stick with the two-handed backhand for anything requiring power, and reserve the one-hander strictly for slice, which is genuinely shoulder-friendly. Ask your shoulder before you ask your ego.

## Chapter 4 — The Slice Backhand: Your 50+ Workhorse

The slice backhand might be the single most underrated shot in recreational tennis. It stays low after the bounce, buys you time, disrupts your opponent's rhythm, and it's gentle on the shoulder. It works on defense, on low balls, as an approach shot, and against big serves you can't get on top of.

Think of it as your insurance policy. Even on a day when your two-handed backhand feels great, there will be other days when your shoulder, elbow, or back just says "not today." On those days, the slice is the shot that keeps you on the court instead of on the sideline.

### The Grip

For a two-handed slice, both hands use Continental — V on bevel #2 for both. For a one-handed slice (the more elegant version, closer to Federer's style), only your right hand is on the racket, same Continental grip you'd use for a volley.

Either way, the face should sit open like a hood — tilted up roughly 30 to 45 degrees from vertical, as if the racket face were the hood of a car pointed at the sky.

### The Motion: Open the Door, Walk Through

Think of the whole swing as a door. The takeback is the door swinging open — racket goes up high, above your shoulder. The swing is the door swinging closed — racket comes down and across your body, face staying open the entire time, contact happening somewhere in the middle of that arc, on the way down. You walk through the door at the moment of contact.

The most important cue here: **slice contacts the ball from underneath, not from above.** The path goes high to low, cutting under the ball, which is what produces the backward-spinning underspin that keeps the ball low after it bounces.

It's a lot like a windshield wiper — high point at one end, low finish at the other, the face angled throughout, contact happening somewhere in the middle as it passes through.

### Contact and Finish

Contact happens around waist height, slightly in front of your lead foot, on the way down — you're cutting the ball from underneath. The finish comes low, across your body, racket head ending up near the opposite hip from where it started, with your weight fully transferred forward.

Here's the paradox worth remembering: your grip pressure should be soft — three or four out of ten — but the contact itself should feel firm, with the strings not giving much on impact. Relaxed hand, solid impact. That combination is what actually produces the clean underspin.

**The three mistakes I see most:**
- **Swinging up at the ball**, which produces a weak floater instead of a controlled slice. The path is down, not up.
- **Closing the face at contact**, which sends the ball diving into the net. Keep that face open the whole way through.
- **Swinging too hard.** This is a placement shot, not a power shot — let the underspin do the work instead of muscling it.

### When to Use It

- **Under pressure:** just get it back deep, no pace required. You're buying time to recover position.
- **As an approach shot:** a low slice forces your opponent into an awkward reply — they can't drive a low ball with pace — while you move forward behind it.
- **Against a big serve:** the underspin absorbs pace and the ball lands short, taking time away from a server who's expecting to dictate the point.
- **To break up rhythm:** slide a slice in after a stretch of topspin rallying. The change of spin throws off your opponent's timing more than you'd think.

**Drill — The 20-Slice Wall Rally.** Stand about 4 meters from a wall and rally slice only, slow pace. Goal: 20 balls in a row bouncing below waist height on your side. Listen for the sound — a clean slice sounds like a soft "fwoosh"; a flatter hit sounds more like a "thwack." If 20 feels too easy, move back to 5 meters. If 10 feels too hard, move in to 3.

**Drill — Slice-Then-Forward.** Have a friend rally topspin with you from the baseline. Every fifth ball, hit a slice instead of a topspin, and the instant your racket touches the ball, split-step and move forward toward the net. The slice keeps the ball low, which forces your opponent into a defensive slice or lob of their own — giving you time to close in. Aim for 5 out of 10 approaches where you actually reach the net and get to volley the next ball.

## Chapter 5 — Drills, Common Mistakes, and Your 50+ Reality Check

### Five Drills to Build Into Your Week

1. **Cross-Hand Wall Rally** — daily, 5 minutes. Rally forehand topspin against a wall for 10 balls, then switch to two-handed backhand without moving your feet, for 10 more. The wall doesn't care which side you're on — this teaches your body that forehand rhythm and backhand rhythm are the same rhythm, just mirrored. Goal: 20 clean backhands in a row.

2. **Left-Shoulder Finish Check** — weekly, 5 minutes, with a partner. They watch 10 backhands and report which shoulder the racket finishes over. Target 8 out of 10 over the left shoulder. If you're under 5, drop the two-hander for a week, do the D-Loop Hold daily, and rebuild the turn.

3. **Slice Wall Rally** — 3 times a week, 10 minutes. Twenty consecutive slices bouncing below waist height off the wall. Listen for that clean "fwoosh" sound.

4. **Mirror Shadow Swings** — daily, 3 minutes. Alternate slow-motion forehands and backhands with no ball, back and forth, for three minutes. Pay attention to the back of the unit turn each time — chin on left shoulder for the forehand, right shoulder for the backhand. Both should look like mirror images.

5. **Quiet Eye Practice** — weekly, on court. After every single shot — forehand, backhand, volley, serve — hold your head still and count "one-Mississippi" before turning to track the next ball. It'll feel like you're wasting time at first. After about four weeks, you'll notice your ball-striking consistency has genuinely improved.

### Seven Mistakes I See Constantly

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Same grip on both hands | Semi-Western on both — face too open | Continental on the non-dominant (left) hand |
| Right-shoulder finish | Backhand ends like a forehand — no real power | Chest-turn drill, left-shoulder finish check |
| Wrist snap | Wrist flicks at contact chasing extra pace | "Elbow leads" cue — try the towel-under-armpit drill |
| Swinging up on the slice | Racket path goes low-to-high instead of high-to-low | "Slice = under the ball" — watch it against the wall |
| Closed face on the slice | Slice dives straight into the net | "Open like a hood" — check your Continental grip |
| Arm-swinging | Both arms push, chest stays still | "Chest turns, arms follow" — shadow swings |
| Shallow unit turn | Backhand turn is smaller or later than the forehand | D-Loop Hold drill, mirror check |

### The 50+ Reality Check

- **Shoulder strain on the one-hander** comes from repeated one-arm load. Use the two-hander for power, keep the one-hander for slice only.
- **Tennis elbow in the non-dominant hand** usually means that brace hand is gripping too hard with a closed face. Loosen it to a 3-4 out of 10 and stop squeezing.
- **Wrist strain toward the thumb side** on the two-hander comes from letting the wrist lead instead of the elbow. Keep the elbow leading, no wrist flick.
- **Lower back twisting** tends to show up from excessive shoulder turn on the one-hander. Lean on the two-hander for wide balls and save the one-hander for neutral ones.
- **Knee pain on low slices** usually comes from a deep, hard-stopping knee bend. Keep your back leg bent and soft, and favor softer court surfaces when you have the choice.
- **Recovery time:** one-handed sessions tend to leave a couple days of soreness. Cap yourself at one one-handed session a week, slice only, once you're past 50.
- **Hesitation on the backhand side** — late contact born from not trusting the shot — responds well to daily mirror drills that rebuild trust in the same four-layer system you already know from your forehand.

The single rule I'd want you to leave this chapter with: **your backhand is not your weak side. It's your mirror.** Treat it with the same respect as your forehand — the same commitment to the unit turn, the same four-layer system, the same finish line. The moment you start thinking of it as "the side I don't like," you're conceding almost a third of the court before the point even starts.
