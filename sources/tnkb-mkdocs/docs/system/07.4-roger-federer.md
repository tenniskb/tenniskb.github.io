# Roger Federer — Forehand Model

**Style: wrist pre-stretch.** Federer's forehand isn't built around a big swing — it's built around curling the wrist to create elastic recoil, then letting that recoil do the accelerating. Where [Andrey Rublev](07.1-andrey-rublev.md) locks the wrist for stability, Federer does almost the opposite thing with it, and gets speed instead of stability out of the deal.

---

## The Secret in the Sequence

At the top of the takeback, the racket tip points off to the right. As the hitting arm starts moving forward, the tip flips left, swinging around behind the back. That single motion stretches the forearm muscles — and they snap back open just before contact, producing racket-head acceleration well beyond what a plain arm swing could generate. It's the same stretch-shortening principle covered in [Wrist Lag and Release](wrist-lag-and-release.md), applied through wrist rotation rather than a simple lag angle.

## How This Compares to the Other Three Models

**vs. Rublev**: Rublev locks the wrist and drives the racket straight at the ball using trunk rotation. Federer keeps the wrist loose and lets the racket head trace an arc — right to left, behind the back. Rublev is optimizing for stability; Federer is optimizing for stored elasticity.

**vs. Alcaraz**: both players use a loose wrist and pre-stretch, but they build it differently. Alcaraz gets his pre-stretch from letting the racket fall with the elbow raised high; Federer keeps the elbow closer to the body and gets his pre-stretch from rotating the racket tip horizontally. Federer's swing path ends up flatter and more compact — less airborne than Alcaraz's.

**vs. Sinner**: Sinner lays the racket down horizontal from the very start of the power position to create lag. Federer never goes horizontal early — he keeps the face closed and only rotates the racket tip around the wrist axis at the very last moment. The resulting whip is smoother and involves less extreme wrist-bending than Sinner's flip.

## What It Looks Like

Setup: the off-hand holds the racket, the trunk turn is compact. At contact: the wrist has already unwound, the face is square, and the tip is no longer pointing left — it happened fast enough that most casual video misses it. Grip leans Eastern, favoring a flatter ball; timing is very early relative to most modern forehands.

## What to Actually Borrow From Federer

If your forehand looks like Rublev's — solid, reliable, but lacking racket-head speed — there's one addition worth trying: at the end of the takeback, let the racket tip point right (for a right-hander), then as the forward swing begins, actively let the tip fall behind the back, pointing left. Keep the hand loose rather than gripping tight — the feel is closer to wringing out the forearm than swinging the arm.

Don't try to copy the whole package. Federer's grip (Eastern, favoring flat) and his very early timing come from a different context than the more Western, more topspin-heavy grips modern players use — Alcaraz and Rublev among them. Take the *principle* — pre-stretch through wrist rotation — and graft it onto Rublev's rotational frame or Alcaraz's elbow-lever setup. The result is a forehand that gains racket-head acceleration without needing a harder swing.
