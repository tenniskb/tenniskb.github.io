# Optimal Arm Configuration (The C-Shape)

## The Big Idea

The key is to create a mass-spring in the arm.

## Three Core Properties

Maintain a loose wrist to stay in control. Hold tension in the arm without letting it go dead. Let the wrist deviate toward the little-finger side — ulnar deviation. This is not the same as a tensed, ramrod-straight arm, which should never be locked out.

Picture the shape: the elbow bent into a large "C," the wrist cocked back and down, the racquet head held up high — never a locked-out straight arm.

This configuration applies to both the serve and the forehand groundstroke.

## Why It Works

It creates spacing between the racquet and the body — the bent arm keeps the contact point out in front, away from the torso, so you're never jammed up. It makes the swing easier, since elastic recoil does more of the work than pure muscle, meaning less effort for more head speed. And it lets you control tension precisely: the big muscles hold the overall shape while the small joints stay loose, so you can dial power up or down without changing the swing itself.

## Four Reasons Looseness Equals Control

This echoes the loose-wrist page directly. It trades a timing problem for a geometry problem — steering the racquet within a 4-millisecond contact window is simply impossible, so a repeatable path matters more than a rigid grip. It moves stability up the chain — the shoulder and torso hold the shape, the elbow stays bent for spacing, and the wrist stays soft, lags, then automatically firms at impact through the stretch reflex. It buys more spin margin — a loose wrist accelerates late, brushes upward, and produces topspin that keeps the ball dipping in even when timing is slightly off. And "loose" has a specific feel: roughly a 3-out-of-10 grip, the wrist hinging back and down under ulnar deviation, and a brief automatic firming at contact — like catching a water balloon, not squeezing a stress ball.

## How to Train It

**Shadow the "C."** Start from the trophy position on the serve, or the take-back on the forehand, with the elbow at 90–120° and the wrist laid back.

**Hold and shake.** Keep a 3/10 grip and shake the wrist so it wiggles freely, while the upper arm stays connected to the chest.

**Slow drop.** Swing slowly and pause at the lowest point of the drop — you should see daylight between the elbow and the ribs. That gap is the spacing you're after.
