# Joao Fonseca — Forehand Model

**Style: the big loop.** A high takeback, a free-falling racket, and legs and hips that throw the whole body into the ball. ATP tracking at the Argentina Open clocked this forehand at 81 mph with 3,019 rpm of spin and a forehand quality rating of 9.8 — numbers that back up what the footage shows: maximum power built from full commitment of the legs and hips, not the arm.

---

## Seven Checkpoints (Melbourne Practice Footage)

**1. Outside foot pivots first — the unit turn begins.** The turn starts with the outside foot rotating. The hip turns before the shoulder does — the same underlying philosophy as [Andrey Rublev](07.1-andrey-rublev.md), just executed with a much bigger amplitude.

**2. Both hands hold the racket — head high, elbows away from the body.** The racket stands upright, both elbows separate from the torso, and the shoulder turn completes fully. Simple, correctly-sequenced kinetics.

**3. The off-hand crosses the shoulder — maximum coil.** He doesn't release the left hand until it has crossed the left shoulder, building the largest possible torsion between shoulders and hips. The goal is maximum coil, not maximum speed yet.

**4. The racket free-falls — strings down, dropping under the ball before pulling up.** From the high point, the racket free-falls below the ball before being pulled upward — a genuine drop-and-release, not a controlled lowering.

**5. The right leg drives — throwing the hip into the ball.** Open stance, a strong push from the right leg, the right hip driving directly into the contact point. The source footage calls this explicitly: "throw right hip into contact."

**6. Drive through the ball — extension past contact.** The arm continues forward after contact rather than just sweeping upward, producing a shot that drives through the court. This is "kinetic chain transfer, early contact, leg engagement" in one package.

**7. Relaxed finish — fast recovery.** The finish is loose rather than held — the hitting arm swings out past the shoulder and pulls back into a ready stance almost immediately.

## Fonseca vs. Rublev

| Factor | Rublev | Fonseca |
|---|---|---|
| Takeback height | Chest height, very compact path | Racket rises well above the head, elbow away from the body — a much bigger loop |
| Off-hand | Extends straight out like a ruler, fixed distance the whole swing | Stays on the racket longer, releasing only once it crosses the left shoulder |
| Racket drop | Falls straight into the slot, butt cap at the ball, almost no free-fall | Genuine free-fall under the ball before pulling up |
| Main power source | Very fast trunk rotation, shoulders opening late, power from the core | Strong push from the right leg, hip driving into the ball more visibly |
| Eyes and contact | Eyes locked for a long time, head nearly still | Head comes up sooner after contact, body relaxes into recovery faster |
| Finish | Long swing, trunk stays closed until late | Relaxed finish early, arm swings past the shoulder and returns to ready quickly |

## Who Should Learn From Whom

**If your forehand collapses in toward your body or feels unstable**: borrow Rublev's off-hand spacer — hold it parallel to the baseline until you're ready to swing forward.

**If your forehand lacks power, or you find yourself pulling it with the arm**: borrow Fonseca's sequence — pivot the outside foot first, hold both hands on the racket longer, and let the racket fall before driving the right hip into the ball.

**In short**: Rublev is the hydraulic press — stable, early, few timing errors. Fonseca is the big loop — maximum power when there's time to load it. Both run on the same underlying principle: feet, hips, shoulders, arm, in that order. See [Kinetic Chain](kinetic-chain.md) for why that order matters more than raw effort.
