# Drop Wrist vs. Correct Takeback

A basic-tennis video breakdown, side by side: the "drop wrist" error and the correct horizontal takeback. This is a beginner-level checkpoint, but it's worth its own page because the error shows up constantly in recreational forehands — including in [My Forehand — Personal Analysis](08.1-my-forehand-analysis.md), where a version of this exact fault gets flagged as one of the four fixes needed.

---

## What the Video Shows

**0–7 seconds — the "drop wrist" error.** The wrist folds down, the racket head points straight at the ground, and the arm works in isolation — the shoulders and hips barely move at all.

**7–11 seconds — a swing attempted with that error still in place.** The result: essentially no power. The follow-through is short, the trunk never fully rotates, and the ball is traveling on arm strength alone.

**11–22 seconds — the correct version.** The wrist stays level, the racket head gets pulled back on a horizontal plane, and the full stroke completes properly.

## Why Dropping the Wrist Kills Power

**It destroys the natural lag.** Forehand power doesn't come from swinging the arm hard — it comes from the delay between the hip-and-shoulder turn and the racket. Dropping the wrist early "releases" that angle before the forward swing even begins. By the time contact arrives, there's nothing left to release. This is the same principle laid out in [Wrist Lag and Release](wrist-lag-and-release.md): the wrist has to stay loaded until it's needed, not spend its lag early.

**It breaks the kinetic chain.** The correct order is legs, hip rotation, upper body, shoulder pulling the arm, racket whip. Dropping the wrist puts the forearm and racket handle in a straight line too early, which forces the arm muscles to muscle the racket upward instead of letting the hips pull it through. See [Kinetic Chain](kinetic-chain.md) for the full sequence this interrupts.

**It opens the racket face too early.** With the head pointing down, the face ends up tilted back toward the sky at the slot position. The ball either floats with no pace, or the player has to fight the wrist at the last second to close the face back down — which is inconsistent by nature.

## The Correct Technique

**Setup (roughly seconds 13–16 in the source footage).** The wrist stays neutral, with a slight extension — not curled down, not cocked up. The racket head stays at or above the level of the wrist through the entire takeback. The takeback itself travels on a horizontal plane rather than dropping — the visual cue given in the source material is "tucking the racket into your back pocket."

**Unit turn before the arm.** Shoulders and hips turn first; the hand holding the racket simply follows along. The off-hand (holding the ball, or resting on the throat of the racket) helps the shoulder complete its turn and build the slot.

**The slot.** By the time the racket reaches its furthest point back, the face is slightly closed, the elbow sits about a hand's width from the body, and the wrist holds a firm, neutral position — not a floppy one.

**Pulled by the hip, not pushed by the arm.** The correct swing (seconds 17–19) starts with the legs driving and the hips opening first. The racket gets pulled along and naturally lags behind — the wrist only bends back slightly as a result of that pull, not as something actively done on purpose.

**Result**: a full follow-through across to the left shoulder, a fully rotated trunk, and a ball that drives through rather than skims up and off the strings.

## Quick Comparison

| "Drop Wrist" Error | Correct "Level" Technique |
|---|---|
| Racket head points at the ground, wrist folded | Racket head level with the shoulder, wrist neutral |
| Swung by the arm, hips stay still | Pulled by hip rotation, arm follows |
| Lag is lost early, forces tension at the end | Lag builds naturally, whip happens at contact |
| Ball lacks pace and spin | Ball is heavy, penetrating, well controlled |

## Fixing It on Court

**1. Shadow swing with a checkpoint.** Turn the shoulders, bring the racket back, and pause. Is the racket head higher than the wrist? Is the palm of the hitting hand angled slightly toward the ground? That's the correct position.

**2. The "back pocket" cue.** Picture tucking the racket handle into your back right pocket — not dropping it toward the ground.

**3. Practice against a wall.** Stand roughly 30 cm out from a wall, back to it, and take the racket back on a horizontal plane. If the racket head touches the wall below hand height, the wrist is dropping.

**4. Swing at half speed.** Focus purely on holding the wrist position and letting the hips do the work. When the racket starts to feel "heavy" in the backswing and then releases on its own — that's the lag arriving correctly.

**Coach's tip**: if you can see the racket's string logo pointing at the ground during setup, something's wrong. The logo should point out toward the side fence.
