# Reflex Wall (Volley) — Closing Out the Point at Net

The volley is blocking, not hitting. You borrow the incoming pace and redirect it - you don't add your own swing on top of it. This is the same non-swing philosophy covered in [Volley Technique - Loose Grip](volley-technique-loose-grip.md), described here through a structural-stiffness lens.

## The "No Swing" Philosophy

Think of the racket face as a stone wall. Your only job is to place it at the right position and the right angle, and let the ball bounce off in the direction you want.

$$V_{out} = V_{in} + Structural\ Stiffness\ (K)$$

## Two Kinds of Volley

| Type | $K_{wrist}$ | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Punch volley | $\geq 0.95$ | Stiff face, fast and deep ball |
| Drop volley | Deliberately lowered | More dwell time, ball dies at the net |

## The Geometry in Space

### The V-Step

Step at a 45-degree angle toward the ball. This intercepts it earlier, narrows the opponent's passing angles, and opens up a wider angle for your own reply.

### The Reflection Angle

$$Reflection\ angle = 180° - (Incoming\ ball\ angle + Racket\ face\ angle)$$

A five-to-ten-degree adjustment in racket face angle is enough to redirect a shot from dead center to a sharp angle down the line.

## The Short Lever Arm

$$\tau_{recoil} = F_{ball} \times L_{arm}$$

Shortening $L_{arm}$ reduces the recoil torque that would otherwise twist back into the shoulder, which is what keeps the racket face stable on contact.

## Fascial Locking

At the instant of contact, the fascia running from chest to shoulder to wrist needs to be tensioned in sync - "stiffness in motion," not a rigid, gripped arm.

## Avoiding the Over-Swing

The racket should never travel back past the plane of the shoulders. Every bit of motion is directed forward, toward the ball.
