# Andrey Rublev — Forehand Model

**Style: "The Hydraulic Press."** Everything locks down — the off-hand holds its distance to the millimeter — and then the whole thing detonates through the trunk. This is one of the three forehand philosophies broken down in [Forehand Technique Comparison - Rublev vs Alcaraz vs Sinner](forehand-technique-comparison-rublev-vs-alcaraz-vs-sinner.md); this page goes deeper into how Rublev actually builds it, checkpoint by checkpoint.

---

## The Seven Checkpoints (Slow-Mo Tennis, Indian Wells footage)

**1. Ready position.** Feet wider than shoulder width, knees bent low — a solid athletic base. The racket head stays up and centered in front of the body rather than dropping early, so he can rotate either direction without losing balance.

**2. Unit turn led by the off-hand.** The left hand sits on the throat of the racket and turns shoulders, hips, and racket together as one block. The turn starts early and stays compact — this is not an arm-only takeback.

**3. Hands separate, torque maxes out.** After the unit turn, the hands split. The shoulders keep rotating past the hips. The obliques and back stretch under tension — he is loading the upper body like a spring. The left arm straightens out parallel to the baseline and becomes a ruler: it fixes the distance to the ball and stops him from crowding it, preserving room to swing.

**4. The racket drops into the slot.** From the high point, the racket falls low behind him, head pointing down. This is exactly when the legs start driving upward — the body uncoils from the ground up. The butt cap points straight at the anticipated contact point: the trunk is pulling the racket through, not the arm whipping it.

**5. Low-to-high, inside-out swing path.** The swing goes from low to high and slightly away from the body rather than across it — pushing the racket outward before lifting it. This produces heavy topspin that still drives through the court rather than looping short.

**6. Contact: eyes, distance, stillness.** The distance to the ball is wide — the arm is nearly straight at contact. The eyes lock onto the contact point and the head stays still, which is the core of his consistency. Shoulders and hips are close to square to the net at impact — he hasn't opened up early.

**7. Extend, then finish late.** After contact, the racket keeps driving long toward the target — a long hitting zone, not an immediate upward yank. He completes most of the swing path before letting the upper body relax. Holding the trunk closed until late is what keeps the ball flight stable instead of pulling it off-line.

---

## Why This Forehand Works

The power comes from trunk rotation, not the arm — which means it's durable and low-injury. The extended left arm acts as a distance brake, so he's consistently hitting out in front with a stable sweet spot. The chain is clean and legible: feet push, hips open, trunk rotates, arm swings, and the racket accelerates last.

## Three Things to Drill (No Speed Required)

1. **Keep the left hand on the racket while the shoulders turn** — don't let it release early.
2. **Let the butt cap point at the ball before you swing forward** — check this position in a mirror or slow-motion video.
3. **Hold your eyes on the contact point half a beat longer** than feels natural, even after the ball has left the strings.

## Learn Rublev When

You're making errors because you drift into the ball or lose your balance mid-swing. The left-arm spacer and the eye discipline stabilize the frame first — speed comes later, once the platform is solid.
