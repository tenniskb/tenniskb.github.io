# Serve and Return: Building the Point

## The Serve: From Weakness to Weapon

Sinner's serve wasn't a strength early in his career. In 2021, his average first-serve speed sat at 196 km/h — below the ATP average of 201 km/h, and notably weaker than most top-20 players. It was the shot Medvedev, Djokovic, and especially Berrettini went after in matches against him.

The shift started in mid-2023, when Cahill decided to fully remodel the serve, using John Isner's motion as a reference — not to chase Isner's raw speed, but to improve consistency and where the ball lands. The remodel took four months and ran in parallel with competing, something few coaches would risk.

The payoff: by the end of 2024, average first-serve speed had risen to 211 km/h, the win rate behind the first serve climbed from 71% to 78%, and — just as important — the first-serve-in percentage rose from 62% to 67%. The serve stopped being a liability and became the launchpad for the whole tactical system.

|---|---|
| Max speed | 228 km/h |
| Average speed | 211 km/h |

## The New Technique, Under Cahill

The remodeled serve rests on four principles, each a change from how he served before.

**A higher, steadier toss.** The old toss varied a lot in position and height. Cahill asked for a toss 15cm higher and 5-10cm further forward, giving more time to coil and extend, and letting contact happen right after the racquet reaches the top of its arc.

**A clearer trophy position.** The moment where the racquet points skyward and the body is fully extended, readying for the "scratch the back" phase — Cahill wanted a more distinct pause here, not a full stop, but a clearer sense of "loading" before the swing.

**Earlier pronation.** Pronation — the inward rotation of wrist and forearm — creates kick and direction. Sinner now pronates 0.02 seconds earlier than before, adding topspin kick and improving slice-serve consistency.

**A fuller finish across the body.** Following through further to the left doesn't just add momentum to the ball — it also lands him better positioned for the +1 shot.

## Tactics: The "Wide + Body" System

Sinner's serve isn't just "hit it over the net." Each one is the opening move of a planned two- or three-shot sequence.

At the deuce court, the go-to pattern is serve wide (out toward the opponent's forehand corner) followed by forehand +1 into the open middle. The wide serve pulls the opponent off the court and opens a big gap in the middle; the forehand +1 into that gap either ends the point or forces a weak defensive reply.

At the ad court, it's serve to the body followed by an aggressive backhand. The body serve attacks the returner's backhand directly, forcing a defensive shot from an uncomfortable position, which Sinner then punishes with an aggressive backhand into space the opponent can't recover.

A third, less-used pattern: serve down the T followed by forehand inside-in — used mainly once an opponent has adjusted to expecting the wide serve.

> "The important part is that when I serve, I already know what the next shot will be. The serve and the +1 are one package." — Jannik Sinner

## Return: The Skill of Stealing Time

If serve is the first strike, return is the counter that shapes the whole match. Sinner's return-game win rate sat at 43.2% in 2024 — top three on tour. That doesn't come from miracle return winners; it comes from a consistent philosophy: steal the server's time.

**Return position:** He typically stands closer to the baseline than the ATP average when receiving serve. Against big serves that's risky — but with his ball-reading ability, it lets him hit earlier on the rise, denying the server recovery time.

**Split-step timing:** His split step is precisely timed. Research shows the optimal window is 0.1–0.15 seconds after the server makes contact; Sinner executes this with only ±0.02 seconds of error — close to perfect.

**Forehand returns:** Where possible, he steps in and attacks the serve with the forehand, especially against second serves and medium-pace first serves. Against big first serves, he switches to a block return — setting the racquet early and steering the ball back in rather than swinging.

## Returning Big Serves: Block vs. Attack

Against tour servers like Opelka, Isner (retired), or Hurkacz — serving 220–240 km/h — the returner has roughly 0.4–0.5 seconds from the ball leaving the racquet to crossing their own baseline. How does Sinner cope?

**Technical block return:** he sets the racquet in a fixed position, angle pre-calculated, letting the serve's own pace push the return back over the net — similar in principle to table-tennis blocking, absorbing force and redirecting it. The return isn't fast, but it's accurate and deep enough to avoid being attacked immediately.

**Reading the tells:** he studies opponents' video beforehand to catch "telegraph" signals — toss angle, shoulder direction, footwork during the service motion. Against some opponents, he can read serve direction 0.1–0.15 seconds before contact — enough to start moving and prepping the return.

Against Hurkacz — a top-3 serve on tour — he applies a clever strategy: stand very close to the baseline with the forehand side slightly open, "inviting" the serve into the backhand. When it comes to the backhand, he blocks it cross-court and deep. When it goes to the forehand (rarer for Hurkacz at the deuce court), he attacks it hard.

## Return Tactics by Opponent

The team spends serious hours breaking down opponents' serve patterns before big matches, letting Sinner "pre-program" some return decisions instead of reacting in real time.

**Vs. Alcaraz:** Alcaraz serves heavily to the T at the deuce court to set up a forehand inside-out. Sinner counters by standing slightly more toward the T than usual, "inviting" a wide serve instead — and attacking with the forehand when it comes.

**Vs. Medvedev:** Medvedev varies serve placement widely but tends toward the body at the ad court on important points. Sinner prepares by standing further from the baseline at the ad court — enough room to execute a jumping backhand against the body serve effectively.

**Vs. Djokovic:** Djokovic varies serve speed superbly (175 to 220 km/h) to disrupt rhythm. Sinner handles this by keeping a fixed return position (rather than adjusting for pace the way many players do), focusing on reading direction instead of guessing speed.

## Serve and Return in the Tie-Break

The tie-break is the highest-pressure situation in tennis, and it's where Sinner's serve-return system shows most clearly. His 2024 tie-break win rate was 67.3% — second on tour behind Djokovic (69.1%).

**Serve strategy in the breaker:** he raises his first-serve-in rate (from ~67% to roughly 72%) by taking 5-8 km/h off the pace in exchange for more precise placement. The guiding idea: "In a tie-break, the mistake has to come from my opponent, not from me."

**Return strategy in the breaker:** he tends to go hyper-aggressive on the first mini-break point on return — hitting earlier and more decisively to apply psychological pressure early. If it lands, that first mini-break creates a big edge. If it doesn't, he resets to his normal approach.

> "A tie-break isn't about playing better. It's about making fewer mistakes than your opponent over ten points." — Jannik Sinner, post-match interview, US Open 2023

## Double Faults: A Controlled Weakness

One of the more striking 2024 numbers: zero double faults across the entire 2024 US Open. For comparison, even Djokovic had four in that same tournament.

The trick: Sinner doesn't try to hit "the perfect second serve." Instead, he has a reliable second serve at 170–185 km/h with clear kick — not the tour's biggest, but consistent and hard to attack. Cahill calls it "the 85% serve": 85% of his best, guaranteed in, no gambling.

His attitude toward double faults matters too. Many players get so obsessed with avoiding them that the second serve becomes too cautious and easy to attack. Sinner treats an occasional double fault as an acceptable cost of serving decisively. As long as the rate stays under 3%, nothing changes. And when a double fault does happen (rarely), he reacts exactly as he does after a forehand error: brief acknowledgment, then it's gone. Zimaglia confirms this is a deliberately trained skill: "We train recovery from mistakes, not the absence of mistakes."

## Serve-and-Volley as a Change of Pace

Few realize Sinner uses serve-and-volley as an occasional tactical tool — not often, but at the right moments to surprise an opponent used to his baseline patterns. It shows up most in the first use of the set (before the opponent has adjusted) or when he senses an opponent anticipating the return and standing too deep. In those spots, it ends the point before a rally can develop.

His volley isn't his strongest shot, but it's good enough for the purpose. He favors the punch volley over the slice volley — quicker and technically simpler, a sensible choice for a player who's primarily a baseliner. On fast indoor courts with low bounce, this tool is becoming more effective, and he's gradually developing it further for the late-season indoor swing.

## Serve and Return as One Foundation

Zoomed out, Sinner's serve and return aren't two separate skills — they're two halves of one consistent system: controlling time.

On serve, he controls time by choosing direction and pace that force an immediate reaction with no room to attack — the serve and the +1 shot that follows are a calculated sequence meant to end the point within two or three shots.

On return, he controls time by standing closer to the baseline and hitting earlier — stealing the server's recovery time and turning them from attacker into defender within the first few shots.

The unifying idea, in the team's own words: "Every point starts with a serve or a return. Both have to serve the goal of controlling rhythm and time. No shot is passive — even a defensive return has to put the opponent in a difficult spot." That's how Sinner and his coaching team approach every match.

> "Tennis doesn't begin after the serve or return. Tennis begins the moment I decide where to serve — and ends when I've executed that plan." — Jannik Sinner
