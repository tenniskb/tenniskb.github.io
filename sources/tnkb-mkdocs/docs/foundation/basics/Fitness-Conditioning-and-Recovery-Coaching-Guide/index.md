# Fitness, Conditioning & Recovery Coaching Guide

*The physical base underneath every technical fix.*

## Why This Matters

Technique work stalls when the body can't support it. A great forehand takeback means nothing if
your legs are too tired to get there by the third set. For players 40+, conditioning isn't about
looking athletic — it's about being able to reproduce good technique on point 6 of a 20-minute
game, not just point 1.

## Core Concepts

**Core strength** is the transmission link between your legs and your racket arm. Without it,
rotational power leaks and the arm/shoulder compensate — which is also why fitness and injury
prevention are two sides of the same coin.

**Flexibility** determines how far you can rotate and reach without strain. Elite players
maintain aggressive daily stretching routines specifically because range of motion under control
generates more racket-head speed with less effort — the same "whip not hammer" principle that
runs through this whole site.

**Agility and change-of-direction speed** matter more in recreational tennis than straight-line
speed. Most points are lost recovering from one shot to be ready for the next, not from a lack
of raw sprint speed.

**Endurance** for a rec player means being able to hold form in long rallies and third sets, not
marathon-level cardio. Interval-based conditioning (short bursts, short rest) mimics point
structure far better than steady-state jogging.

## Action Steps

1. **Twice-weekly core circuit** (planks, anti-rotation presses, dead bugs) — 15 minutes, done
   consistently beats an occasional long gym session.
2. **Daily 10-minute mobility routine** focused on hips, thoracic spine rotation, and shoulders —
   the three areas that most directly limit stroke range of motion.
3. **Interval conditioning**, 20-30 seconds of work / 20-30 seconds of rest, mimicking a rally —
   far more tennis-specific than distance running.
4. **Recovery basics**: sleep is the single highest-leverage recovery tool available to a 40+
   competitor; treat 7-8 hours as part of training, not an afterthought.
5. **Simple nutrition awareness**: hydration and steady blood sugar (avoiding large sugar spikes
   before matches) do more for on-court energy than any specific "diet."

## Common Mistakes

- Doing generic gym workouts unrelated to tennis movement patterns (pure steady-state cardio,
  isolated bicep curls) instead of rotational, multi-directional work.
- Skipping the warm-up and mobility work because it "isn't the fun part."
- Treating conditioning as separate from technique, when a tired body is where technique breaks
  down first.

## Quick Reference

| Element | Minimum Effective Dose | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Core work | 2x/week, 15 min | Protects back, transmits power |
| Mobility | Daily, 10 min | Preserves stroke range of motion |
| Interval conditioning | 2x/week | Match-specific endurance |
| Sleep | 7-8 hrs/night | Highest-leverage recovery tool |
