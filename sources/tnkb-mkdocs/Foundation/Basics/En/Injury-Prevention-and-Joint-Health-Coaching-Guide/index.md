# Injury Prevention & Joint Health Coaching Guide

*Why your elbow, back, and shoulder hurt — and what actually stops it.*

## Why This Matters

If you're a recreational player over 40 chasing a 4.0 rating, your body is the one piece of
equipment you can't replace. Most of the pain that ends adult tennis careers — tennis elbow,
lower back pain, rotator cuff irritation — isn't bad luck. It's almost always a technique or
load-management problem that shows up as an injury months later. Fix the cause, not just the
symptom.

## Core Concepts

**Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis).** Usually caused by leading with the arm instead of the
body on off-center or late-contact shots, and by grip pressure that's too tight through the whole
swing. If your forehand or one-handed backhand relies on the arm to generate pace instead of the
kinetic chain (legs → hips → trunk → arm), the tendons at the elbow absorb repeated shock they
were never designed to take.

**Lower back pain.** Comes from three main sources: hyperextension on the serve (arching too far
back at the trophy position), one-sided rotational overload from thousands of asymmetric
groundstrokes, and poor court-surface shock absorption (hard courts are worse than clay). Trunk
rotation without adequate core stability is the common thread.

**Shoulder and rotator cuff strain.** Overuse from serving with poor scapular loading, or from
compensating for a weak kinetic chain by muscling the ball with the shoulder alone.

## Action Steps

1. **Fix the chain before the symptom.** If your elbow or back hurts, the first place to look is
   footwork and timing — are you consistently late, forcing an arm-only save shot?
2. **Grip pressure audit.** Hold the racket at a 3-4 out of 10 pressure through most of the
   stroke, tightening only at contact. A constant white-knuckle grip is one of the most common
   and most fixable causes of elbow pain.
3. **Core stability work, 10 minutes, 3x/week.** Planks, side planks, and anti-rotation presses
   (Pallof press) build the trunk stability that protects the lower back during rotation.
4. **Serve trophy-position check.** Avoid excessive back-arch; a moderate, athletic bend loads
   the legs instead of the spine.
5. **String and racket setup.** Softer string tension (a few pounds lower than "tournament"
   tension) and a slightly more flexible racket frame reduce shock transmission to the elbow —
   a small equipment change with an outsized effect for players 40+.
6. **Warm up the rotational chain, not just the arm.** Torso twists and leg swings for 5 minutes
   before hitting matter more than arm circles alone.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating elbow pain with rest alone, then returning to the exact same faulty mechanics.
- Ignoring grip pressure because "it's always been like that."
- Serving with a big back-arch because it "feels like more power."

## Quick Reference

| Symptom | Likely Cause | First Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Elbow pain | Arm-only power, tight grip | Fix contact timing, loosen grip |
| Lower back pain | Serve hyperextension, weak core | Core work, moderate trophy position |
| Shoulder strain | Poor scapular loading on serve | Serve mechanics review, shoulder mobility |
