# ART-937: Speed Training for Tennis — Sprint Mechanics for Court Coverage

## Executive Summary

Speed in tennis is not about how fast a player can run 100 meters; it is about how quickly they can cover 5-8 meters in any direction, change direction, and recover to a neutral position. The average tennis point involves 4-6 directional changes within a 5-5 meter radius, with sprint distances rarely exceeding 15 meters. This means that acceleration ability — not maximum velocity — is the critical speed quality for tennis. Training sprint mechanics (acceleration posture, arm action, and foot placement), deceleration ability, and tennis-specific movement patterns can dramatically improve court coverage and reduce the gap between a player's physical potential and their on-court performance.

## Acceleration Mechanics

Acceleration is the ability to increase speed from a stationary or slow-moving position. In tennis, acceleration is used constantly: exploding from the ready position to a wide ball, pushing off for a serve, or closing the net. The mechanics of effective acceleration include:

### Body Position

- **Forward lean**: The torso leans forward approximately 30-45 degrees from vertical during the first 3-5 steps of acceleration. This aligns the body's center of mass with the direction of force application.
- **Low center of mass**: The hips are low, with the knees bent and the chest over the knees. This allows the athlete to apply force against the ground in a horizontal direction.
- **Head position**: The head is neutral, with the eyes looking forward (or toward the ball). Looking down at the ground causes the trunk to flex and the hips to rise, impairing acceleration.

### Force Application

- **Ground contact**: The foot strikes the ground behind the center of mass (during the first 2-3 steps), allowing the athlete to push the ground backward and propel the body forward.
- **Triple extension**: The hip, knee, and ankle of the driving leg extend simultaneously to apply maximum force against the ground.
- **Step length**: Steps are short and rapid during the initial acceleration phase, gradually lengthening as speed increases.

### Arm Action

- **Elbow at 90 degrees**: The arms are bent at approximately 90 degrees at the elbow.
- **Front-to-back motion**: The arms swing forward and backward in the direction of travel (not across the body). The hand should reach approximately chin height in front and hip height in back.
- **Synchronization**: The arm action is synchronized with the leg action — the right arm drives forward as the left leg drives forward, and vice versa.

## Maximum Velocity Mechanics

While tennis rarely involves true maximum velocity sprinting, the ability to reach higher speeds improves a player's ability to cover wide balls and finish points at the net. Maximum velocity sprinting differs from acceleration in several key ways:

- **Upright posture**: As speed increases, the torso becomes more upright, reaching full vertical at top speed.
- **Greater ground contact forces**: At maximum velocity, the foot strikes the ground more directly under the center of mass, with the emphasis on vertical force production (to maintain height) rather than horizontal force production (to accelerate).
- **Shorter ground contact times**: At top speed, the foot is in contact with the ground for only 80-100 milliseconds, requiring greater reactive strength and elastic energy return.
- **Increased stride frequency**: Stride length plateaus at maximum velocity, so further speed increases come from faster stride frequency.

For tennis players, maximum velocity work is best developed through flying sprints (e.g., a 10-meter acceleration zone followed by a 20-meter maximum velocity zone) or resisted sprints (sled pulls, hill sprints).

## Deceleration Training

Deceleration — the ability to reduce speed and change direction — is arguably more important for tennis than acceleration. Every sprint to a wide ball must be followed by a controlled deceleration that allows the player to set up for the next stroke. Poor deceleration mechanics lead to:

- **Wide stances**: The player cannot stop in time and must take extra steps to recover.
- **Knee valgus**: The knees collapse inward during deceleration, increasing ACL injury risk.
- **Excessive trunk lean**: The player leans backward during deceleration, impairing stroke production.

Deceleration training focuses on:

- **Eccentric strength**: The ability of the quadriceps and hamstrings to absorb force during the landing and stopping phase.
- **Soft landings**: Landing with hip and knee flexion (a "soft" landing) rather than a stiff-legged landing.
- **Controlled stopping**: Practicing the ability to stop within a predetermined distance (e.g., stopping within 3 steps after a 10-meter sprint).

Exercises for deceleration development include:

- **Deceleration landings**: Sprint 5-10 meters, then stop as quickly as possible within 3-4 steps. Emphasize a low center of mass and soft foot strikes.
- **Lateral deceleration**: Shuffle laterally, then stop suddenly and hold a low athletic stance.
- **Eccentric squats**: Lower into a squat over 3-5 seconds, emphasizing control during the lowering phase. This builds the eccentric quadriceps strength needed for deceleration.

## Tennis-Specific Sprint Patterns

Tennis movement is multidirectional and reactive. Speed training for tennis must include patterns that replicate the movement demands of the sport:

### Lateral Movement

- **Side shuffles**: Shuffle laterally without crossing the feet, maintaining a low athletic stance. This is the primary movement for covering wide balls on the same side of the body.
- **Crossover steps**: Use a crossover step to accelerate laterally when the ball is hit to the same side. The back leg crosses over the front leg to generate speed.
- **Lateral bounds**: Single-leg hops laterally, landing on the outside leg and immediately pushing back. This develops the lateral power and stability needed for wide forehands and backhands.

### Forward and Backward Movement

- **Split step and drive**: The fundamental tennis movement — a small hop (split step) as the opponent strikes the ball, followed by an explosive drive in the direction of the shot. Practice the split step timing and the first 3-4 steps of acceleration.
- **Backpedal**: Run backward while maintaining an upright torso and keeping the eyes forward. This is used when retreating behind the baseline for a lob or defensive shot.
- **Drop step**: A quick pivot and first step backward, used when the ball is hit behind the player.

### Reactive Speed

- **Mirror drills**: Face a partner who moves randomly in different directions; the player mirrors the partner's movements. This trains reactive speed and anticipation.
- **Ball-drop sprints**: A partner drops a ball from shoulder height; the player sprints to catch it before the second bounce. This trains reaction time and acceleration.
- **Shadow tennis**: Perform shadow swings and footwork patterns without a ball, emphasizing speed and precision of movement.

## Programming Speed Training

Speed training should be performed 2-3 times per week, with at least 48 hours between sessions to allow for neuromuscular recovery. A typical speed session:

1. **Warm-up** (10 minutes): Dynamic stretching, leg swings, A-skips, B-skips, high knees, and short accelerations.
2. **Speed drills** (15-20 minutes): 3-4 exercises, 3-4 sets each, with full recovery between sets (2-3 minutes) to maintain movement quality.
3. **Speed endurance** (10-15 minutes): Short sprints with incomplete recovery (e.g., 8 × 30 meters with 60 seconds rest) to develop the ability to maintain speed under fatigue.
4. **Cool-down** (5-10 minutes): Light jogging and static stretching.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player is slow to the ball despite good fitness | Poor acceleration mechanics | Late to wide balls, defensive positioning | Correct body position (forward lean, low hips); practice first-step drills |
| Player's first step is slow | Poor split step timing or weak drive | Always a step behind the ball | Practice split step timing; strengthen glutes and quads for the drive phase |
| Player over-runs wide balls | Inefficient deceleration | Out of position for next stroke | Add deceleration landing drills; emphasize soft, controlled stops |
| Player's speed drops in long matches | Insufficient speed endurance | Late-match court coverage decline | Add speed endurance sessions (repeated sprints with incomplete recovery) |
| Knee pain during direction changes | Poor deceleration mechanics, weak hamstrings | ACL and meniscus injury risk | Add eccentric hamstring work; correct landing mechanics; screen for movement deficits |
| Player's arm action is inefficient during sprints | Poor upper-body coordination | Reduced stride frequency and speed | Practice arm action drills; strengthen shoulders and upper back |

## Practical Application: "The 15-Minute Speed Session"

Perform this session 2-3 times per week. It covers acceleration, maximum velocity, deceleration, and tennis-specific movement patterns.

**Warm-up (5 minutes):**
- A-skips × 20 meters
- High knees × 20 meters
- Leg swings (forward and lateral) × 10 each leg
- Split step practice × 10 repetitions

**Speed drills (10 minutes):**
- Falling starts (fall forward, catch yourself with a sprint) — 3 × 10 meters
- Resisted sprints (sled or partner resistance) — 3 × 15 meters
- Lateral bounds — 3 × 5 per side
- Deceleration landings — 3 × 15 meters (sprint, stop within 4 steps)

**Speed endurance (5 minutes):**
- 8 × 30 meters with 60 seconds rest between repetitions (85-90% effort)

**Cool-down (5 minutes):**
- Light jogging × 2 minutes
- Static stretching (hamstrings, quads, hip flexors, calves) × 30 seconds each

Track your times for the 30-meter sprints and the speed endurance repetitions. When your times improve by 0.1-0.2 seconds or more, your on-court speed has measurably increased. Speed is a skill — it improves with practice, not just physical conditioning. The players who move best on court are not always the fastest in a straight line; they are the ones who can accelerate, decelerate, and change direction with precision and efficiency.
