# ART-638: The Backhand Volley on the Run — Wide Recovery Technique

## Executive Summary

The backhand volley on the run is hit while moving laterally at the net on the backhand side, typically to intercept a shot that pulls you wide to your backhand. It presents unique challenges because the arm must extend across the body while moving, reducing both reach and control. The key is to use the cross-step for maximum reach, rotate the shoulders to generate power, and prioritize recovery after the shot.

## The Challenge of the Wide Backhand Volley

Wide backhand volleys are more difficult than wide forehand volleys because: (1) the arm extends across the body rather than away from it, reducing reach, (2) the shoulders must rotate more to generate power, (3) the continental grip naturally opens the face on the backhand side, requiring extra face control, (4) recovery to the center is more difficult because you must rotate back against the direction of movement. These factors make the wide backhand volley one of the most error-prone shots at the net.

## The Cross-Step for Wide Backhand Volleys

For a right-handed player moving to the left (backhand side), the right foot crosses over the left foot, landing 12-18 inches to the left. The left foot then steps into the volley position. The cross-step covers more ground than the shuffle step and allows you to reach balls that would otherwise be unreachable. Keep the hips low and the racket in front of the body during the cross — do not let the racket trail behind.

## Shoulder Rotation on Wide Backhand Volleys

Because the arm extends across the body on the backhand side, shoulder rotation is essential for power and control. Rotate the shoulders aggressively — the back shoulder turns toward the ball during preparation, and the shoulders rotate back through contact. The front shoulder should point toward the target at contact. Without sufficient shoulder rotation, the wide backhand volley becomes an arm-only shot that lacks both power and direction.

## Contact Point on Wide Backhand Volleys

The contact point on wide backhand volleys is approximately 18-24 inches ahead of the front foot, similar to the wide forehand volley. However, the racket face must be more carefully controlled because the continental grip naturally opens the face on the backhand side. Slightly close the face compared to a standard backhand volley to counteract the grip's natural opening and the lateral momentum.

## Balance and Recovery

After hitting a wide backhand volley, use the crossover step to recover to the center of the court. The left foot crosses over the right foot, allowing rapid repositioning to the right. The recovery is more difficult than after a wide forehand volley because you must rotate back against the direction of movement. Practice the recovery drill separately: cross-step to the left, volley, cross-step to the right to recover.

## Common Wide Backhand Volley Errors

Not using the cross-step — the shuffle step cannot cover enough ground on the backhand side. Failing to rotate the shoulders — arm-only shots lack power. Opening the face too much — the continental grip's natural opening is amplified on wide shots. Swinging too hard — lateral momentum adds pace, so a gentle redirect is sufficient. Not recovering — leaving the court open for the opponent's next shot.

## Tactical Application

Wide backhand volleys are typically hit: (1) when the opponent pulls you wide with a down-the-line passing shot, (2) when poaching in doubles on the backhand side, (3) when covering a wide serve in doubles. The goal is to keep the ball in play and recover — do not attempt winners. Aim deep to the open court or cross-court for the safest options.

## Wide Backhand Volley Drills

The "wide backhand volley feed" drill feeds wide balls to the backhand side. The "wide backhand recovery" drill practices volleying wide and recovering to the center. The "competitive wide backhand volley" game rewards successful wide backhand volleys and penalizes failure to recover. The "poaching backhand" drill practices wide backhand volleys while poaching in doubles.

## Summary

The wide backhand volley on the run is one of the most difficult shots at the net. It demands cross-step movement, shoulder rotation, careful face control, and quick recovery. Practice the recovery drill separately and focus on keeping the ball in play rather than attempting winners.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cannot reach wide backhand volleys | Using shuffle instead of cross-step | Frame shots, missed volleys | Drill cross-step for wide lateral movement on backhand |
| Wide backhand volley lacks power | Insufficient shoulder rotation | Weak volley, easy return | Rotate shoulders aggressively through contact |
| Wide backhand volley goes long | Face opens too much on contact | Ball past baseline | Close face slightly, firm wrist |
| No recovery after wide volley | Standing and watching | Court left open | Practice crossover recovery steps |
| Off-balance on wide backhand volley | Lunging with arm instead of feet | Falls, errors | Move feet first, rotate shoulders, then extend arm |

## Practical Application: "Rotate, Don't Reach"

On the wide backhand volley, the instinct is to reach out with the arm. Resist this. Instead, rotate your shoulders and let the cross-step bring you to the ball. The shoulder rotation generates the power and control that arm-reaching cannot provide.
