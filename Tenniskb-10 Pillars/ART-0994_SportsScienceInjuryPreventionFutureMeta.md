# ART-994: Nutrigenomics and Tennis — Personalized Nutrition Based on DNA

## Executive Summary

Nutrigenomics — the science of how individual genetic variation affects nutritional responses — is transforming tennis nutrition from a one-size-fits-all approach to a precision discipline. By analyzing genes involved in caffeine metabolism, vitamin D utilization, inflammation response, and macronutrient processing, sports nutritionists can now design dietary strategies tailored to each player's genetic profile. This article examines the key genetic variants relevant to tennis performance, the evidence supporting personalized nutrition interventions, and practical applications for players seeking to optimize their diet through genetic insight.

## The Genetic Basis of Nutritional Response

Individual responses to identical foods and supplements vary enormously. One athlete may experience dramatic performance improvement from caffeine supplementation, while another feels only jitters and anxiety. One player may maintain optimal vitamin D levels with modest sun exposure, while another requires aggressive supplementation. These differences are largely genetic.

Nutrigenomics identifies single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) — single-letter variations in DNA sequence — that alter enzyme activity, receptor sensitivity, and metabolic pathways. By testing for specific SNPs, practitioners can predict how an individual will respond to various nutritional inputs and adjust recommendations accordingly.

For tennis, the most clinically relevant genetic variants fall into several categories: stimulant metabolism, micronutrient utilization, inflammatory response, and macronutrient processing.

## Caffeine Metabolism: The CYP1A2 Gene

Caffeine is the most widely used ergogenic aid in tennis, with research consistently showing 3-5% improvements in reaction time, endurance, and perceived exertion. However, the CYP1A2 gene determines whether an individual is a "fast" or "slow" caffeine metabolizer, dramatically altering the supplement's effects.

The CYP1A2 enzyme, produced in the liver, is responsible for breaking down approximately 95% of ingested caffeine. A single SNP (rs762551) at position -163 determines enzyme activity:

- **AA genotype (fast metabolizers)** — clear caffeine rapidly, experience performance benefits with minimal side effects, optimal dose 3-6 mg/kg body weight 45-60 minutes before play
- **AC genotype (intermediate metabolizers)** — moderate clearance, may experience benefits at lower doses (2-3 mg/kg) with reduced side effects
- **CC genotype (slow metabolizers)** — clear caffeine slowly, experience prolonged stimulation, increased anxiety, sleep disruption, and potential performance impairment at standard doses

For tennis specifically, slow metabolizers who consume caffeine before afternoon matches may still have elevated caffeine levels at bedtime, disrupting sleep quality and next-day performance. A 2018 study in the Journal of Sports Sciences found that fast metabolizers improved serve accuracy by 4.2% with caffeine, while slow metabolizers showed no significant improvement and reported increased anxiety.

The practical implication is profound: genetic testing for CYP1A2 can determine whether caffeine supplementation is beneficial, harmful, or neutral for a specific player — and at what dose and timing.

## Vitamin D Receptor Variants

Vitamin D is critical for tennis players, influencing bone health, muscle function, immune regulation, and injury prevention. However, the vitamin D receptor (VDR) gene contains multiple SNPs that affect how efficiently cells respond to vitamin D:

- **FokI polymorphism (rs2228570)** — the T allele produces a shorter, more active VDR protein, potentially requiring lower vitamin D intake for optimal function
- **BsmI polymorphism (rs1544410)** — the G allele is associated with reduced VDR activity, potentially requiring higher vitamin D levels
- **TaqI polymorphism (rs731236)** — variants affect calcium absorption and bone mineral density

Tennis players with VDR variants associated with reduced receptor activity may need serum 25(OH)D levels of 50-60 ng/mL (rather than the standard 30-40 ng/mL target) to achieve optimal muscle function and bone health. This is particularly relevant for players training indoors or in northern latitudes with limited sun exposure.

## Inflammation and Recovery: The IL-6 Gene

The interleukin-6 (IL-6) gene promoter region contains a SNP (rs1800795) at position -174 that influences inflammatory response to exercise:

- **GG genotype** — higher IL-6 production, greater inflammatory response to intense exercise, potentially slower recovery
- **CC genotype** — lower IL-6 production, reduced inflammatory response, potentially faster recovery
- **GC genotype** — intermediate response

For tennis players, the GG genotype may indicate a need for enhanced anti-inflammatory nutrition strategies: higher omega-3 fatty acid intake (3-4 g/day EPA+DHA), tart cherry juice concentrate (30 mL twice daily during tournaments), and curcumin supplementation (500-1,000 mg/day). Players with the CC genotype may require less aggressive anti-inflammatory support and can focus nutritional resources elsewhere.

## Lactose Intolerance and the LCT Gene

The LCT gene determines lactase persistence — the ability to digest lactose into adulthood. The rs4988235 SNP at position -13910 determines lactase activity:

- **TT genotype** — lactase persistent, can consume dairy without issues
- **CT genotype** — reduced lactase activity, may experience mild symptoms with large dairy intake
- **CC genotype** — lactase non-persistent, likely lactose intolerant

For tennis players, this matters because dairy is a primary source of high-quality protein (whey, casein) and calcium. Players with CC genotype must seek alternative protein sources (pea, rice, soy protein isolates) and ensure adequate calcium from non-dairy sources (fortified plant milks, leafy greens, sardines) or supplements.

## MTHFR and Folate Metabolism

The MTHFR gene encodes methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase, an enzyme critical for folate metabolism and homocysteine regulation. The C677T SNP (rs1801133) reduces enzyme activity:

- **CC genotype** — normal MTHFR activity (100%)
- **CT genotype** — reduced activity (65%)
- **TT genotype** — significantly reduced activity (30%)

Reduced MTHFR activity impairs folate metabolism, potentially elevating homocysteine levels and affecting recovery, cardiovascular health, and cognitive function. Tennis players with TT genotype may benefit from methylfolate supplementation (L-methylfolate, 400-800 mcg/day) rather than standard folic acid, as they cannot efficiently convert synthetic folic acid to its active form.

## Practical Implementation

Implementing nutrigenomics in tennis involves several steps:

1. **Genetic testing** — saliva or blood sample analyzed for relevant SNPs (commercial panels available from 23andMe, AncestryDNA, or specialized sports genetics companies)
2. **Interpretation** — sports nutritionist translates genetic results into actionable recommendations
3. **Baseline assessment** — current diet, blood work (vitamin D, iron, B12, homocysteine), and performance metrics
4. **Personalized plan** — dietary modifications based on genetic profile combined with individual needs
5. **Monitoring** — regular blood work and performance tracking to validate interventions

## Practical Application: "The Genetic Nutrition Blueprint"

Every competitive tennis player should consider genetic testing as part of their nutritional optimization strategy. Start with a comprehensive panel covering CYP1A2 (caffeine), VDR (vitamin D), IL-6 (inflammation), LCT (lactose), and MTHFR (folate). Work with a sports nutrigenomics specialist to translate results into specific, measurable interventions: if you are a CYP1A2 slow metabolizer, eliminate caffeine after 2 PM and limit pre-match intake to 2 mg/kg; if you carry VDR variants, target serum vitamin D of 50-60 ng/mL with appropriate supplementation; if you are IL-6 GG, prioritize omega-3 intake at 3-4 g/day during tournament blocks. Reassess blood markers every 3 months and adjust accordingly. The era of generic tennis nutrition is ending — the players who embrace genetic personalization will gain measurable advantages in recovery, energy management, and long-term health.
