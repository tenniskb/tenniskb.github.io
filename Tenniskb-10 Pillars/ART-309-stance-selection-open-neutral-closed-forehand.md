# ART-309: Stance Selection — Open, Neutral, and Closed on the Forehand Side

## Executive Summary

The three primary forehand stances—open, neutral, and closed—each offer distinct biomechanical advantages and tactical applications that elite players select based on court position, ball characteristics, and shot intention. The open stance (feet parallel to the net) facilitates rapid recovery and hip-driven power but limits linear weight transfer. The neutral stance (feet at 45 degrees) balances rotational power with directional control. The closed stance (front foot across the body) maximizes linear weight transfer and directional accuracy but slows recovery. Modern forehand technique requires proficiency in all three stances, with the ability to transition seamlessly between them.

## The Open Stance Forehand

In the open stance, both feet are approximately parallel to the net or angled slightly toward the sideline, with the hips and shoulders facing the net at the set position. This stance is the most commonly used in modern tennis, particularly on the baseline, because it allows the player to begin recovery immediately after contact while still generating significant power through hip and trunk rotation.

The open stance's primary power source is rotational: the hips and trunk rotate against a relatively stable lower body, creating torque that drives the forward swing. Linear weight transfer is limited because the front foot cannot plant and block the forward momentum as effectively as in the closed stance. Research shows that open stance forehands generate 10 to 15 percent less ground reaction force than closed stance forehands but compensate with higher rotational velocities.

The open stance excels in wide forehand situations where the player must cover significant court distance and return quickly. It is also preferred for balls at or below waist height, where the hip rotation is more efficient than the linear drive required by the closed stance.

## The Neutral Stance Forehand

The neutral stance positions the feet at approximately 45 degrees to the baseline, with the front foot closer to the net than the rear foot but not crossed in front of the body. This intermediate position allows both rotational and linear power generation, making it the most versatile stance for forehand production.

The neutral stance is preferred for mid-court forehands where the player has sufficient time to set up but needs to generate both power and directional accuracy. The moderate hip rotation (approximately 60 to 75 degrees) combined with partial linear weight transfer creates a balanced power profile that adapts well to varying ball heights and speeds.

Players transitioning from closed to open stance technique often use the neutral stance as a stepping stone, as it provides the rotational benefits of the open stance while maintaining some of the directional control of the closed stance.

## The Closed Stance Forehand

The closed stance positions the front foot across the body (closer to the opposite sideline than the rear foot), effectively blocking hip rotation and forcing the forward swing to rely primarily on linear weight transfer and trunk rotation. This stance generates the highest ground reaction forces of the three options but limits recovery speed.

The closed stance is most appropriate for: (1) attacking balls in the strike zone with maximum power, (2) hitting passing shots where directional accuracy is paramount, and (3) generating maximum topspin on high-bouncing balls where the low-to-high swing path is most effective.

The primary disadvantage of the closed stance is recovery time: because the front foot is crossed over the body, the player must "uncross" before moving laterally, adding 0.1 to 0.2 seconds to the recovery phase. In modern tennis, where rallies are faster and court coverage demands are higher, this recovery penalty limits the closed stance to specific tactical situations.

## Stance Transitions and Adaptability

Elite forehand technique requires the ability to transition between stances based on the incoming ball's characteristics. A player who is committed to an open stance as the ball arrives may need to switch to a neutral stance if the ball is lower than anticipated, or to a closed stance if they decide to attack aggressively. These mid-preparation stance adjustments are a hallmark of advanced footwork and are trained through variable ball-feeding drills.

The transition from any stance to the open stance is faster than the reverse, because opening the hips requires less repositioning than closing them. Therefore, players often begin in a neutral or slightly closed stance and open up as needed, rather than beginning open and attempting to close.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Always using open stance | Limited power on attack opportunities | Predictable, attackable forehands | Practice closed stance on mid-court balls |
| Always using closed stance | Slow recovery; vulnerable to wide balls | Poor court coverage | Train open stance on wide ball drills |
| Inappropriate stance for ball height | Mechanical compromise; reduced efficiency | Inconsistent quality across ball heights | Drill stance selection by ball height zones |
| Feet too narrow in any stance | Reduced base of support; balance loss | Off-balance shots; directional errors | Maintain feet at least shoulder-width apart |
| Stance changes mid-swing | Disrupted timing; loss of power | Mishits; late adjustments | Commit to stance by set position; adjust earlier |

## Practical Application: "The Stance Menu"

During practice, consciously select a specific stance before each forehand. Call out "open," "neutral," or "closed" as you prepare, and execute the shot in that stance regardless of convenience. This deliberate selection trains the motor programs for each stance and develops the ability to choose the optimal stance based on the tactical situation rather than habit.
