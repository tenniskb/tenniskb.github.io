# ART-925: Diabetes and Tennis — Blood Glucose Management for Athletes

## Executive Summary
Tennis presents unique challenges for athletes with diabetes, particularly Type 1 diabetes, due to the sport's variable intensity, unpredictable duration, and the risk of hypoglycemia during and after exercise. Proper blood glucose management before, during, and after play allows diabetic tennis players to compete safely and effectively. Understanding how exercise affects blood glucose, recognizing hypoglycemia, and managing insulin and carbohydrate intake are essential skills for diabetic athletes.

## Exercise and Blood Glucose
Physical activity affects blood glucose in complex ways that differ between Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. In Type 1 diabetes, exercise typically causes blood glucose to fall because the exogenous insulin injected or pumped does not decrease in response to activity, while muscle glucose uptake increases. The risk of hypoglycemia is highest during and immediately after exercise, but can occur up to 24 hours later due to increased insulin sensitivity. In Type 2 diabetes, exercise typically improves blood glucose control by increasing insulin sensitivity and promoting glucose uptake by muscles. The American Diabetes Association recommends that individuals with diabetes engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, with resistance training 2-3 times per week.

## Pre-Exercise Blood Glucose Management
Before playing tennis, blood glucose should be checked and managed according to established guidelines. For individuals with Type 1 diabetes, the target pre-exercise blood glucose is typically 90-250 mg/dL (5.0-13.9 mmol/L). If blood glucose is below 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L), 15-30 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate should be consumed before exercise. If blood glucose is above 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L), ketones should be checked; if ketones are present, exercise should be postponed until ketones clear. If blood glucose is above 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L) without ketones, exercise may proceed with caution. Pre-exercise insulin dosing may need to be reduced, particularly for the insulin dose that covers the time period of exercise.

## Hypoglycemia Recognition and Treatment
Hypoglycemia (blood glucose below 70 mg/dL or 3.9 mmol/L) is the most common acute complication of exercise in Type 1 diabetes. Symptoms include shakiness, sweating, palpitations, hunger, anxiety, dizziness, confusion, and in severe cases, loss of consciousness or seizures. Neuroglycopenic symptoms (caused by insufficient glucose to the brain) include difficulty concentrating, blurred vision, and confusion. The "15-15 rule" guides treatment: consume 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes, and recheck blood glucose. If still below 70 mg/dL, repeat the treatment. Examples of 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate include 4 glucose tablets, 1 tube of glucose gel, 1/2 cup (4 oz) of fruit juice, or 1 tablespoon of honey or sugar. After blood glucose returns to normal, a small snack with protein and complex carbohydrate helps prevent recurrence.

## Insulin Pump Considerations
Insulin pump users have specific considerations for tennis. The pump can be worn during play, but the infusion site should be protected from impact and sweat. Some players disconnect the pump for short matches (under 60 minutes) to avoid delivering insulin during exercise, which increases hypoglycemia risk. However, disconnecting for extended periods can cause hyperglycemia and ketone production. The pump should be secured to the body using a pouch or armband, and the infusion site should be checked before, during, and after play. Insulin pump users should always carry a backup injection method (insulin pen or syringe) in case of pump failure. Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) are particularly valuable for athletes, providing real-time glucose readings and trend arrows.

## Post-Exercise Blood Glucose Management
After tennis, blood glucose should be checked immediately and monitored for several hours. Post-exercise hypoglycemia can occur 6-15 hours after activity as muscles replenish glycogen stores. A post-exercise snack containing carbohydrate and protein helps prevent late-onset hypoglycemia. Insulin sensitivity remains elevated for 24-48 hours after intense exercise, requiring potential adjustments to basal insulin rates and mealtiming bolus doses. Overnight monitoring is important after intense or prolonged exercise, as nocturnal hypoglycemia is a significant risk. Some players reduce their basal insulin rate by 10-20% after intense exercise to prevent overnight hypoglycemia.

## Type 2 Diabetes Considerations
For individuals with Type 2 diabetes, exercise is a cornerstone of blood glucose management. Tennis can be an excellent form of exercise for Type 2 diabetic individuals, combining aerobic and resistance elements. The primary concern for Type 2 diabetic athletes is generally hyperglycemia rather than hypoglycemia, particularly if taking insulin or sulfonylureas. Medication adjustments may be needed as fitness improves, as exercise reduces insulin requirements over time. Regular monitoring of blood glucose helps identify patterns and guide medication adjustments.

## The Diabetes Athlete's Kit
Every diabetic tennis player should carry a diabetes kit that includes: blood glucose meter with test strips and lancets, continuous glucose monitor (if using), fast-acting carbohydrate (glucose tablets or gel), longer-acting carbohydrate (granola bar or crackers), insulin and delivery method (pen, syringe, or pump supplies), medical identification (bracelet or card), glucagon emergency kit (for severe hypoglycemia), and emergency contact information. The kit should be accessible from the court at all times — in a bag bench-side or in a cooler for insulin storage.

## Practical Application: "Monitor, Fuel, Play"
If you have Type 1 diabetes, check your blood glucose before every match and keep fast-acting carbohydrates court-side. During long matches or tournaments, check blood glucose during changeovers when possible. Learn how your body responds to tennis specifically — every athlete is different. Keep a log of your blood glucose patterns during play to identify trends and adjust your insulin and carbohydrate strategy. If you are a coach or training partner of a diabetic player, learn to recognize hypoglycemia and know how to assist. Diabetes should not prevent you from playing tennis — with proper management, you can compete at any level.
