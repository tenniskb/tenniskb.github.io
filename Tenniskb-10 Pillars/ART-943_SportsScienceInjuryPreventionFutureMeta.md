# ART-943: Supplements for Tennis — What Works and What Doesn't

## Executive Summary

The global supplement industry is worth over $150 billion, and tennis players are among its most aggressive marketing targets. Walk into any tennis academy or tournament venue and you will see shelves of products promising faster serves, quicker recovery, and endless energy. The reality is far more sober: only a handful of supplements have strong scientific evidence supporting their use in tennis, and many popular products are no more effective than placebo. This article separates the evidence-based supplements from the marketing hype, providing a clear guide for tennis players who want to make informed decisions about supplementation.

## The Evidence-Based Supplements

### Creatine Monohydrate

Creatine is one of the most researched supplements in sports science, with over 500 published studies. It works by increasing the availability of phosphocreatine in the muscle, which enhances the body's ability to produce ATP (the immediate energy source) during high-intensity, short-duration efforts — exactly the energy system that dominates tennis.

**Evidence for tennis**:
- Improves repeated sprint ability (the capacity to maintain speed across multiple points).
- Increases maximal strength and power output when combined with resistance training.
- May improve recovery between high-intensity efforts by enhancing phosphocreatine resynthesis.

**Dosing**:
- **Loading phase (optional)**: 20 grams per day (divided into 4 doses of 5 grams) for 5-7 days.
- **Maintenance phase**: 3-5 grams per day indefinitely.
- Loading is not necessary; it simply saturates the muscle stores faster. Without loading, saturation occurs in 3-4 weeks.

**Safety**: Creatine monohydrate is one of the safest supplements available. The most common side effect is mild water weight gain (1-2 kg), which is actually beneficial for tennis players in hot conditions (greater fluid reserve). Concerns about kidney damage have been thoroughly debunked in healthy individuals.

### Caffeine

Caffeine is the most widely used psychoactive substance in the world and one of the most well-supported ergogenic aids. It works by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain, reducing the perception of fatigue and increasing alertness, focus, and pain tolerance.

**Evidence for tennis**:
- Improves endurance performance by 2-5%.
- Enhances reaction time and decision-making speed.
- Increases pain tolerance, which may help players push through fatigue in long matches.
- Improves focus and concentration during long matches and training sessions.

**Dosing**:
- 3-6 mg per kilogram of body weight, consumed 30-60 minutes before competition.
- For a 70 kg player, this equals 210-420 mg (approximately 1-3 cups of coffee).
- Individual tolerance varies; start with a low dose (1-2 mg/kg) and experiment during training.

**Timing**: Caffeine peaks in the blood 30-60 minutes after consumption and has a half-life of 3-6 hours. For an afternoon match, avoid caffeine after 2 PM to avoid disrupting sleep.

**Safety**: Caffeine is generally safe at recommended doses. Side effects include jitteriness, anxiety, gastrointestinal distress, and insomnia. Habitual users develop tolerance, which may reduce the ergogenic effect. Avoid caffeine if you have heart arrhythmias or anxiety disorders.

### Beta-Alanine

Beta-alanine is an amino acid that combines with histidine in the muscle to form carnosine, a buffer that helps neutralize the hydrogen ions produced during high-intensity exercise. This delays the onset of muscular fatigue and improves performance in efforts lasting 1-4 minutes.

**Evidence for tennis**:
- May improve performance during repeated high-intensity efforts (the repeated sprint demands of tennis).
- May delay muscular fatigue during long rallies.
- Effects are most pronounced in efforts lasting 1-4 minutes, which matches the duration of many tennis points and games.

**Dosing**:
- 3-6 grams per day, divided into 2-3 doses (to reduce the harmless side effect of paresthesia — a tingling sensation in the skin).
- Effects take 2-4 weeks to manifest as carnosine stores gradually increase.

**Safety**: Beta-alanine is safe. The only side effect is paresthesia (tingling), which is harmless and can be avoided by dividing the dose.

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin that plays a critical role in bone health, immune function, and muscle function. Deficiency is common, particularly in players who train indoors or live in northern latitudes.

**Evidence for tennis**:
- Deficiency is associated with increased risk of stress fractures, muscle weakness, and impaired immune function.
- Supplementation in deficient individuals improves muscle strength and reduces injury risk.
- Optimal levels may support immune function during heavy training blocks.

**Dosing**:
- Test your blood level (25-hydroxyvitamin D). Optimal range: 40-60 ng/mL.
- If deficient (less than 30 ng/mL), supplement with 2,000-4,000 IU per day under medical supervision.
- If adequate, a maintenance dose of 1,000-2,000 IU per day is sufficient, especially in winter months.

**Safety**: Vitamin D toxicity is rare but can occur with very high doses (more than 10,000 IU per day for extended periods). Test your blood level before supplementing.

## Supplements with Limited or Mixed Evidence

### Branched-Chain Amino Acids (BCAAs)

BCAAs (leucine, isoleucine, and valine) are marketed for muscle recovery and reduced soreness. However, research shows that BCAAs are no more effective than consuming adequate total protein (1.4-1.7 g/kg per day) from whole food sources. If your protein intake is sufficient, BCAAs provide no additional benefit.

### Glutamine

Glutamine is the most abundant amino acid in the body and is marketed for immune support and muscle recovery. While it may benefit critically ill patients, research in healthy athletes shows no meaningful effect on muscle recovery, immune function, or performance.

### Tart Cherry Juice

Tart cherry juice has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties and has been studied for its effects on muscle soreness and recovery. Some studies show a small benefit for reducing delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), but the evidence is mixed and the effect size is small. It may be worth trying for players with significant muscle soreness, but it is not a priority supplement.

### Collagen Peptides

Collagen supplements are marketed for joint health and injury prevention. Some emerging evidence suggests that collagen peptides (10-15 grams per day, consumed with vitamin C) may support tendon and ligament health when combined with resistance training. However, the evidence is still early, and more research is needed before strong recommendations can be made.

## Supplements to Avoid

### Proprietary Blends

Many supplements contain "proprietary blends" — mixtures of ingredients where the individual doses are not disclosed. This is a red flag. If the manufacturer will not tell you how much of each ingredient is in the product, you cannot assess its safety or effectiveness.

### Weight Loss Supplements

Weight loss supplements are largely ineffective and sometimes dangerous. They often contain stimulants, laxatives, or untested herbal extracts. Avoid them entirely.

### Testosterone Boosters

Over-the-counter "testosterone boosters" (D-aspartic acid, tribulus terrestris, fenugreek, etc.) have minimal to no effect on testosterone levels in healthy individuals. They are a waste of money and may contain contaminants.

### Anabolic Steroids and Prohormones

These are illegal in sport, banned by WADA (World Anti-Doping Agency), and carry serious health risks (liver damage, cardiovascular disease, psychiatric effects, infertility). They have no place in legitimate sport.

## The Importance of Third-Party Testing

The supplement industry is poorly regulated, and contamination is common. Studies have found that 10-20% of supplements contain undeclared substances, including banned stimulants and anabolic agents. To protect yourself:

- Choose products that are third-party tested by NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport.
- Look for the NSF or Informed Sport logo on the label.
- Avoid supplements from unknown brands or those sold exclusively online with no third-party verification.

## A Hierarchical Approach to Supplements

Not all supplements are created equal. A useful framework for prioritization:

1. **Tier 1 (strong evidence)**: Creatine, caffeine, vitamin D (if deficient), beta-alanine.
2. **Tier 2 (moderate evidence)**: Tart cherry juice, collagen peptides (for joint health).
3. **Tier 3 (limited evidence)**: BCAAs (if protein intake is adequate), glutamine.
4. **Tier 4 (avoid)**: Proprietary blends, weight loss supplements, testosterone boosters, any product promising unrealistic results.

Focus your budget on Tier 1 supplements and whole-food nutrition. Everything else is marginal at best.

## Practical Application: "The Four-Supplement Foundation"

For most tennis players, four supplements cover the evidence-based basics:

1. **Creatine monohydrate** — 3-5 grams per day (improves power and repeated sprint ability)
2. **Caffeine** — 3-6 mg/kg, 30-60 minutes before matches (improves focus and endurance)
3. **Beta-alanine** — 3-6 grams per day (improves muscular buffering capacity)
4. **Vitamin D** — 1,000-4,000 IU per day, depending on blood level (supports bone health and immune function)

These four supplements, combined with a well-planned diet (Article 941) and proper hydration (Article 942), cover the nutritional foundations of tennis performance. Everything else — the fat burners, the test boosters, the recovery blends — is marketing. Spend your money on quality food, quality training, and these four evidence-based supplements. The margin between winning and losing is rarely found in a pill bottle.
