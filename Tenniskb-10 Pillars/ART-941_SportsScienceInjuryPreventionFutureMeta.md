# ART-941: Nutrition for Tennis — Fueling Performance

## Executive Summary

Tennis is one of the most nutritionally demanding sports in the world. Matches can last 3-5 hours, training days often include multiple sessions, and tournament schedules can compress 5-7 matches into consecutive days. The fuel demands are enormous: a competitive player can expend 500-1,000 calories per hour of match play, with carbohydrate being the primary fuel source. Meeting these demands requires a structured approach to macronutrient intake, meal timing, and match-day fueling. The research is clear: players who optimize their nutrition perform better in the third set, recover faster between matches, and sustain higher training volumes with fewer injuries.

## Macronutrient Needs for Tennis Players

### Carbohydrate

Carbohydrate is the primary fuel for high-intensity tennis performance. The body stores carbohydrate in the muscles and liver as glycogen, and these stores are limited (approximately 400-500 grams total). A single 3-hour match can deplete muscle glycogen by 50-75%. Replenishing these stores is the top nutritional priority.

Daily carbohydrate needs for tennis players:

- **Training days (moderate volume)**: 5-7 grams per kilogram of body weight (g/kg)
- **Training days (high volume or multiple sessions)**: 7-10 g/kg
- **Match days**: 6-8 g/kg, with a focus on pre-match and during-match intake
- **Rest days**: 3-5 g/kg, adjusted for body composition goals

For a 70 kg player, 5-7 g/kg equals 350-490 grams of carbohydrate per day. This is significantly more than the average sedentary intake and requires deliberate planning.

Sources: rice, pasta, bread, oats, potatoes, fruits, vegetables, legumes, and sports drinks.

### Protein

Protein is essential for muscle repair, immune function, and the synthesis of enzymes and hormones. Tennis players have elevated protein needs due to the muscle damage caused by high-intensity, eccentric movements (the deceleration phase of sprints and the follow-through of strokes).

Daily protein needs for tennis players:

- **General training**: 1.4-1.7 g/kg
- **Strength-building phase**: 1.6-2.0 g/kg
- **Injury rehabilitation**: 1.6-2.2 g/kg

For a 70 kg player, 1.4-1.7 g/kg equals 98-119 grams of protein per day. This should be distributed across 4-5 meals, each containing 20-40 grams of protein, to maximize muscle protein synthesis.

Sources: chicken, turkey, fish, eggs, dairy, tofu, legumes, and protein supplements (whey, casein, or plant-based).

### Fat

Fat is a secondary fuel source during tennis (particularly during low-intensity periods between points), a component of cell membranes, and essential for the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Fat intake should account for 20-35% of total daily calories.

Sources: olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds, fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), and whole eggs.

## Meal Timing for Tennis

Meal timing — when you eat relative to training and competition — can significantly affect performance and recovery.

### Pre-Training or Pre-Match Meal

Consume a meal 2-4 hours before the session. This meal should be:

- **High in carbohydrate**: To top off muscle glycogen stores. Target 1-2 g/kg of carbohydrate.
- **Moderate in protein**: To provide amino acids for muscle protein synthesis. Target 20-30 grams.
- **Low in fat and fiber**: To speed digestion and reduce gastrointestinal distress during play.

Example (2-3 hours before a match): Grilled chicken breast (150 g), white rice (200 g cooked), steamed vegetables (1 cup), and a banana.

### Pre-Training or Pre-Match Snack

Consume a snack 30-60 minutes before the session if the meal was more than 3 hours ago. This snack should be:

- **High in carbohydrate**: To provide readily available glucose. Target 30-60 grams.
- **Low in fat, fiber, and protein**: To speed digestion.

Example: A banana, a handful of dried fruit, or a sports bar (check label for carbohydrate content).

### During-Match Fueling

During matches longer than 60-90 minutes, carbohydrate intake becomes critical to maintain performance. The research supports consuming 30-60 grams of carbohydrate per hour during prolonged exercise, with some studies suggesting up to 90 grams per hour for events exceeding 2.5 hours (using multiple transportable carbohydrates like glucose and fructose).

Practical strategies:

- **During changeovers (every 2 games)**: Drink a sports drink (6-8% carbohydrate solution) or consume a banana, energy gel, or a few dates.
- **Avoid solid food during matches** unless it is well-tolerated (e.g., a small energy bar at a long changeover).
- **Hydration**: Pair carbohydrate intake with fluid to aid absorption and prevent dehydration.

### Post-Match Recovery

The 30-60 minutes after a match or training session is the "recovery window" — a period when the muscles are most receptive to glycogen replenishment and protein synthesis. Consume:

- **Carbohydrate**: 1.0-1.2 g/kg within 30 minutes, then another 1.0-1.2 g/kg within 2 hours.
- **Protein**: 20-40 grams to stimulate muscle protein synthesis.
- **Fluid**: 1.5 liters of fluid for every kilogram of body weight lost during play.

Example recovery meal: A smoothie with whey protein (30 g), banana (1), oats (50 g), berries (1 cup), and milk or water. Follow with a balanced meal (chicken, rice, vegetables) within 2 hours.

## Hydration and Electrolytes

Hydration is addressed in detail in Article 942, but it is worth noting here that fluid and electrolyte balance is inseparable from nutrition. A sports drink containing sodium (300-600 mg per liter) and carbohydrate (6-8%) serves a dual purpose: it provides fuel and promotes fluid retention and absorption.

## Supplements for Tennis

Supplements are addressed in detail in Article 943. The key takeaway: most tennis players can meet their nutritional needs through whole foods. Supplements (creatine, caffeine, beta-alanine, vitamin D, and protein powder) may provide marginal gains but are not substitutes for a well-planned diet.

## Common Nutritional Mistakes in Tennis

- **Under-fueling on match days**: Players often eat less than they expend, leading to glycogen depletion and late-match fatigue. Track your intake and expenditure during a tournament to identify gaps.
- **Over-reliance on supplements**: Protein bars and shakes are convenient but should not replace whole foods, which provide a broader spectrum of nutrients.
- **Poor recovery nutrition**: Players who skip the post-match recovery meal or snack delay glycogen replenishment and muscle repair, compromising the next day's performance.
- **Inadequate hydration**: Even mild dehydration (2% of body weight) impairs cognitive function, decision-making, and endurance. Hydration is a nutritional issue, not just a fluid issue.
- **Ignoring individual variability**: Some players tolerate high-carbohydrate loads well; others experience gastrointestinal distress. Experiment during training to find what works for you.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player fades in the third set of matches | Inadequate carbohydrate intake or glycogen depletion | Late-match performance decline | Increase daily carbohydrate to 7-10 g/kg; add during-match fueling |
| Player loses weight during tournaments | Caloric deficit from under-fueling | Fatigue, reduced performance, injury risk | Track intake vs. expenditure; increase carbohydrate and protein on match days |
| Player is sore and fatigued for days after matches | Insufficient recovery nutrition | Impaired training quality, accumulated fatigue | Add post-match recovery meal with 1.0-1.2 g/kg carbohydrate and 20-40 g protein |
| Gastrointestinal distress during matches | Poor meal timing or intolerable foods | Cramping, bloating, reduced performance | Experiment with low-fiber, low-fat pre-match meals; avoid new foods on match days |
| Player relies heavily on supplements | Whole-food nutrition gaps | Suboptimal micronutrient intake | Build meals around whole foods; use supplements only to fill specific gaps |
| Player's energy levels crash between matches | Inadequate recovery nutrition and hydration | Poor performance in next-day matches | Implement post-match nutrition protocol; hydrate aggressively between matches |

## Practical Application: "The Tournament Day Nutrition Plan"

For a player competing in a tournament with one match per day, here is a sample nutrition timeline:

**Breakfast (3 hours before match)**: Oats (80 g) with banana, honey, and milk; 2 eggs; 1 slice of toast with jam. (Approximately 80 g carbohydrate, 25 g protein.)

**Pre-match snack (1 hour before)**: A sports bar (40 g carbohydrate) and a sip of sports drink.

**During match (changeovers)**: Sip sports drink (6-8% carbohydrate) every changeover; eat a banana or a few dates at the set break.

**Post-match recovery (within 30 minutes)**: Smoothie with whey protein (30 g), banana, oats, and berries. (Approximately 70 g carbohydrate, 30 g protein.)

**Post-match meal (within 2 hours)**: Grilled chicken (150 g), white rice (250 g cooked), steamed vegetables, and a glass of fruit juice.

**Dinner (evening)**: Salmon (150 g), sweet potato (200 g), salad with olive oil dressing, and a bowl of fruit.

This plan provides approximately 400-500 grams of carbohydrate and 120-140 grams of protein on a match day, meeting the elevated demands of competition. Adjust portions based on body weight (target 6-8 g/kg carbohydrate and 1.4-1.7 g/kg protein). The key is consistency — eat the same way before every match so your body adapts and your glycogen stores are reliably topped off.
