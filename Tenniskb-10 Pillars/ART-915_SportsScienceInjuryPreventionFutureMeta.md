# ART-915: Hip Mobility and Tennis — The Missing Link in Power Generation

## Executive Summary
The hips are the engine of tennis power, serving as the critical link between the ground reaction forces generated by the legs and the rotational forces delivered to the racket. Hip mobility — particularly internal and external rotation — is essential for efficient movement, powerful groundstrokes, and injury prevention. Restrictions in hip mobility are among the most common yet overlooked limitations in tennis players, contributing to reduced power output and increased risk of lower back, knee, and hip injuries.

## Hip Anatomy and Tennis Demands
The hip joint is a ball-and-socket joint capable of flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. In tennis, the hip is required to move through large ranges of motion in multiple planes simultaneously. During a serve, the lead hip undergoes significant internal rotation and flexion during the cocking phase, followed by explosive external rotation and extension during the acceleration phase. During groundstrokes, the hips rotate to generate torque, with the open stance forehand requiring the lead hip to internally rotate and the trail hip to externally rotate. Lateral movement patterns require hip abduction and adduction. Research shows that elite tennis players typically demonstrate hip internal rotation of 35-45 degrees and external rotation of 45-55 degrees, well above population averages.

## Common Mobility Restrictions
Sitting for extended periods, a hallmark of modern lifestyle, causes adaptive shortening of the hip flexors (particularly the iliopsoas and rectus femoris) and inhibition of the gluteal muscles. This pattern, known as lower crossed syndrome, results in an anterior pelvic tilt and reduced hip extension. In tennis players, additional restrictions develop from the repetitive rotational demands of the sport. The dominant hip often develops greater external rotation but reduced internal rotation due to the repetitive nature of groundstrokes and serves. Common restrictions include limited hip internal rotation (less than 25 degrees), limited hip extension (less than 10 degrees), and limited hip flexion (less than 110 degrees). These restrictions force compensatory movements at the lumbar spine, knee, and shoulder, increasing injury risk and reducing power transfer.

## Assessment Protocols
Several clinical tests can assess hip mobility in tennis players. The FABER test (Flexion, ABduction, External Rotation) assesses combined hip mobility and identifies restrictions in the hip joint or surrounding tissues. The internal rotation test, performed in supine with the hip and knee flexed to 90 degrees, measures the available range of hip internal rotation. The external rotation test in the same position measures external rotation. The 90/90 test assesses the ability to internally and externally rotate the hip in a functional position. The Thomas test evaluates hip flexor length by having the patient lie supine, flex one hip to the chest, and observe whether the opposite leg remains flat on the table. A comprehensive assessment should also include the squat assessment to observe how hip mobility limitations affect functional movement patterns.

## Mobility Programs
An effective hip mobility program for tennis players should address all planes of motion and both hips, with additional attention to the dominant side. Key exercises include the 90/90 hip switch, which develops internal and external rotation in a functional position. The pigeon pose and its variations target hip external rotation and flexion. The couch stretch addresses hip flexor length and anterior pelvic positioning. The lateral lunge develops adductor length and hip mobility in the frontal plane. The hip airplane drill trains hip stability through full ranges of rotation. Foam rolling the hip flexors, glutes, and tensor fasciae latae before mobility work can improve tissue quality and enhance the effectiveness of stretching. Each session should last 15-20 minutes and be performed at least 3-4 times per week, with daily practice for players with significant restrictions.

## Hip Mobility and Power
Research has established a clear link between hip mobility and power output in tennis. Players with greater hip internal rotation on the dominant side generate higher racket head speeds on the forehand and greater serving speeds. The mechanism is straightforward: greater hip range of motion allows for a longer effective lever arm and greater elastic energy storage in the trunk muscles. A mobility program of 6-8 weeks has been shown to improve hip internal rotation by 8-15 degrees and increase serve speed by 3-5% in collegiate players.

## Hip Mobility and Injury Prevention
Restricted hip mobility is a significant risk factor for several tennis injuries. Limited hip internal rotation is associated with femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) and labral tears. Reduced hip extension increases stress on the lumbar spine, contributing to disc injuries and facet joint irritation. Limited hip flexion and internal rotation alter knee tracking, increasing the risk of patellofemoral pain syndrome and meniscal injuries. Addressing hip mobility through a structured program can reduce the incidence of these injuries and improve movement quality.

## Practical Application: "Unlock Your Hip Power"
Begin every practice session with a 10-minute hip mobility routine. Start with foam rolling the hip flexors and glutes for 2 minutes each. Perform the 90/90 hip switch for 2 minutes per side, focusing on controlled rotation. Hold the couch stretch for 90 seconds per side. Finish with 5 slow, deep squats, focusing on maintaining an upright torso and full depth. Track your progress by measuring hip internal rotation monthly. You should notice improvements in your ability to rotate into open stance forehands, reach wide balls without losing balance, and generate power without arm tension. The hips truly are the missing link in tennis power generation.
