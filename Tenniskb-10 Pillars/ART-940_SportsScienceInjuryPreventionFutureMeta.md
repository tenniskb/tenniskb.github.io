# ART-940: On-Court Conditioning Drills — Suicide Sprints, Star Drills, and More

## Executive Summary

While off-court conditioning (gym work, track sessions, and cycling) builds the aerobic and anaerobic engine, on-court conditioning drills provide the sport-specific transfer that makes that engine usable in a match. Drills like suicide sprints, star drills, and interval rallies replicate the movement patterns, work-rest ratios, and psychological demands of actual tennis. When programmed with appropriate volume, intensity, and progression, on-court conditioning drills can improve a player's ability to maintain speed, power, and decision-making quality under fatigue — the factors that determine match outcomes in the third, fourth, and fifth sets.

## The Rationale for On-Court Conditioning

Off-court conditioning is general; on-court conditioning is specific. A player can have an excellent VO2max on a bike but still gas out in a five-set match because the bike does not train the specific movement patterns, the repeated sprint ability, or the cognitive demands of tennis. On-court conditioning drills address these gaps by:

- **Replicating match movement patterns**: Multi-directional sprints, lateral movements, and split steps train the neuromuscular coordination needed for tennis-specific movement.
- **Matching work-rest ratios**: Drills can be designed with the same work-rest ratios as tennis points (e.g., 10 seconds of work, 20 seconds of rest).
- **Integrating decision-making**: Drills that involve reacting to a partner's direction or hitting actual shots train the brain to make decisions under fatigue — a critical match skill.
- **Providing mental conditioning**: The discomfort of conditioning drills builds the mental toughness needed to push through fatigue in competition.

## Suicide Sprints

Suicide sprints are a classic conditioning drill that involves running to progressively farther points on the court and returning to the starting point. The standard tennis suicide drill:

1. Start at the baseline.
2. Sprint to the service line and back.
3. Sprint to the net and back.
4. Sprint to the opposite service line and back.
5. Sprint to the opposite baseline and back.

This sequence covers approximately 100 meters of sprinting per repetition. Perform 4-8 repetitions with 60-90 seconds of rest between repetitions. The heart rate should reach 85-95% of maximum during each repetition and drop to 60-70% during the rest period.

**Progression**: Increase the number of repetitions, reduce the rest period, or add a split step before each sprint direction. For example, a more advanced version might include a split step and a shadow swing at each line, integrating tennis-specific movement into the conditioning.

## Star Drills

Star drills involve moving in multiple directions from a central point, replicating the star-like movement patterns of tennis. Set up five cones (or use court lines) in a star pattern around a central starting point. The player sprints from the center to each point and back, in a predetermined or random order.

**Random star drill**: A partner or coach calls out directions (e.g., "net, right, baseline, left, service line"), and the player must react and sprint to each point. This adds a cognitive demand to the physical conditioning, closely matching the reactive nature of tennis.

**Continuous star drill**: Perform the star drill continuously for a set time (e.g., 60-90 seconds) without rest between repetitions. This develops the ability to maintain movement quality under accumulating fatigue.

## Interval Rally Drills

Interval rally drills combine conditioning with technical practice, making them the most tennis-specific conditioning tool. The player rallies with a partner (or a coach feeding balls) for a set period, then rests.

**30-30 intervals**: Rally for 30 seconds at a high intensity (80-90% of maximum heart rate), rest for 30 seconds. Repeat 10-15 times. This closely matches the work-rest ratio of individual tennis points.

**Set simulation**: Play a full set with regulation rest periods (25 seconds between points, 90 seconds at changeovers). Maintain a competitive intensity throughout. This is the most specific conditioning drill and should be performed 1-2 times per week during the in-season.

**Rally endurance**: Rally continuously for 8-12 minutes at a moderate-to-high intensity. This develops the aerobic capacity and lactate clearance needed for extended baseline exchanges.

## Speed and Agility Drills

These drills focus on the acceleration, deceleration, and change-of-direction abilities that tennis demands.

- **T-drill**: Set up four cones in a T shape. Sprint forward, shuffle right, shuffle left, then backpedal to the start. This trains forward acceleration, lateral movement, and backward running.
- **Pro agility drill**: Set up three cones in a line, 5 yards apart. Sprint to the middle cone, touch the line, sprint to the far cone, touch the line, and sprint back to the start. This trains acceleration, deceleration, and 180-degree change of direction.
- **Spider drill**: Place five cones around the court (baseline center, both baseline corners, both service line corners). Start at the center and sprint to each cone and back in a predetermined order. This trains multi-directional movement and court coverage.

## Heart Rate Monitoring and Training Zones

Heart rate monitoring provides objective feedback on conditioning intensity and ensures the athlete is training in the appropriate zone. Use a chest strap monitor (more accurate than wrist-based monitors) and track:

- **Average heart rate during the session**: Should be in Zone 3-4 (70-90% of max HR) for conditioning sessions.
- **Heart rate recovery**: How quickly the heart rate drops during rest periods. A well-conditioned player's heart rate should drop 20-30 beats per minute during a 60-second rest.
- **Heart rate variability (HRV)**: HRV measures the variation in time between heartbeats and is a marker of recovery status. Low HRV indicates incomplete recovery and may warrant a lighter training day.

## Progressive Overload in On-Court Conditioning

Like strength training, on-court conditioning drills should be progressively overloaded to drive continued adaptation. Methods of progression include:

- **Volume progression**: Increase the number of repetitions (e.g., from 6 to 8 suicide sprints).
- **Intensity progression**: Reduce the rest period (e.g., from 90 to 60 seconds between repetitions) or increase the work period (e.g., from 30 to 45 seconds).
- **Complexity progression**: Add cognitive demands (e.g., reactive star drills) or technical elements (e.g., hitting actual shots during interval drills).
- **Density progression**: Perform the same volume of work in less time (e.g., complete 8 suicide sprints in 12 minutes instead of 15).

## Programming On-Court Conditioning

On-court conditioning should be integrated into the weekly training schedule based on the annual phase:

- **Preparatory phase**: 2-3 sessions per week, with a mix of suicide sprints, star drills, and interval rallies. Volume is high, intensity is moderate.
- **Pre-competition phase**: 2 sessions per week, emphasizing set simulations and high-intensity interval rallies. Volume decreases, intensity increases.
- **In-season**: 1-2 sessions per week, focusing on maintaining conditioning through set simulations and short, sharp interval drills. Volume and intensity are managed around the tournament schedule.
- **Off-season**: On-court conditioning is replaced by cross-training (cycling, swimming, running) and recreational activity.

A sample on-court conditioning session (45-60 minutes):

1. **Warm-up** (10 minutes): Dynamic stretching, split steps, and short accelerations.
2. **Suicide sprints** (10 minutes): 6 repetitions, 75 seconds rest between repetitions.
3. **Interval rallies** (15 minutes): 30-30s, 12 repetitions.
4. **Star drill** (10 minutes): Continuous star drill, 90 seconds on, 60 seconds rest, 3 repetitions.
5. **Cool-down** (10 minutes): Light jogging and static stretching.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player's speed drops in the third set | Insufficient sport-specific conditioning | Late-match decline in court coverage | Add set simulation and interval rally drills 2x per week |
| Player's heart rate stays elevated between drills | Poor aerobic base | Incomplete recovery, accumulated fatigue | Add Zone 2 training off-court; reduce drill intensity temporarily |
| Player cannot complete conditioning drills | Excessive volume or intensity without progression | Overtraining, poor adherence | Reduce volume 30-50%; progress more gradually |
| Player's movement quality degrades under fatigue | Insufficient conditioning or mental toughness | Late-match errors and slow movement | Add high-intensity drills with technical elements; emphasize mental resilience |
| Player's heart rate fails to reach target zones | Drills not intense enough or too much rest | Inadequate conditioning stimulus | Reduce rest periods; increase work intensity |
| Player is sore for days after conditioning | Excessive eccentric load or insufficient recovery | Impaired subsequent sessions | Add foam rolling and active recovery; reduce drill volume |

## Practical Application: "The 20-Minute Match Simulator"

This drill is the most tennis-specific conditioning tool available. It requires only a partner and a heart rate monitor.

Play a best-of-three-sets match with regulation rest periods. Track your heart rate during each set and during each changeover. Your goal is to maintain a competitive intensity throughout all three sets, with heart rate peaks in Zone 4-5 during rallies and recovery to Zone 2-3 during changeovers.

After the match, analyze your heart rate data:
- Did your heart rate peak higher in the third set than the first? (This is normal, but the increase should not be excessive.)
- Did your heart rate recover adequately during changeovers? (A drop of 20-30 bpm is a sign of good conditioning.)
- Did your movement quality decline in the third set? (If so, you need more conditioning — or more recovery between matches.)

Perform this drill once every 2-3 weeks during the in-season. It is the ultimate test of your tennis conditioning — and the best preparation for the physical and mental demands of competition. The player who can maintain speed, power, and decision-making in the third set of a five-set match is the player who wins the close ones.
