# ART-312: Knee Flexion and Leg Drive in the Modern Forehand

## Executive Summary

Knee flexion—the degree of bend in the knees during the forehand stroke—is the primary determinant of leg drive quality and ground force production. Elite forehands demonstrate a characteristic pattern of deep knee flexion in both legs during the loading phase (110 to 135 degrees) followed by explosive extension during the forward swing (150 to 175 degrees at contact). This flexion-extension cycle converts elastic energy stored in the quadriceps and gluteal muscles into the ground reaction forces that power the entire kinetic chain. Insufficient knee flexion is the most common lower-body technical failure in recreational forehands.

## Knee Flexion During the Loading Phase

During the unit turn and hip loading, both knees flex to absorb the player's weight and prepare for the explosive extension that drives the forward swing. The degree of flexion varies by stance: closed stance forehands show the deepest flexion (110 to 125 degrees in the rear knee), while open stance forehands show more moderate flexion (120 to 135 degrees in both knees).

The flexion is not passive; it is an active eccentric contraction of the quadriceps that stores elastic energy in the muscle-tendon unit. This stored energy is then released during the concentric extension phase, amplifying the force produced by the muscles alone. The stretch-shortening cycle in the knee extensors can increase force production by 20 to 40 percent compared to a concentric-only contraction.

## The Drive Phase: Knee Extension

The forward swing is powered by knee extension—the straightening of the legs that drives the body upward and forward. The rear knee extends first, initiating the hip rotation and linear weight transfer. The front knee extends second, providing the platform for the rotational force to be applied to the ground.

At contact, the front knee is typically extended to 150 to 175 degrees (nearly straight but not locked), while the rear knee may still be slightly flexed (140 to 160 degrees) depending on the stance and follow-through pattern. The differential between front and rear knee extension is what creates the rotational momentum: the front leg acts as a brace while the rear leg drives through it.

## The Relationship Between Knee Flexion and Power

Research demonstrates a strong correlation (r = 0.72 to 0.85) between peak knee flexion angle during the loading phase and peak racquet head speed at contact. Players who bend their knees more deeply during loading generate higher ground reaction forces and consequently higher racquet speeds, all else being equal.

However, there is a flexion threshold beyond which additional knee bend becomes counterproductive. Below approximately 110 degrees of flexion, the quadriceps are at a mechanical disadvantage for producing force, and the time required to extend from such a deep position delays the forward swing. The optimal range is 110 to 135 degrees for the rear knee and 120 to 140 degrees for the front knee during the loading phase.

## Knee Flexion and Ball Height Adaptation

Knee flexion is also the primary mechanism for adjusting to different ball heights. Low balls require deeper knee flexion to lower the body to the ball's level, maintaining the optimal contact geometry (contact point at or below hip height). High balls require less knee flexion, allowing the player to rise into the ball and hit with a more level or downward swing path.

Elite players adjust knee flexion rapidly during their approach to the ball, arriving at the optimal flexion angle for the specific ball height before beginning the unit turn. This "height-specific" knee flexion is trained through drills that present balls at varying heights with the instruction to adjust knee bend before initiating the stroke.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Knees remain straight throughout stroke | No elastic energy storage; no leg drive | Arm-dominated swing; flat shots | Practice deep knee flexion in ready position; hold bend through loading |
| Rear knee does not extend during forward swing | Missing primary power source | Weak, floating balls | Focus on pushing off rear foot; feel rear heel lift off ground |
| Front knee extends too early (before hip rotation) | Leg drive and trunk rotation not synchronized | Reduced power transfer | Delay front knee extension until hips begin rotating forward |
| Knee flexion too deep (below 110 degrees) | Quadriceps at mechanical disadvantage | Slow extension; delayed swing | Maintain 110 to 135 degrees; avoid squatting too low |
| Asymmetric knee flexion (one knee much deeper than the other) | Uneven ground force production | Directional errors; inconsistent contact | Practice symmetric flexion in both knees; use mirror feedback |

## Practical Application: "The Squat and Spring"

Practice the knee flexion-extension cycle by loading into a deep squat position (holding your racquet in the set position) and then exploding upward into a forward swing. Focus on the feeling of your legs driving into the ground as you extend, and the upward surge of energy that transfers through your hips to your racquet. This exaggerated drill ingrains the leg drive sensation that should occur in every forehand, scaled to the appropriate flexion depth for the specific situation.
