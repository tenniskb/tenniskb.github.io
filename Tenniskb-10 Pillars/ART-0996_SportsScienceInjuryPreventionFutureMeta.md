# ART-996: Tennis and Climate Change — Adapting to Extreme Heat

## Executive Summary

Climate change is transforming the environmental conditions under which tennis is played, with rising temperatures, increased humidity, and more frequent extreme heat events posing serious health risks to players. Tournament organizers, governing bodies, and individual athletes must adapt through modified scheduling, advanced cooling technologies, and revised heat policies. This article examines the science of heat stress in tennis, the specific challenges posed by climate change to the global tournament calendar, and the innovative cooling strategies being developed to protect player health while maintaining competitive integrity.

## The Science of Heat Stress in Tennis

Tennis is uniquely vulnerable to heat stress because it combines high-intensity intermittent exercise with prolonged exposure (matches can last 3-5 hours) in direct sunlight. During intense play, a tennis player's metabolic heat production reaches 1,200-1,800 watts — comparable to a household space heater. Dissipating this heat requires efficient sweating and evaporative cooling, both of which become less effective as ambient temperature and humidity rise.

The key metric for assessing heat stress risk is wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which combines:
- **Wet-bulb temperature** (humidity-dependent evaporative cooling potential)
- **Black globe temperature** (radiant heat from the sun)
- **Dry-bulb temperature** (ambient air temperature)

When WBGT exceeds 32°C (90°F), the risk of exertional heat illness becomes severe. At these levels, the body can no longer dissipate metabolic heat through sweating, and core temperature rises uncontrollably. Tennis matches at WBGT above 32°C carry genuine risk of heat stroke, organ damage, and death.

## Climate Change and the Tennis Calendar

The global warming trend is directly affecting tennis tournaments:

**Australian Open**: Melbourne summer temperatures have increased by 1.2°C since 1990. The 2014 Australian Open saw four consecutive days above 40°C (104°F), with players reporting the court surface was hot enough to melt their shoes. Subsequent years have seen multiple matches suspended due to heat, and the tournament now routinely activates its Extreme Heat Policy.

**US Open**: New York's late-summer climate has always challenged players, but the combination of urban heat island effect and rising baseline temperatures has pushed WBGT levels higher. The 2023 US Open experienced its hottest day on record during qualifying rounds, with court surface temperatures exceeding 50°C (122°F).

**French Open**: Paris in late spring/early summer is experiencing more frequent heat waves. The 2022 French Open saw temperatures above 35°C (95°F) during the second week, unusual for early June.

**Asian Swing**: Tournaments in Beijing, Tokyo, and Wuhan face some of the most extreme heat-humidity combinations globally. The Tokyo 2020 Olympics (held in 2021) saw multiple tennis players require medical attention for heat stress despite being held in late July/early August rather than the traditional Tokyo summer.

**Middle East Swing**: Dubai and Doha tournaments in February/March are increasingly affected by rising temperatures, with Doha's outdoor courts now occasionally exceeding 35°C during daytime play.

## The Escalating Risk

Climate projections suggest that by 2050, many tennis-hosting cities will experience 2-4 times more days above the critical 32°C WBGT threshold. For tennis specifically, this means:

- More match interruptions and scheduling disruptions
- Increased heat-related medical emergencies
- Reduced performance quality as players struggle with heat
- Potential health consequences from chronic heat exposure over careers
- Tournament viability questions for outdoor events in certain locations

The WTA and ATP have both recognized climate change as a significant threat to the sport's future, with the WTA establishing a Sustainability Department in 2021 specifically to address environmental challenges.

## Cooling Technology Advances

Innovation in cooling technology offers several strategies for mitigating heat stress:

### Pre-Cooling Strategies
- **Cooling vests** — phase-change material vests worn during warm-up reduce core temperature by 0.3-0.5°C before play begins
- **Cold water immersion** — 20-minute immersion in 10-15°C water before matches extends time to exhaustion by 15-20%
- **Ice slurries** — consuming 7.5 kg of ice slurry (frozen sports drink) before play reduces core temperature by 0.4°C and improves intermittent sprint performance by 7%

### During-Match Cooling
- **Misting fans on changeovers** — reduce skin temperature by 2-3°C through evaporative cooling
- **Cold towels** — applied to neck, wrists, and groin (areas of high blood flow) provide 5-10 minutes of cooling per application
- **Cooled hydration** — beverages at 4°C vs. 22°C improve fluid consumption by 30% and provide internal cooling
- **Ice vests during medical timeouts** — new WTA rules allow ice vest use during medical breaks

### Advanced Technologies
- **Personal cooling systems** — battery-powered micro-pump systems circulating cooled water through wearable garments (currently too bulky for competition but in development)
- **Radiative cooling fabrics** — new textile technologies that reflect solar radiation while emitting body heat as infrared radiation, reducing heat gain by 5-8°C compared to standard fabrics
- **Phase-change materials (PCM)** — materials that absorb heat as they transition from solid to liquid, maintaining constant temperature for 30-60 minutes

## Scheduling Modifications

The most effective adaptation strategy is scheduling modification:

- **Night sessions only** during extreme heat events — used effectively at the Australian Open since 2015
- **Split sessions** — morning (10 AM-2 PM) and evening (5-10 PM) sessions avoiding peak heat
- **Seasonal shifts** — moving tournaments to cooler months where possible
- **Indoor/covered court preference** — increasing roofed court availability at tournaments in hot climates
- **Shorter match formats** — best-of-three sets for all rounds (already implemented at some events) or no-ad scoring in extreme heat conditions
- **Extended changeovers** — 3-minute changeovers instead of 90 seconds in extreme heat
- **Mandatory cooling breaks** — 10-minute breaks between second and third sets (already used at the US Open)

## Revised Heat Policies

Modern heat policies should incorporate:

1. **WBGT monitoring** at court level (not just weather station data)
2. **Tiered response system** — green (WBGT <28°C), yellow (28-30°C), orange (30-32°C), red (>32°C)
3. **Mandatory suspension** above WBGT 32°C with no exceptions
4. **Player right to suspend** — any player can request heat suspension without penalty when WBGT exceeds 30°C
5. **Medical monitoring** — core temperature spot-checks for players in extended matches above 28°C WBGT
6. **Acclimatization requirements** — minimum 7-day heat acclimatization period before competition in extreme heat

## Practical Application: "The Heat-Ready Player"

Preparing for tennis in a warming climate requires a comprehensive heat-readiness protocol: (1) **Acclimatization** — begin heat exposure training 10-14 days before competing in hot conditions, with 60-90 minutes of moderate exercise in heat (30-35°C) daily to trigger physiological adaptations (earlier sweating onset, increased sweat volume, reduced electrolyte concentration in sweat, expanded plasma volume); (2) **Pre-cooling routine** — on match day, consume 7.5 kg of ice slurry 30 minutes before play, wear a cooling vest during warm-up, and apply cold towels to the neck and wrists; (3) **During-match cooling** — use every changeover for active cooling (cold towels, misting fan, cold hydration), request extended changeovers in extreme heat, and use medical timeouts for ice vest application if available; (4) **Recovery** — after matches, use cold water immersion (10-15°C for 10-15 minutes) to accelerate core temperature normalization. Players who systematically implement these strategies can maintain performance in conditions that would otherwise cause 10-15% performance declines. Climate change is not a distant threat — it is already reshaping tennis, and players who adapt their heat management will gain a competitive edge while protecting their health.
