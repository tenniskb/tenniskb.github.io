# ART-942: Hydration for Tennis — Sweat Rate and Fluid Replacement

## Executive Summary

Tennis is played in some of the hottest and most humid conditions in sport — the Australian Open regularly sees temperatures above 35°C (95°F), and the US Open's Arthur Ashe Stadium can feel like an oven. Under these conditions, a player can lose 1.5-3 liters of sweat per hour. Even mild dehydration (a loss of just 2% of body weight) impairs endurance, cognitive function, and decision-making — the exact qualities that decide close tennis matches. Understanding your personal sweat rate, replacing fluids and electrolytes strategically, and recognizing the early signs of dehydration are essential skills for any tennis player who wants to perform at their best in the heat.

## Sweat Rate: The Foundation of Hydration Strategy

Sweat rate is the amount of fluid lost through sweating per hour of exercise. It varies enormously between individuals (from 0.5 to 3+ liters per hour) and within the same individual depending on temperature, humidity, intensity, clothing, and acclimatization status.

### How to Measure Your Sweat Rate

The most accurate and practical method is the "weigh-in, weigh-out" test:

1. **Before exercise**: Empty your bladder, then weigh yourself nude or in minimal clothing (record your weight in kilograms).
2. **Exercise**: Train or play for exactly 1 hour. During this hour, drink a measured amount of fluid (record the volume in milliliters).
3. **After exercise**: Towel off sweat, empty your bladder, and weigh yourself again (record your weight in kilograms).
4. **Calculate**: Sweat rate (liters per hour) = (pre-exercise weight − post-exercise weight) + fluid consumed − urine produced.

Example: Pre-weight = 70.0 kg, post-weight = 68.5 kg, fluid consumed = 500 mL, urine = 0 mL.
Sweat rate = (70.0 − 68.5) + 0.5 − 0 = 2.0 liters per hour.

Perform this test in conditions similar to your competition environment (temperature, humidity, intensity). Repeat it several times, as sweat rate varies day to day. You may also want to measure it at different times of day and in different seasons.

### Interpreting Your Sweat Rate

- **Low sweater**: Less than 1.0 L/hour. You may not need aggressive hydration strategies, but you still need to replace fluids during long matches.
- **Moderate sweater**: 1.0-2.0 L/hour. Most competitive tennis players fall in this range. A structured hydration plan is important.
- **Heavy sweater**: More than 2.0 L/hour. You are at high risk of dehydration and need a proactive, aggressive hydration strategy. You may also lose more sodium and need to pay attention to electrolyte replacement.

## Fluid Replacement Strategies

The goal of hydration during exercise is to prevent body weight loss exceeding 2% (the threshold at which performance begins to decline). For a 70 kg player, this means losing no more than 1.4 kg during a match.

### Pre-Hydration

Arrive at the match well-hydrated. The strategy:

- **2-3 hours before**: Drink 500-600 mL of water or a sports drink.
- **30-60 minutes before**: Drink another 200-300 mL.
- **Check urine color**: Pale yellow (like lemonade) indicates good hydration. Dark yellow (like apple juice) indicates dehydration.

### During-Match Hydration

During a match, you have limited opportunities to drink: the 25-second rest between points (not enough time to drink), the 90-second changeover (the primary drinking opportunity), and the set break (if applicable).

At each changeover:

- **Drink 150-250 mL** of a sports drink (6-8% carbohydrate, 300-600 mg sodium per liter).
- **Do not wait until you feel thirsty**. Thirst is a delayed indicator of dehydration; by the time you feel thirsty, you are already dehydrated.
- **Use a squeeze bottle** for quick drinking during the changeover. Do not rely on a water fountain or a bottle with a screw cap that is difficult to open quickly.

### Post-Match Rehydration

After a match, replace 150% of the fluid lost (to account for ongoing urine production). For example, if you lost 1.5 kg during the match, drink 2.25 liters of fluid over the next 2-4 hours.

Include sodium in your post-match fluids (sports drink, salty foods, or added salt) to promote fluid retention and restore electrolyte balance.

## Electrolyte Replacement

Sweat contains water and electrolytes — primarily sodium (400-1,200 mg per liter of sweat, depending on the individual) and smaller amounts of potassium, magnesium, and calcium. Replacing sodium is important for:

- **Maintaining fluid balance**: Sodium helps the body retain fluid and prevents over-dilution of the blood.
- **Preventing muscle cramps**: While the exact cause of exercise-associated muscle cramps is debated, sodium depletion and neuromuscular fatigue are believed to play a role.
- **Sustaining performance**: Sodium is the primary electrolyte lost in sweat, and its replacement is more important than any other electrolyte for tennis players.

### Sodium Replacement Strategies

- **Sports drinks**: Choose a drink with 300-600 mg of sodium per liter. Many commercial sports drinks contain only 100-200 mg per liter, which may be insufficient for heavy sweaters.
- **Salt tablets or electrolyte capsules**: For players with very high sweat rates, additional sodium may be needed during long matches. Consult a sports dietitian before using these.
- **Salty foods at changeovers**: A few pretzels or a small bag of salted nuts can provide sodium and carbohydrate during a long match.
- **Post-match**: Include salty foods (soup, pretzels, cheese) in your recovery meal.

## Signs of Dehydration

Recognizing dehydration early allows you to adjust your strategy before performance declines. Signs include:

- **Early (1-2% body weight loss)**: Thirst, dry mouth, decreased urine output, dark urine, mild fatigue, and reduced endurance.
- **Moderate (2-4% body weight loss)**: Increased heart rate, decreased sweating (a dangerous sign), headache, dizziness, muscle cramps, nausea, and impaired decision-making.
- **Severe (more than 4% body weight loss)**: Confusion, weakness, collapse, and heat illness (heat exhaustion or heat stroke). This is a medical emergency.

## Heat Acclimatization

The body adapts to heat exposure over 7-14 days of regular exercise in hot conditions. These adaptations include:

- **Increased sweat rate**: The body starts sweating earlier and at a higher rate, improving evaporative cooling.
- **Expanded blood volume**: The body retains more fluid, improving cardiovascular stability.
- **Reduced sodium concentration in sweat**: The body conserves sodium, reducing electrolyte losses.
- **Lower heart rate at a given intensity**: Cardiovascular strain is reduced.

For a tournament in hot conditions, arrive 7-14 days early and train in the heat (if possible) to acclimatize. If you cannot arrive early, train in a warm room or wear extra clothing during training to simulate heat stress.

## Hydration Plan for Match Day

A sample hydration plan for a match expected to last 2-3 hours:

| Time | Action |
| :--- | :--- |
| 3 hours before | Drink 500-600 mL of water or sports drink |
| 1 hour before | Drink 200-300 mL of water or sports drink |
| 15 minutes before | Drink 150-200 mL of sports drink |
| Every changeover | Drink 150-250 mL of sports drink |
| Set break | Drink 200-300 mL; eat a few pretzels or a banana |
| Post-match (within 30 min) | Drink 500-750 mL of recovery drink with sodium |
| Post-match (next 2-4 hours) | Drink 1.5 L for every 1.0 kg of body weight lost |

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player's performance drops sharply in hot conditions | Inadequate pre-hydration or poor sweat rate knowledge | Dehydration, heat illness risk | Measure sweat rate; implement pre-hydration protocol |
| Player experiences muscle cramps during long matches | Sodium depletion or neuromuscular fatigue | Cramping, reduced performance | Add sodium to sports drink; include salty foods at changeovers |
| Player's heart rate is elevated in the heat | Dehydration reducing blood volume | Cardiovascular strain, premature fatigue | Increase fluid intake; acclimatize 7-14 days before event |
| Player loses more than 2% body weight during matches | Insufficient during-match fluid intake | Impaired performance, cognitive decline | Increase fluid volume at changeovers; use a squeeze bottle |
| Player feels bloated during matches | Over-drinking plain water without sodium | Hyponatremia risk (rare but serious) | Switch to sports drink with sodium; avoid excessive plain water |
| Player recovers slowly between tournament matches | Inadequate post-match rehydration | Cumulative dehydration across tournament | Implement post-match rehydration protocol (150% of fluid lost) |

## Practical Application: "The Weigh-In, Weigh-Out Habit"

Make sweat rate testing a regular part of your training routine. Weigh yourself before and after every match and every hard training session. Track the data in a notebook or a spreadsheet. Over time, you will develop a personal database of your sweat rates in different conditions — and you will know exactly how much to drink to stay within the 2% threshold.

The math is simple: if you sweat 2.0 liters per hour and your match lasts 3 hours, you lose 6.0 liters of fluid. You cannot replace all of it during the match (your stomach can only absorb about 1.0-1.5 liters per hour), so you must start well-hydrated and drink aggressively at every changeover. Aim to replace 60-80% of your sweat losses during the match, and the remainder in the post-match recovery period.

Players who know their sweat rate and follow a hydration plan consistently outlast their opponents in the heat. It is not glamorous, but it is decisive. The player who can think clearly and move fluidly in the third set of a 35°C match is the one who prepared — and hydration is preparation.
