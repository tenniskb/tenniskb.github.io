# ART-0193: Recovery Positioning Metrics — Calculating Ideal Re-Entry Coordinates

## Executive Summary

"Get back to the middle" is the least useful instruction in tennis, because the middle is almost never the right place to be. The correct recovery coordinate — a specific lateral position and depth — can be calculated from two inputs: where your shot placed the opponent, and which reply options remain open to them. This article formalizes that calculation as the **recovery cone**, converts it into bisector geometry and depth rules, and distills it into three rules a player can apply mid-point without doing any math.

## The Recovery Cone

After your shot crosses the net, the opponent's possible replies fan out from wherever they will make contact. Draw two lines from that contact point to the extremes of your court they can realistically reach — typically the singles sidelines, or a meter inside them under time pressure. These lines enclose the **recovery cone**, usually 50-90° wide for a neutral baseline exchange and much wider when the opponent has time to attack.

You cannot cover the cone's extremes simultaneously — that is the geometric fact that makes tennis a positional game. The optimal single position lies on or near the **bisector** of the cone: the line splitting the angle into equal halves, minimizing your worst-case travel to either edge. Two refinements:

- **Probability weighting.** Replies are not equally likely. From a stretched, wide contact, on the order of 60-70% of replies come back crosscourt or centrally. Shift one step from the pure bisector toward the high-probability sector — this weighted bisector is where professionals actually stand.
- **Threat weighting.** One sector may contain the *losing* shot — the clean winner — while another contains only neutral balls. Bias a half-step away from sectors that cannot hurt you.

## Lateral Coordinates by Shot Placement

The cone's apex moves with the opponent's contact position, so your lateral coordinate is a direct function of where you sent the ball:

- **Deep crosscourt, pulling them wide.** Their cone compresses toward your same side: the down-the-line reply is geometrically harder, and the highest-probability ball comes back crosscourt toward you. The weighted bisector sits roughly 1-2 m from the center mark on the side you hit toward — often just 2-3 shuffle steps of recovery.
- **Deep down the line.** The most expensive recovery in tennis. Their crosscourt reply now points into the space you just vacated, and the bisector shifts 2-3 m *across* the center mark to the opposite side. You have bought a changing shot at the price of roughly double the recovery distance — which is why the down-the-line ball must be a genuinely better shot, not a stylistic preference.
- **Deep and central.** Symmetric cone; recover to the center mark, weighted one step toward the opponent's stronger wing.
- **Short ball (landing inside the service line).** The cone explodes: sharp angles and time-taking become available, so your lateral position widens and — more importantly — your depth retreats.

The rule of thumb for the lateral axis: **crosscourt buys half the recovery; down the line costs double.** For identical shot quality, the down-the-line hitter covers roughly 2-3 m more ground before their next contact.

## Depth Coordinates: The Three-Zone Ladder

Depth is set by the *time* the opponent will have, which is set by your shot's quality:

- **Defense zone — 2-3 m behind the baseline.** The home for short, floating, or weakly struck balls. Expect pace, angle, or an approach; the extra meters convert their offense into rally balls. On clay, add roughly half a meter for the higher bounce.
- **Neutral zone — on the baseline to 1 m behind.** After a deep, heavy shot when the opponent must hit up. Standard residence for baseline exchanges.
- **Attack zone — inside the baseline.** Only when your shot lands deep with penetration *and* the opponent is stretched: their reply will likely be slow and central, so stepping in takes its time away. Moving up without the shot quality to justify it is how players get passed.

The compact version: **the depth of your shot dictates the depth of your recovery.** Most club players violate this constantly — they retreat after short balls (correct) but also retreat after their best deep shots, wasting free court.

## The Time Budget Check

Position is only "correct" if it is reachable. The budget — ball flight time to the opponent plus their stroke cycle, minus roughly 300-400 ms for your own split and preparation — leaves the travel window. At 3-4 m/s of lateral speed, a 1.2 s window covers about 3.5-4.5 m of recovery. If the geometry does not fit the budget — a down-the-line gamble from a very wide position, say — the shot choice itself was the error (see ART-0198 for when leaving early is justified).

## Three Rules to Apply on the Fly

1. **Bisect, then bias.** Find the middle of their options, then step one toward their most likely reply.
2. **Your shot's depth is your recovery depth.** Deep shot, hold the line; short shot, retreat 2-3 m.
3. **Crosscourt to recover short; line to recover long.** Choose direction knowing what it costs later.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Always recovers to the center mark | Generic "middle" habit; cone geometry ignored | Wide open angles on every pull-wide ball | Mark the weighted bisector with a cone each rally; score arrivals against it |
| Recovers symmetrically after being pulled wide | Same recovery distance for every shot direction | Down-the-line shots expose the vacated side | Chart recovery coordinates after crosscourt vs. line shots on video |
| Holds the baseline after short balls | Depth not linked to shot quality | Burned by angles and approaches | Three-zone ladder: coach calls the zone after each strike; player must be in it before next contact |
| Stands 2-3 m behind baseline after deep, heavy shots | Unnecessary retreat | 2-3 m of court and time surrendered every rally | "Deep ball, shallow recovery" scoring; contact inside the baseline on deep shots |
| Overshoots the recovery mark by a meter or more | Path not calibrated to a coordinate | Wasted steps and a late re-split (see ART-0186) | Mark the coordinate; score arrivals within ±50 cm, then re-split on arrival |
| Never biases toward opponent tendencies | Pure geometry, no probability weighting | Consistently one step wrong vs. pattern players | Chart 20 opponent replies; shift the bisector one step toward any 65%+ sector |
| Same recovery depth on clay and hard court | Surface ignored in the depth calculation | Bounced high on clay; jammed on hard | Add ~0.5 m depth on clay for the higher bounce; hold closer to the line on hard |

## Practical Application: "The Tape Triangle"

Before the next practice session, place three strips of court tape (or flat cones) at that player's three most common recovery coordinates: the crosscourt bisector, the down-the-line bisector 2-3 m across center, and the defense-zone mark 2.5 m deep. Play live points in which the coach freezes the rally at random moments with the call "Position!" — the player's location is scored against the correct mark: 2 points for the correct zone, 1 for adjacent, 0 for wrong. Ten freezes per set. Within two weeks most players move from scoring 3-4 out of 10 to 7 or better — and the coach stops saying "middle" forever.
