# ART-433: One-Handed Slice Technique

## Executive Summary
The one-handed backhand slice is one of the most elegant and versatile shots in tennis. It uses a Continental grip, a high-to-low swing path, and an open racket face to produce backspin that makes the ball float and skid low. Mastering the one-handed slice expands a player's tactical options and provides a reliable tool for difficult situations.

## Grip for the One-Handed Slice
The Continental grip is the most common grip for the one-handed backhand slice because it naturally presents an open racket face. The base knuckle of the index finger sits on bevel 2, positioning the racket face tilted upward. This grip allows the player to hit the slice without changing grips, which is particularly useful at the net.

Players who use an Eastern or Semi-Western grip for drives must change to Continental for slice, which requires practice. Many players who use the one-handed backhand keep the Continental grip for both drive and slice, accepting less topspin on drives in exchange for faster transitions.

## The Slice Swing Path
The backhand slice uses a high-to-low swing path, fundamentally different from the low-to-high path of a topspin drive. The racket starts above the contact point and descends through the ball, brushing the back and underneath. This descending path is what creates backspin.

The angle of descent controls slice character. A steeper descent (more vertical) produces more backspin but less forward momentum. A shallower descent (more horizontal) produces less spin but more pace and penetration. Players adjust the descent angle based on tactical needs.

## Contact Point for the One-Handed Slice
Contact for the one-handed slice occurs in front of the body, typically at chest to shoulder height. The racket face is tilted open approximately 30-45 degrees. The ball contacts the strings on the upper portion of the string bed because the racket is descending through contact. The follow-through continues downward and slightly forward, finishing around waist height for a standard slice or lower for a dropping slice.

The one-handed slice contact point is typically slightly further forward than the two-handed slice, as the single hand provides more reach. Players should adjust the contact point based on the incoming ball height and the desired slice trajectory.

## Racket Face Angle at Contact
The racket face angle at contact determines the slice's trajectory and spin. A wider open face (more tilted upward) produces more height and spin, creating a high, looping slice that drops sharply. A slightly closed face produces a flatter, skidding slice that stays low and penetrates the court.

Players should practice varying the racket face angle to produce different slice trajectories. This versatility allows the player to adjust the slice based on the tactical situation, producing a high defensive slice or a low offensive slice as needed.

## Generating Backspin
Backspin is generated by the racket face brushing down and under the ball. The combination of the open racket face and the descending swing path creates the spin. The amount of backspin depends on racket head speed through contact and the angle of the swing path.

Players who want to increase backspin should focus on accelerating the racket through the contact zone and steepening the swing path. Drills that emphasize the descending brush help develop the feel for effective backspin production.

## The Role of the Non-Dominant Arm in the Slice
The non-dominant arm plays a role in the one-handed slice, even though it does not touch the racket. During the take-back, the non-dominant arm extends backward, balancing the body and facilitating rotation. During the forward swing, the non-dominant arm moves across the body, helping to maintain balance and control.

Players who ignore the non-dominant arm often struggle with balance during the slice. Developing awareness of the non-dominant arm's position is essential for an effective one-handed slice.

## Common One-Handed Slice Errors
One common error is failing to maintain the open racket face, resulting in a flat shot with little backspin. Another is swinging too slowly, resulting in a floaty ball that does not skid. Some players also struggle with the non-dominant arm, failing to use it for balance.

Players who struggle with the one-handed slice often benefit from drills that emphasize the open racket face and the descending path. Practicing the swing with a focus on these elements helps develop the technique needed for an effective slice.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix
| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Slice sails too long | Racket face too closed or path too flat | Ball goes out or is attackable | Open face more; steepen descent angle |
| Slice pops up too high | Racket face too open or path too steep | Easy overhead for opponent | Reduce open face; shallow the descent |
| No backspin, ball floats | Swing path level or ascending | Floaty, attackable ball | Emphasize high-to-low path; brush under ball |
| Slice lacks penetration | Swing speed too slow or contact too far back | Ball dies at opponent's feet | Accelerate through contact; move contact forward |
| Wrist pain on slice | Wrist too loose or bent at contact | Inconsistent contact, potential injury | Stabilize wrist; check grip pressure |

## Practical Application: "The One-Handed Slice Practice"
Stand on the baseline and hit ten one-handed slice backhands, focusing on producing a low, skidding bounce. After each shot, note whether the ball stays below net height after the bounce. If it bounces high, the racket face is too open or the path is too steep. Adjust until at least seven of ten shots skid low, building the feel for an offensive one-handed slice.
