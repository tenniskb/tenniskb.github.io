# ART-909: Ankle Sprains in Tennis — Prevention, Treatment, and Chronic Instability

## Executive Summary

Ankle sprains are the most common acute injury in tennis, accounting for 15-25% of all tennis-related injuries. The lateral ankle ligaments are most vulnerable during the rapid directional changes, lunging, and recovery movements that define modern tennis. While often dismissed as minor, inadequate rehabilitation leads to chronic ankle instability in 30-40% of cases.

## Ankle Anatomy for Tennis

### Lateral Ligament Complex
- Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL): Weakest ligament, resists anterior displacement in plantarflexion; injured in 70-85% of ankle sprains
- Calcaneofibular ligament (CFL): Resists inversion in neutral/dorsiflexion; injured in moderate-severe sprains
- Posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL): Strongest lateral ligament; rarely injured except in severe sprains

### Dynamic Stabilizers
- Peroneus longus and brevis: Evertors that resist inversion; critical for dynamic stability
- Tibialis anterior: Dorsiflexor, provides medial support
- Intrinsic foot muscles: Provide arch support and proprioception

## Mechanism of Injury

### Inversion Sprain (Most Common)
The ankle rolls inward (inversion) while in plantarflexion — the ATFL is maximally stressed in this position. In tennis, this occurs during:
- Lateral movement with foot catching on the court surface
- Landing on an opponent's foot after a net approach
- Rapid directional change with the foot planted
- Sliding on clay with unpredictable surface contact

## Grading System

### Grade I (Mild)
- Pathology: Microscopic tearing of ligament fibers
- Symptoms: Mild pain, minimal swelling, no instability
- Weight-bearing: Full, with mild discomfort
- Recovery: 1-3 weeks

### Grade II (Moderate)
- Pathology: Partial ligament tear
- Symptoms: Moderate pain, swelling, ecchymosis
- Weight-bearing: Painful but possible with support
- Recovery: 3-6 weeks

### Grade III (Severe)
- Pathology: Complete ligament tear
- Symptoms: Significant swelling, ecchymosis, mechanical instability
- Weight-bearing: Unable without severe pain
- Recovery: 6-12 weeks; may require surgical intervention

## Acute Management

### POLICE Principle (Updated from RICE)
- Protection: Brace, taping, or ankle support to prevent re-injury
- Optimal Loading: Begin gentle, pain-free movement immediately
- Ice: 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours for pain and swelling reduction
- Compression: Elastic bandage or compression sleeve
- Elevation: Above heart level when possible

## Rehabilitation Protocol

### Phase 1: Acute (Days 1-7)
- Goal: Control pain and swelling, maintain mobility
- Ankle pumps: 3 sets of 20 repetitions, hourly
- Alphabet tracing: Trace A-Z with foot, 3 sets
- Isometric strengthening: Press foot into towel in all 4 directions, 10 sets of 10-second holds

### Phase 2: Subacute (Weeks 2-4)
- Goal: Restore full ROM, begin strengthening, normalize gait
- Resistance band eversion/inversion/dorsiflexion: 3 sets of 15 each direction
- Calf raises: Double-leg progressing to single-leg, 3 sets of 15

### Phase 3: Strengthening (Weeks 4-6)
- Goal: Restore strength, proprioception, dynamic stability
- Single-leg balance: Progress from stable to unstable surface
- Star excursion balance test: Reach in multiple directions on one leg
- Peroneal strengthening: Band-resisted eversion with emphasis on eccentric control

### Phase 4: Return to Sport (Weeks 6-12)
- Goal: Sport-specific movements, confidence restoration
- Lateral shuffles, figure-8 running, T-drill
- Graduated return to tennis: Mini-tennis to half-court to full-court to match play
- Bracing/taping during return

## Return-to-Play Criteria
- Full pain-free range of motion
- Ankle strength >90% of uninjured side
- Single-leg balance for 30 seconds with eyes closed
- Successful completion of sport-specific agility tests
- Psychological confidence in the ankle

## Chronic Ankle Instability

### Contributing Factors
- Mechanical instability: Laxity of ligaments due to incomplete healing
- Functional instability: Impaired proprioception and neuromuscular control
- Strength deficits: Peroneal weakness, particularly eccentric control

### Treatment
- Rehabilitation: Intensive proprioceptive training (minimum 12 weeks)
- Bracing: Semi-rigid ankle braces reduce re-sprain risk by 50%
- Taping: Athletic tape provides proprioceptive feedback; loses effectiveness after 20 minutes
- Surgery: Consider after failed 6-month rehabilitation (Brostrom-Gould repair)

## Prevention Strategies

### Proprioceptive Training
A 10-minute daily balance program reduces ankle sprain incidence by 35-50%:
- Single-leg stance on stable surface: 3 sets of 30 seconds
- Single-leg stance on foam pad: 3 sets of 30 seconds
- Single-leg stance with eyes closed: 3 sets of 20 seconds
- Star excursion balance: 3 reaches in each of 8 directions

### Appropriate Footwear
- Court-specific shoes with lateral support
- Proper fit (thumb-width space between longest toe and shoe end)
- Replace after 40-60 hours of play or 6 months

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Repeated lateral sprains | ATFL laxity with impaired peroneal response | Chronic instability, movement apprehension | Peroneal eccentric strengthening; proprioceptive training |
| Giving-way sensation during direction change | Functional instability (proprioceptive deficit) | Confidence loss, compensatory movement | Wobble board training; star excursion balance progression |
| Stiffness after sprain immobilization | Capsular tightness and arthrofibrosis | Reduced dorsiflexion, gait abnormality | Joint mobilization; controlled ankle dorsiflexion stretches |
| Fear of landing on outside foot | Psychological barrier | Altered biomechanics, secondary injury risk | Gradual exposure therapy; sport psychology consultation |

## Practical Application: "Balance Before You Rally"

Before every practice session, perform 3 minutes of single-leg balance work: stand on each leg for 60 seconds, first with eyes open, then closed. If you cannot maintain balance for 20 seconds with eyes closed on either leg, your proprioceptive system needs attention. Add this to your daily routine — 3 minutes of balance training prevents more ankle injuries than any brace, and it requires zero equipment.
