# ART-0621: The Australian Formation — Disguise and Pressure in Doubles

## Executive Summary

The Australian formation is a doubles serving alignment where the net player positions on the same side of the court as the server (rather than the traditional opposite side). This formation disguises the serve direction, creates poaching opportunities, and disrupts the returner's rhythm. It is a tactical surprise weapon used to neutralize strong cross-court returns and put immediate pressure on the serving team's opponents.

## Standard Doubles Formation vs. Australian Formation

In the standard doubles formation, the server stands on one side of the center mark while the net player stands on the opposite side, near the net. This provides balanced court coverage: the net player covers angles on their side while the server covers the middle and their own side after the serve.

In the Australian formation, both the server and net player stand on the same side of the court. The server serves from the deuce court (or ad court) while the net player stands at the net on the same side. The net player's side of the court is left open, but the returner must guess whether the serve will go to their forehand or backhand — the same side as the net player or the opposite side.

## Tactical Advantages

The Australian formation provides several advantages: (1) the returner cannot use the net player's position as a cue for serve direction, (2) the net player can poach more aggressively because they are already on the side where most cross-court returns go, (3) the formation is psychologically disruptive — returners are uncomfortable with the unusual alignment, and (4) it protects the server's weaker side (typically the backhand) by keeping the net player on the stronger side.

## When to Use the Australian Formation

Use the Australian formation: (1) against opponents who consistently hit cross-court returns, (2) when the server has a strong serve to one side and wants to exploit it, (3) to disrupt the rhythm of a returner who is finding a pattern, (4) when the net player is a strong poacher, and (5) in pressure situations (break points, tiebreaks) where a tactical surprise can shift momentum. Do not use it: (1) against strong down-the-line returners, (2) when the net player is inexperienced, or (3) as a permanent strategy (it becomes predictable).

## The Server's Role in the Australian Formation

The server must hit their serve to the side away from the net player (the "open" side) to maximize the net player's poaching opportunity. For example, if the net player is on the deuce court side, the server should hit the serve to the returner's backhand on the ad court. This forces the returner to hit cross-court (toward the net player) or down-the-line (into the open court, but with less angle). The server must also be prepared to cover the open court if the returner hits down-the-line.

## The Net Player's Role in the Australian Formation

The net player positions approximately 6-8 feet from the net, on the same side as the server. Their job is to poach aggressively on any cross-court return, intercepting the ball and volleying it to the open court. The net player must also be prepared to retreat for a lob if the returner recognizes the formation and lobs over their head. Communication between server and net player is essential — the net player should signal their poaching intentions before the serve.

## Covering the Open Side

The primary weakness of the Australian formation is the open court on the net player's side. If the returner hits down-the-line, the server must cover this area. The server should position themselves slightly toward the center of the court after serving, ready to move to the open side. Some teams use a "shift" — the server moves toward the open side after the serve while the net player stays on their original side, providing better coverage.

## Common Australian Formation Errors

The server hits to the same side as the net player (giving the returner an easy cross-court return away from the net player). The net player poaches too early (before the return direction is clear). The server fails to cover the open side after serving. The net player does not retreat for lobs. Using the formation too often (becomes predictable and loses its surprise value).

## Tactical Variations

The "fake Australian" has the net player start in the Australian position but shift to the standard position just before the serve, confusing the returner. The "double Australian" has both players on the same side after an approach shot (not just on serves). The "Australian poach" combines the formation with an aggressive poach on every return.

## Australian Formation Drills

The "Australian serve" drill practices serving with the Australian formation and covering the open side. The "Australian poach" drill practices the net player's poaching from the Australian position. The "return against Australian" drill gives returners practice facing the formation. The "Australian competition" game uses the Australian formation for entire service games.

## Summary

The Australian formation is a tactical surprise that disrupts return patterns and creates poaching opportunities. Its success depends on the server's ability to hit to the open side, the net player's aggressive poaching, and the team's coverage of the open court. Use it selectively for maximum impact.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Returner consistently hits down-the-line | Server doesn't cover open side | Easy passing shot | Server shifts toward open side after serve |
| Net player misses poach opportunities | Slow reaction, late movement | Missed interception | Drill poach timing from Australian position |
| Returner reads formation easily | Telegraphing with body language | Returner adjusts, hits away | Disguise formation with varied timing |
| Server hits to wrong side | Serving to net player's side | Easy return away from net player | Drill serving to the open side consistently |
| Lob over net player's head | Net player too close, no retreat | Easy lob winner | Net player drops back, reads lob early |

## Practical Application: "Same Side, Same Pressure"

The Australian formation is about deception. Both players on the same side forces the returner to guess. Trust the formation, serve to the open side, and poach aggressively. The surprise element is your greatest weapon.
