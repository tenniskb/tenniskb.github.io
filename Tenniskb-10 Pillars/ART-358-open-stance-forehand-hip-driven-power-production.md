# ART-358: The Open Stance Forehand — Hip-Driven Power Production

## Executive Summary

The open stance forehand, where the feet are approximately parallel to the net at contact, is the dominant stance in modern baseline tennis. It relies on hip-driven rotational power rather than linear weight transfer, with the hips rotating aggressively against a relatively stable lower body to generate racquet head speed. The open stance offers faster recovery and better court coverage than the closed stance, making it the preferred choice for wide forehands and situations where quick recovery is essential. Understanding hip-driven power production is essential for maximizing the open stance forehand's effectiveness.

## Hip-Driven vs. Linear Power

The open stance forehand generates power primarily through hip rotation, while the closed stance forehand generates power through a combination of hip rotation and linear weight transfer. In the open stance, the feet are parallel to the net, preventing the front foot from blocking forward momentum. This means the forward weight transfer that powers the closed stance forehand is largely absent.

To compensate, the open stance forehand relies more heavily on rotational power: the hips rotate aggressively against the relatively stable lower body, creating torque that is transferred through the trunk to the racquet. Research shows that open stance forehands generate 15 to 25 percent higher hip rotation velocities than closed stance forehands, partially compensating for the absence of linear weight transfer.

## The Mechanics of Hip-Driven Power

Hip-driven power in the open stance forehand is generated by:

1. Hip rotation: The pelvis rotates toward the target at angular velocities of 400 to 700 deg/s, driven by the hip rotators (gluteus medius, piriformis, and deep external rotators).

2. Trunk rotation: The trunk rotates beyond the hip rotation, creating the separation angle that stores elastic energy. Peak trunk rotation occurs 50 to 100 milliseconds after peak hip rotation.

3. Ground reaction force: The rear foot pushes into the ground, creating a ground reaction force that is transmitted through the legs to the hips, driving the rotation.

The combination of hip rotation, trunk rotation, and ground force creates the rotational power that drives the open stance forehand.

## Advantages of the Open Stance

The open stance forehand offers three primary advantages:

1. Faster recovery: Because the feet are parallel to the net, the player can push off and begin recovery immediately after contact, without needing to "uncross" the feet as in the closed stance.

2. Better court coverage: The open stance allows the player to cover wide balls more effectively, as the lateral movement required to reach the ball is more natural with the feet parallel to the net.

3. Consistent contact point: The open stance allows a more consistent contact point because the hips can rotate to adjust to the ball's position without changing the foot placement.

## Disadvantages of the Open Stance

The open stance forehand also has disadvantages:

1. Lower maximum power: The absence of linear weight transfer reduces the maximum power potential compared to the closed stance.

2. Limited directional control: The open stance makes it harder to hit down-the-line shots because the hips are already facing the net, reducing the ability to redirect the ball laterally.

3. Greater hip demand: The hip rotators must work harder to generate the same power that the closed stance generates through a combination of hip rotation and linear transfer.

## Training Hip-Driven Power

Training hip-driven power for the open stance forehand involves:

1. Hip rotation exercises: Medicine ball rotational throws, cable rotations, and resisted hip rotation drills.

2. Core stability exercises: Side planks, rotational planks, and anti-rotation exercises to develop the core stability needed for efficient energy transfer.

3. Open stance-specific drills: Practice hitting forehands exclusively in the open stance to develop the hip-driven power mechanics.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hips do not rotate aggressively enough | Insufficient rotational power | Weak shots | Strengthen hip rotators; practice rotational exercises |
| Trunk rotation does not exceed hip rotation | No separation angle; no elastic energy | Reduced power | Increase hip-shoulder separation; delay trunk rotation |
| No ground force from rear foot | Missing power platform | Weak shots | Drive rear foot into ground; feel force transfer |
| Recovery is slow after open stance | Poor footwork; inadequate push-off | Vulnerable to next shot | Practice push-off from open stance; develop recovery speed |
| Down-the-line shots are difficult | Limited directional control | Predictable cross-court shots | Practice down-the-line from open stance; develop lateral redirection |

## Practical Application: "The Hip Snap"

Practice the hip-driven power by hitting forehands in the open stance with a conscious focus on hip rotation. Feel your hips rotating aggressively toward the target, driven by your rear foot pushing into the ground. This "hip snap" sensation is the essence of the open stance forehand's power production.
