# ART-0106: The Split-Step Pulse — Frequency, Width, and Weight Distribution Metrics

## Executive Summary

The split step is a measurable event with four key metrics: hop height (5-10 cm), stance width (roughly 1.5-2× shoulder width), forefoot loading (55-70%), and frequency (one pulse per opponent stroke). Each metric can be measured with a phone camera at 120 fps and a few reference marks, giving coaches a low-cost objective profile of a player's split step. This article defines each metric, explains why the target ranges matter, and lays out a phone-camera protocol for measuring them.

## The Four Metrics

The split step's quality can be reduced to four numbers:

- **Height.** The vertical displacement of the center of mass — or, more practically, the feet — during the hop. The target range is 5-10 cm.
- **Width.** The distance between the feet at landing, measured as a multiple of shoulder width. The target range is 1.5-2× shoulder width.
- **Weight distribution.** The percentage of body weight on the forefoot versus the heel at landing. The target range is 55-70% forefoot.
- **Frequency.** The number of split-step pulses per opponent stroke. The target is one pulse per stroke — a 1:1 ratio.

Each metric captures a different dimension of the split step: height is the unweighting, width is the base, weight distribution is the spring loading, and frequency is the rhythm. Together, they form a profile that can be tracked over time.

## Height: The 5-10 cm Band

The height target exists because of the physics of unweighting. Below 5 cm, the unweighting is too small to pre-activate the leg spring; above 10 cm, the flight time is wasted (see ART-0102). The 5-10 cm band is the sweet spot: enough to unweight and pre-load, not so much that the player is airborne when the read arrives.

Measuring height with a phone camera: film side-on at 120 fps. Place a reference in the frame — a racket leaning against a wall (68 cm long) or a marked line on the fence. Freeze the frame at the hop's apex and the frame at landing; count the pixels between the feet and the reference; convert to centimeters. The difference is the hop height. A 5-10 cm hop is 5-10 cm on the reference scale.

## Width: The 1.5-2× Shoulder-Width Rule

The width target exists because of the base of support. A stance narrower than 1.5× shoulder width is unstable for a lateral push; a stance wider than 2× is slow to load and slow to move from. The 1.5-2× band is the sweet spot: a base wide enough to push from, narrow enough to move quickly.

Measuring width: film from behind at 120 fps. The player's shoulder width is measured first (or estimated from a known reference). Freeze the frame at landing and measure the distance between the feet. Divide by shoulder width. A ratio of 1.5-2.0 is in the band; below 1.5 is too narrow, above 2.0 is too wide.

## Weight Distribution: 55-70% Forefoot

The weight distribution target exists because of the spring loading. Forefoot loading pre-tensions the calf-Achilles spring; heel loading bypasses it (see ART-0103). The 55-70% forefoot band is the sweet spot: enough to load the spring, not so much that the player is on their toes and unable to absorb.

Measuring weight distribution: film from the side at 120 fps. Freeze the frame at landing and note the contact point: is the foot flat, forefoot-first, or heel-first? A forefoot-first landing with the heel slightly raised is in the band; a flat-foot landing is borderline; a heel-first landing is out. For a more objective measure, a simple balance scale or pressure insole can be used, but the side-on video is usually enough.

## Frequency: One Pulse Per Stroke

The frequency target exists because of the rhythm. The split step is tied to the opponent's stroke, not the player's. A player who splits twice per stroke is wasting energy; a player who splits every other stroke is late on the alternate strokes. The 1:1 ratio is the metronome.

Measuring frequency: count the split-step pulses and the opponent's contacts over a 10-stroke rally. The ratio should be 1:1. A ratio of 2:1 means the player is double-splitting; a ratio of 1:2 means the player is skipping every other split. Both are errors, and both are visible on video.

## The Phone-Camera Protocol

The full protocol takes 10 minutes and requires only a phone camera and a few reference marks:

1. **Setup.** Place the phone on a tripod or a stack of books, side-on to the player's split step. Set the camera to 120 fps (slow motion). Place a reference in the frame — a racket or a marked line.
2. **Height.** Film 10 split steps. Freeze the apex and landing frames; measure the displacement against the reference. Average the 10.
3. **Width.** Move the phone behind the player. Film 10 split steps. Freeze the landing frames; measure the foot separation and divide by shoulder width. Average the 10.
4. **Weight distribution.** Film from the side. Freeze the landing frames; score each as forefoot-first, flat, or heel-first. Target 7 or more forefoot-first out of 10.
5. **Frequency.** Film a 10-stroke rally. Count the split-step pulses and the opponent's contacts. The ratio should be 1:1.

The result is a four-number profile: height in cm, width as a ratio, weight distribution as a percentage, and frequency as a ratio. Track the profile over time; improvements in the metrics should match improvements in first-step time and court position.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hop height under 3 cm | Insufficient unweighting | No elastic pre-load; weak spring | Cue "drop and reload"; practice the 5-10 cm band with video feedback |
| Hop height over 12 cm | Treating the split as a jump | 100-150 ms wasted flight; late first step | Cue "drop, don't jump"; measure and target the 5-10 cm band |
| Stance width under 1.5× shoulder width | Narrow base | Unstable lateral push; slow first step | Widen the landing; measure and target 1.5-2× |
| Stance width over 2.5× shoulder width | Over-wide base | Slow to load; slow to move from | Narrow the landing; measure and target 1.5-2× |
| Heel-first landing | Heel loading | Achilles spring bypassed; slow push-off | Forefoot-first landing; pogo hops to recalibrate |
| Double-splitting (2 pulses per stroke) | Rhythm error | Wasted energy; late on the second pulse | Metronome drills; cue one pulse per opponent stroke |
| Skipping split steps on some strokes | Rhythm error | Late on the skipped strokes | Randomized feeds; the split is non-negotiable on every stroke |

## Practical Application: "Profile Your Pulse"

Give the player one image: **the split step is a measurable event, and the numbers tell you what to fix.** Then run the profile:

1. **Baseline.** Run the phone-camera protocol and record the four metrics. This is the baseline profile.
2. **Targeted fix.** Identify the metric that is most out of range and design a drill for it. If height is low, practice the drop-and-reload. If width is narrow, widen the landing. If weight distribution is heel-first, do pogo hops. If frequency is off, use a metronome.
3. **Re-test.** After two weeks of targeted drills, re-run the protocol. The metrics should move toward the target ranges, and the first-step time should improve.

A player with a measured split step does not guess what to fix — the numbers tell them.
