# ART-922: Growth Plate Injuries in Young Tennis Players

## Executive Summary
Growth plate injuries are a significant concern in young tennis players because the growth plates (physes) are the weakest links in the developing musculoskeletal system. Conditions such as Sever's disease (heel), Osgood-Schlatter disease (knee), Sinding-Larsen-Johansson syndrome (knee), and Little League shoulder equivalent in tennis (shoulder) are common overuse injuries in junior players. Proper recognition, load management, and prevention strategies are essential for protecting young athletes' long-term development.

## Growth Plate Anatomy
The growth plate, or physis, is a layer of cartilage near the end of long bones where bone growth occurs. It is weaker than the surrounding bone and ligamentous tissue, making it vulnerable to injury. Growth plates are present in the humerus (upper arm), radius and ulna (forearm), femur (thigh), tibia and fibula (lower leg), and calcaneus (heel). The growth plates typically close between ages 14-16 in girls and 16-18 in boys, though timing varies. During periods of rapid growth, the growth plate is particularly vulnerable to injury because it cannot withstand the same loads as mature bone and tendon.

## Sever's Disease (Calcaneal Apophysitis)
Sever's disease is the most common cause of heel pain in young athletes and is analogous to Little League elbow in the throwing arm. It involves inflammation of the growth plate in the calcaneus (heel bone) caused by repetitive stress from running and jumping. The condition typically affects children ages 8-14 and is particularly common in tennis players due to the sport's demands on the Achilles tendon, which attaches to the heel. Symptoms include heel pain during and after activity, limping, and pain with squeezing the sides of the heel. Management includes activity modification, heel cups or orthotics, calf stretching, and gradual return to play. The condition is self-limiting and resolves when the growth plate closes, but symptoms can persist for months if not properly managed.

## Osgood-Schlatter Disease
Osgood-Schlatter disease involves inflammation of the patellar tendon at its insertion on the tibial tuberosity, which is the growth plate on the front of the shinbone. It typically affects adolescents ages 10-15 during growth spurts and is common in tennis players due to the repeated jumping, landing, and cutting movements. Symptoms include pain and swelling at the tibial tuberosity, pain with kneeling, and a visible bony prominence. The condition is self-limiting and resolves with skeletal maturity, but symptoms can last 12-24 months. Management includes activity modification, patellar tendon stretching, quadriceps strengthening, and ice application after activity. In most cases, players can continue to play with modified activity.

## Sinding-Larsen-Johansson Syndrome
Sinding-Larsen-Johansson (SLJ) syndrome involves inflammation at the inferior pole of the patella (kneecap) where the patellar tendon originates. It is similar to Osgood-Schlatter disease but occurs at the top of the patellar tendon rather than the bottom. SLJ syndrome typically affects adolescents ages 10-14 and presents with pain at the lower pole of the patella, swelling, and pain with activity. Management is similar to Osgood-Schlatter, including activity modification, stretching, and strengthening. The condition resolves with skeletal maturity.

## Little League Shoulder Equivalent in Tennis
While "Little League shoulder" refers to proximal humeral epiphysitis in throwing athletes, tennis players can develop a similar condition involving the growth plate of the humeral head. The repetitive overhead motion of serving places stress on the proximal humeral growth plate, leading to pain, decreased serving speed, and altered mechanics. Symptoms include shoulder pain with serving, particularly in the late cocking and acceleration phases. Management includes serving reduction, mechanics correction, rotator cuff strengthening, and gradual return to full serving loads. The condition is self-limiting but can persist for several months if not properly managed.

## Risk Factors for Growth Plate Injuries
Several risk factors increase the likelihood of growth plate injuries in young tennis players. Rapid growth during puberty makes the growth plates particularly vulnerable. High training volumes, particularly serving loads for junior players, increase repetitive stress. Inadequate rest between training sessions prevents tissue recovery. Poor technique places abnormal stress on growing structures. Training on hard surfaces increases impact forces. Early specialization in tennis without multi-sport participation limits overall athletic development and increases overuse risk.

## Prevention Strategies
Prevention of growth plate injuries requires a comprehensive approach. Training load management is critical: the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that junior tennis players limit organized tennis to no more than 16 hours per week, with at least 2 rest days per week. Multi-sport participation should be encouraged until age 14 to develop overall athleticism and reduce repetitive stress. Gradual progression of serving loads, with attention to proper technique, reduces shoulder stress. Adequate rest between training sessions allows tissue recovery. Monitoring growth patterns helps identify periods of increased vulnerability. Education of coaches and parents about growth plate injuries ensures early recognition and appropriate management.

## Return to Play
Return to play after a growth plate injury should be guided by symptom resolution and functional readiness. The player should be pain-free with daily activities before returning to sport. Gradual progression of activity, beginning with low-intensity hitting and progressing to full-intensity play, should follow a structured timeline. Any return of symptoms warrants a step back in activity. Medical clearance should be obtained before return to competitive play, particularly for shoulder injuries.

## Practical Application: "Protect the Growth, Preserve the Future"
If you are a young tennis player, speak up when something hurts — pain is not normal and pushing through it can cause long-term damage. If you are a coach or parent, monitor training volume carefully and ensure young players have adequate rest. Limit organized tennis to 16 hours per week with at least 2 rest days. Encourage participation in multiple sports until age 14. Remember: the goal is not just winning junior tournaments but developing a healthy, durable athlete who can compete for decades. Growth plate injuries are temporary, but poor management can have lasting consequences.
