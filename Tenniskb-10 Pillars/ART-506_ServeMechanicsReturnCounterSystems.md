# ART-506: The Backscratch Position: Achieving Full Shoulder Rotation
## Executive Summary

The backscratch position — where the racket drops behind the back and the shoulder reaches maximum external rotation — is the loading phase of the upper body in the tennis serve. Achieving a deep, stable backscratch position stores elastic energy in the anterior shoulder muscles that releases during internal rotation at contact. This article covers the biomechanics, common faults, and training methods for optimizing this critical position.

## Defining the Backscratch Position

The backscratch position occurs at the peak of the serve's loading phase, when:
- The racket head points downward toward the ground behind the back
- The hitting elbow is raised to approximately ear height or above
- The shoulder is in maximum external rotation
- The trunk is loaded with rotation away from the net
- The eyes are focused upward at the toss

## Biomechanical Parameters

### Shoulder External Rotation Angle
Elite servers achieve external rotation angles of 160-180 degrees at the shoulder during the backscratch position. This means the forearm is nearly parallel to the ground or pointing slightly upward when viewed from the side. Greater external rotation = longer acceleration path = more time to build racket speed.

### Elbow Height
The elbow should be at or above shoulder height in the backscratch position. Players who drop the elbow below shoulder height (a "low elbow" backscratch) reduce the range of available internal rotation and lose power.

### Racket Head Position
The racket head should point toward the ground, with the strings facing slightly sideways. If the racket head points backward (toward the back fence), the player has excessive external rotation that may indicate a loss of control. If it points straight down, the position is optimal.

## The Elastic Energy Advantage

The backscratch position stretches the anterior shoulder muscles (pectoralis major, subscapularis, latissimus dorsi) and the internal rotators. Like stretching a rubber band, this loading stores elastic energy that recoils during the acceleration phase. Studies suggest this elastic energy contribution may account for 20-30% of total serve power.

## Common Backscratch Faults

### Insufficient External Rotation
Players who do not achieve full external rotation limit their acceleration path and rely more on active muscle contraction rather than elastic recoil. This results in lower serve speeds and higher energy expenditure.

### Excessive External Rotation with Loss of Control
Some hypermobile players achieve extreme external rotation but lose control of the racket head position. The racket swings too far behind the body, and the player must "catch up" during acceleration, causing timing inconsistencies.

### Low Elbow Position
When the elbow drops below shoulder height during the backscratch, the internal rotation path is shortened. This is often caused by:
- Weakness in the rotator cuff
- Tightness in the pectoral muscles
- Poor trophy pose mechanics
- Rushing the serve motion

### Trunk Collapse
If the trunk leans excessively forward or laterally during the backscratch, the shoulder cannot achieve full rotation against a stable base. Core stability is prerequisite to a proper backscratch.

## Training the Backscratch

### Shoulder External Rotation Stretching
Use resistance bands or a stretching partner to gradually increase shoulder external rotation range of motion. Hold stretches for 30-60 seconds, never forcing into pain.

### Isometric Backscratch Holds
Assume the backscratch position and hold for 10-15 seconds. This builds isometric strength in the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers at the exact joint angles used in the serve.

### Wall Lean Backscratch
Stand with your back against a wall and assume the backscratch position, pressing the back of the shoulder blade into the wall. This provides proprioceptive feedback about shoulder position.

### Video Analysis
Record your serve from the side and compare your backscratch position to elite player footage. Look for: elbow height relative to shoulder, racket head orientation, and trunk angle.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Elbow below shoulder at backscratch | Low elbow position | Reduced internal rotation range | Isometric backscratch holds at correct height; rotator cuff strengthening |
| Racket head points backward | Excessive external rotation | Loss of control; timing issues | Range-of-motion control drills; proprioception exercises |
| Trunk leans forward excessively | Poor core stability | Shoulder cannot rotate fully against stable base | Core strengthening; posture drills at trophy pose |
| Shoulder pain after serving | Impingement or labral stress from poor backscratch | Chronic shoulder issues | Physical therapy assessment; gradual ROM improvement |
| Inconsistent racket drop path | Variable backscratch mechanics | Unreliable serve quality | Repetition drills with video feedback; metronome timing |

## Practical Application: "The Phone Drill"

Hold a phone (or small object) between your hitting-side scapula and your trunk as you assume the trophy pose and backscratch position. If the phone drops during the motion, your shoulder is collapsing away from the trunk — a sign of poor scapular stability. If the phone stays in place throughout the backscratch and early acceleration, your shoulder is maintaining a stable, loaded position. This simple drill provides real-time feedback on scapular control during the serve.
