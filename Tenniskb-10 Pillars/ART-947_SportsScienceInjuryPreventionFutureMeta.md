# ART-947: Tennis Elbow Prevention — A Complete Protocol

## Executive Summary

Lateral epicondylitis, commonly known as tennis elbow, is one of the most prevalent overuse injuries in tennis, affecting an estimated 10-30% of recreational players and a significant minority of professionals. Despite its name, the condition is not limited to tennis players — it affects anyone who performs repetitive wrist extension and gripping motions. The injury involves degeneration of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon at its attachment to the lateral epicondyle of the humerus. Prevention requires a multi-faceted approach: equipment optimization (racket, strings, grip), technique modification (stroke mechanics), conditioning (eccentric strengthening of the wrist extensors), and early recognition of warning signs. A comprehensive prevention protocol can reduce the incidence of tennis elbow by 50% or more.

## Understanding the Injury

Lateral epicondylitis is not primarily an inflammatory condition (the suffix "-itis" is a misnomer). Histological studies show that chronic tennis elbow is characterized by tendinosis — degeneration of the tendon collagen, disorganized tissue repair, and the absence of inflammatory cells. The ECRB tendon is particularly vulnerable because:

- It is subjected to high loads during the backhand (particularly the one-handed backhand), where it acts to stabilize the wrist against the forces of ball impact.
- It has a relatively poor blood supply at its insertion on the lateral epicondyle, limiting its healing capacity.
- It is subjected to repetitive eccentric loading (the wrist flexes under load during the follow-through of strokes), which is the primary mechanism of tendinopathy.

## Equipment Optimization

### Racket Selection

- **Weight**: A heavier racket increases the forces transmitted to the elbow during ball impact. Players prone to tennis elbow should use a racket in the lighter end of the competitive range (280-310 g unstrung).
- **Balance**: A head-light racket (more weight in the handle) reduces the swing weight and the inertial load on the arm. Avoid head-heavy rackets.
- **Stiffness**: A stiffer racket frame transmits more shock to the arm. A more flexible racket (lower stiffness rating) absorbs more impact and reduces elbow stress. Look for a stiffness rating below 65 RA.

### Strings

- **String tension**: Higher string tension increases the forces transmitted to the arm. Players with a history of tennis elbow should string at the lower end of the recommended range (e.g., 48-52 lbs instead of 55-60 lbs).
- **String material**: Polyester strings are stiff and transmit more shock. Natural gut and multifilament strings are more arm-friendly. If polyester is desired for control, use a thinner gauge (1.25 mm or lower) and string at lower tension.
- **Hybrid setup**: Combining a soft multifilament or natural gut in the mains with a polyester in the crosses can provide control while reducing arm stress.

### Grip Size

- **Grip size matters**: A grip that is too small forces the player to squeeze harder to control the racket, increasing the load on the wrist extensors. A grip that is too large can also cause problems by limiting wrist mobility. The correct grip size allows the index finger of the opposite hand to fit snugly in the gap between the fingertips and the base of the thumb when gripping the racket.
- **Overgrips**: Replace overgrips regularly (every 5-10 hours of play) to maintain tackiness and reduce the need for excessive grip pressure.

## Technique Modification

### One-Handed Backhand

The one-handed backhand is the stroke most commonly associated with tennis elbow because the wrist extensors must stabilize the wrist against the full force of ball impact with only one arm on the racket. Technique modifications that reduce elbow stress:

- **Firm wrist at contact**: Avoid excessive wrist flexion or "breaking" the wrist at contact. The wrist should be firm and stable, with the racket face controlled by the forearm rather than the wrist.
- **Body rotation**: Use the legs and trunk to generate power rather than relying on the arm. The kinetic chain should sequence from the ground up, with the arm and wrist as the final link.
- **Follow-through**: A high, relaxed follow-through reduces the eccentric load on the wrist extensors. A short, abrupt follow-through increases the stress.

### Two-Handed Backhand

The two-handed backhand distributes the load across both arms and is generally less stressful for the elbow. However, the non-dominant arm (the left arm for a right-handed player) can be stressed if the player relies too heavily on the non-dominant arm to generate power. Ensure that both arms contribute to the stroke, with the dominant arm providing stability and the non-dominant arm providing power.

### Serve

The serve also loads the wrist extensors, particularly during the pronation phase. Technique modifications:

- **Pronation**: Ensure full pronation of the forearm during the serve. Incomplete pronation increases the load on the wrist extensors.
- **Grip**: Use a continental grip (or a slightly modified continental) to allow natural pronation. An eastern or semi-western grip on the serve can increase wrist stress.
- **Relaxation**: A relaxed grip and arm during the serve reduce the load on the elbow. A tight, rigid arm transmits more force to the tendon.

## Conditioning Program

The most evidence-based intervention for preventing and treating tennis elbow is eccentric strengthening of the wrist extensors. Eccentric contractions (the muscle lengthens under load) stimulate collagen remodeling and improve tendon strength.

### Eccentric Wrist Extension Protocol

- **Exercise**: Seated, forearm supported on a table, holding a light dumbbell (1-3 kg to start). Use the non-injured arm to lift the weight into wrist extension, then slowly lower it with the injured arm over 3-5 seconds.
- **Sets and repetitions**: 3 sets of 15 repetitions, once daily.
- **Progression**: Increase the weight by 0.5-1 kg when 3 sets of 15 can be performed without pain. Continue for 12 weeks.
- **Pain monitoring**: Some discomfort during the exercise is acceptable (up to 5 out of 10 on a pain scale). Sharp or increasing pain is a sign to reduce the load.

### Additional Conditioning Exercises

- **Wrist flexion and extension (concentric)**: 3 sets of 15 repetitions with a light dumbbell.
- **Pronation and supination**: 3 sets of 15 repetitions with a hammer or a light dumbbell.
- **Grip strengthening**: Squeeze a tennis ball or a grip trainer for 3 sets of 10-15 seconds.
- **Forearm pronation/supination with a band**: 3 sets of 15 repetitions.

## Early Warning Signs

Recognizing the early signs of tennis elbow allows for early intervention before the injury becomes chronic:

- **Pain on the outside of the elbow** during or after play, particularly during backhands or when gripping objects.
- **Pain when shaking hands** or lifting objects with the palm facing down.
- **Stiffness in the elbow** in the morning or after periods of inactivity.
- **Weakness in the grip** compared to the non-dominant side.

If any of these signs appear, reduce playing volume, implement the eccentric strengthening protocol, and consult a physical therapist if symptoms persist beyond 1-2 weeks.

## Load Management

Tennis elbow is an overuse injury, and load management is a critical component of prevention:

- **Gradual progression**: Increase playing volume gradually (no more than 10% per week) to allow the tendon to adapt.
- **Rest days**: Include at least 1-2 rest days per week from tennis to allow tissue recovery.
- **Cross-training**: Replace some tennis sessions with low-impact cross-training (cycling, swimming) to maintain fitness without loading the elbow.
- **Warm-up**: Always warm up before playing, including dynamic stretching and light hitting, to prepare the tendon for load.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player experiences pain on the outside of the elbow during backhands | Early-stage lateral epicondylitis | Progression to chronic tendinopathy if unaddressed | Implement eccentric wrist extension protocol; reduce playing volume; review technique |
| Player uses a stiff racket at high tension | Excessive shock transmission to the arm | Increased elbow stress, tendinopathy risk | Switch to a more flexible racket; reduce string tension; consider hybrid strings |
| Player's grip size is too small | Excessive grip pressure | Increased wrist extensor load | Measure and correct grip size; replace overgrips regularly |
| Player's one-handed backhand has a short, abrupt follow-through | High eccentric load on wrist extensors | Tendon degeneration | Modify technique for a high, relaxed follow-through; emphasize body rotation |
| Player's elbow pain persists despite rest and exercises | Chronic tendinopathy | Prolonged time away from tennis | Refer to physical therapist for manual therapy and progressive loading program |
| Player returns to full volume too quickly after a break | Insufficient tendon adaptation | Recurrence of tendinopathy | Progress volume gradually (10% per week); maintain eccentric strengthening program |

## Practical Application: "The 10-Minute Elbow Prevention Routine"

Perform this routine 3-4 times per week to maintain elbow health and prevent tennis elbow:

1. **Eccentric wrist extension** — 3 × 15 per side (1-3 kg dumbbell, 3-5 second lowering phase)
2. **Wrist flexion and extension (concentric)** — 2 × 15 per side (light dumbbell)
3. **Pronation and supination** — 2 × 15 per side (hammer or light dumbbell)
4. **Grip squeeze** — 3 × 10 seconds (tennis ball or grip trainer)
5. **Forearm stretch** — 30 seconds per side (wrist flexor and extensor stretches)

This routine takes approximately 10 minutes and requires minimal equipment. Perform it after tennis practice or as a standalone session. The eccentric wrist extension exercise is the most important component — it is the single most evidence-based intervention for preventing and treating tennis elbow. Combined with proper equipment, technique, and load management, this routine can keep your elbows healthy for years of competitive play.
