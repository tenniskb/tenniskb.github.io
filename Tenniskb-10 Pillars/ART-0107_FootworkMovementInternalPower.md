# ART-0107: Neurological Pre-Activation — Muscle Spindle Sensitivity During the Unweighting Phase

## Executive Summary

The unweighting phase of the split step is not empty time — it is when the nervous system sets the sensitivity of the muscle spindles that will govern the landing. Spindles are the body's length sensors; their sensitivity is tuned by gamma motor neurons, which adjust how tightly the spindle's internal fibers are stretched. Pre-activation during the drop primes the stretch reflex so that the landing impact triggers a faster, stronger push-off. This article explains the spindle, the gamma drive, and the training that builds the pre-activation.

## The Spindle: A Length Sensor with a Gain Knob

Muscle spindles are small sensory organs buried in the muscle belly, lying parallel to the muscle fibers. They are the body's length sensors: they detect how long the muscle is and how fast it is changing length. When a muscle is stretched, the spindle's internal fibers stretch with it, and the spindle fires — sending a signal to the spinal cord that says "lengthening, and fast."

The signal travels on the Ia afferent nerve, one of the fastest in the body, and reaches the spinal cord in 20-40 ms. There, it synapses directly onto the alpha motor neuron that innervates the same muscle. The alpha motor neuron fires back, and the muscle contracts. This is the stretch reflex — the knee-jerk reflex the doctor tests with a rubber hammer. Its latency is 30-50 ms, far faster than any voluntary movement.

The spindle has a gain knob. The sensitivity of the spindle — how much it fires for a given stretch — is set by the gamma motor neurons. These small neurons innervate the spindle's internal fibers and adjust their tension, like tuning a guitar string. A tighter string vibrates more; a sensitized spindle fires more. The gamma gain is set by the brain, in advance of movement, based on expected load.

## Gamma Drive in Plain Terms

Gamma drive is the brain's way of setting the spindle's sensitivity before the movement happens. The mechanism: the gamma motor neurons contract the spindle's internal fibers, keeping them taut even as the muscle lengthens. A taut spindle is a sensitive spindle — it fires strongly when stretched. A slack spindle is an insensitive spindle — it fires weakly.

The purpose is prediction. The brain knows that a landing is coming, so it raises the gamma gain in the 100-200 ms before contact. The spindles are sensitized; the landing impact then triggers a strong, fast reflex. This is pre-activation: the nervous system prepares the sensor before the event, so the response is ready when the event arrives.

The gamma gain is not fixed. It is adjusted for the expected load: a higher gain for a heavy landing, a lower gain for a light one. It is also adjusted for the intended movement: a higher gain for a push-off, a lower gain for an absorption. The brain is not just reacting; it is setting the gain for the response it expects.

## The Unweighting Phase as a Tuning Window

The unweighting phase — the drop before the landing — is when the gamma gain is set. During the drop, the nervous system raises the gamma drive and pre-activates the alpha motor neurons. The spindles are sensitized; the motor pool is ready. The landing then finds a prepared system.

This is why the drop matters (see ART-0102). A player who drops into the split step has time to set the gamma gain; a player who jumps and lands stiff has not. The drop is not just a mechanical unweighting; it is a neurological tuning window. The 100-150 ms of flight is the time the nervous system uses to prepare the landing.

The pre-activation is visible in the electromyography (EMG) signals of elite movers: the calf and quadriceps show activity before the foot touches the ground. The muscle is not waiting for the impact; it is already firing. The landing impact then adds to the pre-activation, and the push-off is the sum of both.

## How Landing Primes the Push-Off

The landing impact stretches the muscle-tendon unit. The sensitized spindle fires strongly — the Ia afferent signal is large because the gamma gain is high. The alpha motor neuron fires back with a strong, fast reflex that adds to the voluntary drive: the push-off is the sum of the voluntary contraction and the reflex.

The result is a stiffer, faster, more powerful push-off. The reflex contributes force within 30-50 ms of the stretch — faster than any voluntary response. A player with a sensitized spindle gets a "free" boost on every landing.

The difference between a "live" and a "dead" landing is the gamma gain. A live landing — sensitized spindles, pre-activated motor pool — produces a strong reflex and a fast push-off. A dead landing — slack spindles, cold motor pool — produces a weak reflex and a slow push-off. The mechanical event is the same; the neurological preparation is different.

## Training Spindle Sensitivity

The pre-activation is trainable, and the training is specific:

- **Plyometrics.** Jumping, hopping, and bounding train the stretch-shortening cycle and the pre-activation that supports it.
- **Drop landings.** Stepping off a box and landing with a short amortization trains the pre-activation for a specific impact.
- **Reactive hops.** Hopping in response to a visual or auditory cue trains the pre-activation for an external event — exactly the split step's demand.
- **Unexpected perturbations.** Landing on a slightly unstable surface or with an unexpected push trains the nervous system to adjust the gamma gain on the fly.

The principle is varied, unpredictable loading. A player who trains only with predictable landings develops a rigid pre-activation; a player who trains with varied landings develops a flexible one.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| No EMG activity before landing | No pre-activation | Weak reflex; slow push-off | Pre-activation drills: cue the legs to "fire" before contact |
| Landing feels "dead" — no bounce | Low gamma gain | Weak stretch reflex; slow push-off | Pogo hops and drop landings to sensitize the spindles |
| Pre-activation collapses under fatigue | Gamma gain degrades | Late, weak landings in third sets | Fatigue-resistant plyometrics; maintain pre-activation in late sets |
| Overly stiff landing | Gamma gain too high | Joint shock; no elastic storage | "Soft but active" landing; reduce the gain for the expected load |
| Pre-activation is rigid — same for every landing | Inflexible gamma gain | Poor adjustment to varied loads | Varied, unpredictable loading drills; perturb the landing |
| Slow push-off despite good mechanics | Weak reflex contribution | 50-100 ms slower than a live landing | Reactive hops; train the stretch reflex with fast, short landings |

## Practical Application: "Tune the Gain"

Give the player one image: **the landing is a reflex you prepare for, not an impact you survive.** Then build the pre-activation in three steps:

1. **The pre-activation cue.** Before each split step, the player cues the legs to "fire" — a mental trigger that raises the gamma gain. The cue is set during the drop, so the spindles are sensitized before the landing.
2. **The drop landing.** The player steps off a 20-30 cm box and lands with a short amortization, focusing on the "bounce" — the sign that the reflex is contributing. The goal is a live landing, not a stuck one.
3. **The varied landing.** The player practices landings with varied loads and surfaces — flat, slightly unstable, with a light push — so the gamma gain becomes flexible. The pre-activation adjusts to the event, not a fixed setting.

A player with a tuned gamma gain does not push harder — they push sooner, because the reflex is already primed when the foot touches the ground.
