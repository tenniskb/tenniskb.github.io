# ART-812: Injury Prevention Strategies in Tennis

## Executive Summary

Tennis injuries are common due to the sport's repetitive overhead movements, sudden directional changes, and high-impact loading. The most prevalent tennis injuries affect the shoulder, elbow, lower back, knee, and ankle. Research indicates that structured injury prevention programs can reduce injury incidence by 30-50%. Effective injury prevention requires understanding common tennis injuries, identifying individual risk factors through screening, implementing targeted prehabilitation exercises, and managing training loads appropriately.

## Common Tennis Injuries by Body Region

### Shoulder Injuries

- **Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy:** The most common shoulder injury in tennis, caused by repetitive overhead serving and overhead smashes. Characterized by pain during the cocking and acceleration phases.
- **Shoulder Impingement:** Compression of the rotator cuff tendons between the humeral head and the acromion. Often secondary to poor scapular control or muscle imbalances.
- **Labral Tears:** Damage to the cartilage ring surrounding the glenoid. Often associated with serving mechanics and repetitive overhead loading.
- **GIRD (Glenohumeral Internal Rotation Deficit):** Loss of internal rotation range in the dominant shoulder compared to the non-dominant side. Increases injury risk.

### Elbow Injuries

- **Lateral Epicondylitis (Tennis Elbow):** Degeneration of the extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon. Caused by repetitive wrist extension, particularly on the backhand and serves.
- **Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer's Elbow):** Less common in tennis but affects the medial epicondyle. Associated with forearm pronation during serves and forehand topspin.
- **UCL (Ulnar Collateral Ligament) Strain:** The ligament on the inner elbow can be stressed during the serve's acceleration phase.

### Lower Back Injuries

- **Muscle Strains:** The most common lower back injury, caused by sudden rotational movements or poor core stability.
- **Disc Herniation:** Compression and rotational forces on the lumbar spine can cause disc bulging or herniation. Common in players with poor serving mechanics.
- **Spondylolysis:** A stress fracture in the pars interarticularis of the lumbar vertebrae. Associated with repetitive hyperextension during serving.
- **Sacroiliac Joint Dysfunction:** Pain and dysfunction at the SI joint, often related to leg length discrepancies or pelvic asymmetry.

### Knee Injuries

- **Patellar Tendinopathy (Jumper's Knee):** Degeneration of the patellar tendon. Caused by repetitive jumping and landing during serving and overheads.
- **Meniscal Tears:** Damage to the knee's shock-absorbing cartilage. Often caused by twisting movements during directional changes.
- **ACL Injuries:** Less common in tennis than in cutting sports, but can occur during sudden stops and directional changes.
- **Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome:** Pain around the kneecap, often related to poor patellar tracking or muscle imbalances.

### Ankle Injuries

- **Ankle Sprains:** The most common acute tennis injury, occurring during lateral movements and sudden directional changes.
- **Achilles Tendinopathy:** Degeneration of the Achilles tendon, associated with repetitive push-off and sudden acceleration.
- **Stress Fractures:** Overuse injuries in the foot and ankle bones, particularly in players who rapidly increase training volume.

## Injury Risk Factors

### Intrinsic Risk Factors

- **Previous Injury:** The strongest predictor of future injury. Players with a history of shoulder, elbow, or ankle injuries are significantly more likely to re-injury.
- **Age:** Younger players are susceptible to growth plate injuries; older players face degenerative conditions and reduced tissue elasticity.
- **Flexibility/Mobility Deficits:** Limited shoulder internal rotation, hip mobility, or ankle dorsiflexion increases injury risk.
- **Muscle Imbalances:** Strength ratios between agonist/antagonist muscles (e.g., quadriceps/hamstrings, internal/external rotators) outside normal ranges.
- **Biomechanical Factors:** Poor stroke mechanics that place excessive stress on specific tissues.
- **Anatomical Variations:** Leg length discrepancies, scoliosis, and joint hypermobility.

### Extrinsic Risk Factors

- **Training Load:** Sudden increases in training volume or intensity are the primary extrinsic injury risk factor.
- **Surface:** Hard courts increase impact loading; clay courts increase sliding-related injuries.
- **Equipment:** Racket weight, string tension, and shoe type influence injury risk.
- **Recovery:** Inadequate sleep, nutrition, and recovery practices increase injury susceptibility.
- **Environmental Conditions:** Heat, cold, and altitude affect performance and injury risk.

## Injury Screening

### Pre-Season Screening

A comprehensive pre-season screening should include:

1. **Medical History:** Previous injuries, surgeries, and current symptoms.
2. **Movement Screening:** Functional movement assessment (FMS or similar) to identify movement deficits.
3. **Range of Motion Assessment:**
   - Shoulder internal and external rotation (GIRD screening)
   - Hip internal and external rotation
   - Ankle dorsiflexion
   - Thoracic spine rotation and extension
4. **Strength Testing:**
   - Rotator cuff strength (internal/external rotation ratio)
   - Core stability (plank, side plank, single-leg bridge)
   - Lower body strength (squat, lunge, single-leg squat)
   - Grip strength (dominant vs. non-dominant)
5. **Balance and Proprioception:**
   - Single-leg balance (eyes open and closed)
   - Star excursion balance test
   - Y-balance test
6. **Cardiovascular Fitness Assessment:** VO2max estimation or field-based fitness test.

### Ongoing Monitoring

- **Daily Wellness Questionnaires:** Track sleep, soreness, mood, and energy.
- **Weekly Training Load Tracking:** Monitor acute:chronic workload ratios.
- **Monthly Movement Re-Screening:** Re-assess movement quality and range of motion.
- **Injury Surveillance:** Log all injuries, even minor ones, to identify patterns.

## Prehabilitation Exercise Programs

### Shoulder Prehabilitation (Thrower's Ten Program)

1. External rotation at 0 degrees abduction
2. Internal rotation at 0 degrees abduction
3. External rotation at 90 degrees abduction
4. Internal rotation at 90 degrees abduction
5. Shoulder abduction
6. Scaption (scapular plane elevation)
7. Prone horizontal abduction
8. Prone rowing
9. Prone external rotation
10. Push-ups (plus)

Perform 2-3 sets of 15-20 repetitions, 3-4 times per week.

### Core Stability Program

1. **Plank:** 30-60 seconds, 3 sets
2. **Side Plank:** 30-45 seconds per side, 3 sets
3. **Dead Bug:** 10-15 repetitions per side, 3 sets
4. **Bird Dog:** 10-15 repetitions per side, 3 sets
5. **Pallof Press:** 10-15 repetitions per side, 3 sets
6. **Single-Leg Bridge:** 15-20 repetitions per side, 3 sets

Perform 3-4 times per week.

### Lower Body Prehabilitation

1. **Nordic Hamstring Curls:** 6-10 repetitions, 3 sets (eccentric hamstring strengthening)
2. **Single-Leg Squat:** 10-15 repetitions per leg, 3 sets
3. **Lateral Band Walks:** 15-20 steps per direction, 3 sets
4. **Calf Raises (bent and straight knee):** 15-20 repetitions, 3 sets
5. **Ankle Mobility Drill:** 10-15 repetitions per ankle, 3 sets

Perform 3-4 times per week.

## Load Management for Injury Prevention

### Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR)

- **Safe Zone:** 0.8-1.3
- **Danger Zone:** Above 1.5 (spike in loading)
- **Undertraining Zone:** Below 0.8 (detraining, loss of protective adaptations)

### The 10% Rule

Increase training volume by no more than 10% per week to allow tissue adaptation.

### Periodized Rest Days

- Minimum 1 complete rest day per week during the competitive season
- Active recovery days every 3-4 days during heavy training periods
- Strategic rest days before and after major tournaments

## Practical Application: "The Pre-Match Warm-Up as Injury Prevention"

Structure the pre-match warm-up to incorporate injury prevention elements:

1. **General Warm-Up (5 minutes):** Light jogging, skipping, or cycling to raise core temperature.
2. **Dynamic Mobility (5 minutes):**
   - Arm circles (shoulder preparation)
   - Leg swings (hip preparation)
   - Thoracic rotations (spine preparation)
   - Lunges with rotation (hip and trunk preparation)
3. **Prehabilitation Activation (5 minutes):**
   - Band pull-aparts (rotator cuff activation)
   - Mini-band lateral walks (glute activation)
   - Plank holds (core activation)
   - Single-leg balance (proprioception activation)
4. **On-Court Progression (5 minutes):** Graduated hitting from baseline to full-court rallies.

This structured warm-up prepares the body for performance while activating key injury-prevention muscle groups.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Recurring shoulder pain | GIRD exceeding 15 degrees, poor scapular control | Rotator cuff tendinopathy, serving pain | Sleeper stretches, thoracic mobility drills, scapular stabilization exercises |
| Chronic lower back pain | Poor core stability, excessive lumbar hyperextension in serve | Disc issues, muscle strains | Core stability program; serving mechanics review; lumbar stabilization exercises |
| Frequent ankle sprains | Poor proprioception, inadequate ankle stability | Lateral ankle sprains, chronic instability | Balance training; ankle strengthening; proprioceptive exercises |
| Patellar tendinopathy | Excessive jumping/landing volume, poor quadriceps flexibility | Knee pain, serving discomfort | Eccentric squat program; quadriceps flexibility; load management |
