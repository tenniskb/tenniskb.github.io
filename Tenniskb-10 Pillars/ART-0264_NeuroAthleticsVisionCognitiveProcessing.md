# ART-0264: Feedback Types and Their Effects on Motor Learning

## Executive Summary
Feedback is information about performance that guides future attempts. In tennis, feedback comes in several forms: intrinsic (internal sensory feedback), extrinsic (coach or video), knowledge of results (outcome), and knowledge of performance (movement quality). Each type has different effects on learning, and effective coaching matches feedback type to the learner's stage and the task's demands.

## Types of Feedback in Tennis

### Intrinsic Feedback
Intrinsic feedback is the sensory information generated by the movement itself: the feel of the ball on the strings, the sound of contact, the visual perception of ball flight. Intrinsic feedback is always available and is the primary source of feedback for advanced players.

### Extrinsic Feedback
Extrinsic feedback comes from outside the performer: a coach's verbal instruction, video analysis, or a ball machine's settings. Extrinsic feedback supplements intrinsic feedback and is particularly useful for beginners who cannot yet interpret their intrinsic feedback.

### Knowledge of Results (KR)
Knowledge of results is information about the outcome of the movement: "the ball landed in" or "the ball landed out." KR is essential for calibrating performance but can be overused, leading to dependence on external information.

### Knowledge of Performance (KP)
Knowledge of performance is information about the movement itself: "your contact point was too far back" or "your follow-through was incomplete." KP is particularly useful for refining technique and correcting errors.

## Feedback Schedules

### Concurrent Feedback
Concurrent feedback is provided during the movement. For example, a coach saying "bend your knees" while the player is hitting. Concurrent feedback can disrupt performance and should be used sparingly.

### Terminal Feedback
Terminal feedback is provided after the movement is complete. For example, "that ball landed 2 meters long." Terminal feedback allows the performer to complete the movement without interference.

### Faded Feedback
Faded feedback starts with frequent feedback and gradually reduces it as the skill develops. This schedule prevents dependence on external feedback and promotes self-correction.

### Bandwidth Feedback
Bandwidth feedback provides feedback only when performance falls outside an acceptable range. For example, a coach only comments on serves that land outside the target zone. This schedule promotes self-correction and reduces feedback dependence.

## Matching Feedback to Learner Stage

### Cognitive Stage
Provide frequent, detailed extrinsic feedback (both KR and KP) to help the learner understand the movement. Use concurrent feedback sparingly to avoid disruption.

### Associative Stage
Reduce extrinsic feedback frequency and promote self-discovery. Use terminal feedback and encourage the learner to interpret their intrinsic feedback.

### Autonomous Stage
Minimize extrinsic feedback and rely primarily on intrinsic feedback. Provide feedback only for major errors or when introducing new techniques.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | 
| Cannot self-correct errors | Extrinsic feedback dependence | Poor problem-solving | Faded feedback schedule |
| Performs well in practice, poorly in matches | Context-dependent learning | Ineffective transfer | Variable practice conditions |
| Ignores coach feedback | Intrinsic feedback preference | Missed corrections | Match feedback to learning stage |
| Feedback disrupts performance | Concurrent feedback overload | Poor execution | Terminal feedback |

## Practical Application: "The Feedback Ladder"
Climb the feedback ladder as your skills develop: (1) start with frequent extrinsic feedback to learn the movement, (2) gradually reduce feedback frequency as you develop intrinsic feedback, (3) use bandwidth feedback to promote self-correction, and (4) rely primarily on intrinsic feedback for well-practiced skills. This progression builds the ability to self-correct and adapt without external input, which is essential for autonomous performance under match conditions.
