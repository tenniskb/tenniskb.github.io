# ART-0618: Defending Against the Lob — Net Player Recovery Tactics

## Executive Summary

The lob is the net player's nemesis — a high, arcing shot that turns aggressive net positioning into a defensive scramble. Defending against the lob requires quick recognition, efficient backward movement, and reliable overhead technique. A net player who can handle lobs confidently is one who can stay at the net without fear, knowing they have a reliable recovery plan.

## Recognizing the Lob Early

The key to lob defense is early recognition. Watch the opponent's racket face at contact: an open face with an upward swing path indicates a lob. Also watch the opponent's body position — a lobber typically opens their stance and drops their back shoulder. The earlier you recognize the lob, the more time you have to retreat and set up an overhead. Practice reading lobs from the opponent's contact rather than waiting for the ball to reach its peak.

## The Drop-Step Technique

The first movement on recognizing a lob is the drop-step — the foot nearest the direction of the lob steps back and slightly to the side, rotating the body to face the sidewall. For a lob to the backhand side (left side for right-handed players), the right foot drops back and the body rotates so the left shoulder points toward the ball. For a lob to the forehand side, the left foot drops back. The drop-step initiates the backward movement while keeping the body balanced.

## Backward Movement Patterns

There are three primary backward movement patterns: (1) the backpedal — facing the net and stepping backward quickly (fastest for short distances, poor for long), (2) the side-shuffle — turning sideways and shuffling backward (slower but more stable), and (3) the crossover run — turning completely and running backward (fastest for long distances, most difficult to stop and hit). For lobs that land inside the service line, use the side-shuffle. For deep lobs, use the crossover run.

## Positioning for the Overhead

The goal of backward movement is to get behind and under the ball for the overhead. Position yourself so the ball is slightly in front of your head at the peak of its trajectory. For a backhand-side lob, position slightly to the left of the ball (for right-handed players). For a forehand-side lob, position slightly to the right. The non-dominant arm points up at the ball throughout the movement for tracking.

## The Recovery Shot

If you cannot reach a position for an effective overhead (the lob is too deep or you are moving too fast), play a recovery shot: a defensive lob of your own that buys time to get back to the net. The recovery lob should be high and deep, landing near the baseline. This prevents the opponent from hitting an easy smash and gives you time to recover your net position.

## The Skyhook

The "skyhook" is a desperation overhead hit while jumping backward to reach an extremely deep lob. Made famous by Jimmy Connors, the skyhook involves jumping off one foot, rotating in the air, and hitting the overhead at the peak of the jump. It is a low-percentage shot used only when the alternative is letting the ball bounce and losing the point. Practice skyhooks as a last resort, not a primary tactic.

## Common Lob Defense Errors

Staying at the net too long (failing to retreat early enough). Running backward while facing the net (slow, unstable). Failing to point the non-dominant arm at the ball (loses tracking). Rushing the overhead when time is available. Panicking and hitting a defensive lob when an overhead was playable. Giving up on the point without attempting a recovery.

## Tactical Application

Against strong lobbers: (1) stay 2-3 feet deeper at the net, (2) watch the opponent's racket face for early lob recognition, (3) practice overheads until they are automatic, and (4) use the drop volley occasionally (lobbers often stay deep, making drop volleys effective). Against weak lobbers: (1) move closer to the net for sharper angles, (2) be aggressive with overheads, and (3) use the lob volley to exploit their deep positioning.

## The Lob and the Wind

Wind dramatically affects lob defense. With the wind behind you, lobs will travel deeper — retreat earlier and faster. Against the wind, lobs will fall shorter — you may be able to play them as mid-court overheads rather than retreating to the baseline. In crosswinds, account for lateral drift by positioning to the windward side of the ball's trajectory.

## Lob Defense Drills

The "lob-overhead exchange" drill has one player lobbing and the other hitting overheads. The "moving overhead" drill practices overheads hit while retreating at various speeds. The "recovery lob" drill practices hitting defensive lobs when the overhead is not available. The "lob competition" game rewards successful overheads and recovery shots.

## Summary

Lob defense is a critical skill for any net player. Early recognition, efficient backward movement, reliable overhead technique, and the willingness to play recovery shots when necessary form the foundation of lob defense. Practice these skills regularly, and the lob will become a manageable challenge rather than a feared weapon.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cannot reach deep lobs | Slow recognition, poor backward movement | Easy lob winners for opponent | Drill early lob recognition, practice drop-step |
| Overhead misses after retreating | Poor tracking, rushing the swing | Unforced errors | Point non-dominant arm at ball, take your time |
| Consistently caught by surprise lobs | Not watching opponent's racket face | Late reactions, weak overheads | Focus on opponent's contact, read face angle |
| Recovery lob lands short | Insufficient pace or height on recovery | Attacking smash for opponent | Practice recovery lobs for depth and height |
| Gives up on deep lobs without trying | Mental resignation | Lost points without effort | Always attempt a shot, even if unlikely to succeed |

## Practical Application: "Turn and Chase"

The moment you read a lob, turn and chase the ball with commitment. Hesitation at the net is fatal — the ball sails over your head while you debate whether to retreat. Recognize early, drop-step, and move. The overhead will come.
