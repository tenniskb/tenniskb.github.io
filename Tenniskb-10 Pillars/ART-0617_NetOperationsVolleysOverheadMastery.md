# ART-0617: Poaching in Doubles — Aggressive Net Interception

## Executive Summary

Poaching is the act of a doubles net player crossing into their partner's side of the court to intercept a cross-court return, typically on a serve or approach shot. It is the most aggressive tactic in doubles, putting immediate pressure on the returner and often producing easy volley winners. Successful poaching requires anticipation, quick lateral movement, and excellent communication with your partner.

## The Mechanics of the Poach

The poach begins with a split step as the returner contacts the ball. If the ball is hit cross-court toward your partner's side, you explode laterally across the court, cross-stepping to intercept the ball at the net. The volley is hit as a punch volley (compact backswing, firm wrist) directed to the open court or at the opposing net player's feet. The key is timing: you must begin your movement the instant you read the cross-court direction, not after the ball has passed your body.

## When to Poach

Poach when: (1) the server has hit a strong serve that pushes the returner wide or forces a weak return, (2) you read the returner's body position indicating a cross-court shot, (3) the returner has been hitting predictable cross-court returns, and (4) you and your partner have established that poaching is the game plan. Do not poach when: (1) the serve is weak and the returner is set, (2) you cannot read the return direction, (3) the returner is known for down-the-line shots, or (4) your partner is not prepared to cover your vacated side.

## The Fake Poach

A variation is the "fake poach" — starting to cross as if you will poach, but stopping and returning to your side. This freezes the returner, who hesitates for a split second trying to decide whether you will actually poach. That hesitation often produces a weak return that your partner can handle. The fake poach is also useful for disrupting the returner's rhythm without the risk of leaving your side uncovered.

## Communication in Poaching

Clear communication is essential. Before the point, establish signals: a closed fist behind the back means "I will poach," an open hand means "I will stay," and a finger pointed to a side means "poach to that side." During the point, verbal cues like "go" or "no" help coordinate the movement. After the point, discuss what worked and what did not. Poor communication leads to both players poaching (leaving the court open) or neither poaching (missing opportunities).

## Covering the Poach

When you poach and miss the ball (it passes you), your partner must cover the open court on your side. This requires the partner to cross to your side immediately upon seeing you poach. If both players are moving in the same direction (toward the ball), the court is covered. If the partner stays on their side while you poach, the court is wide open. Drill this "poach and cover" movement until it becomes automatic.

## The Poach Volley Technique

The poach volley is typically hit on the move — you are crossing the court as you intercept the ball. This demands exceptional balance and a compact swing. The racket must be in front of the body (not trailing behind) to ensure clean contact. Aim the poach volley at the opposing net player's feet (the "body jab") or into the open court behind the net player. Do not aim cross-court — the cross-court net player is your partner's responsibility.

## Common Poach Errors

Poaching too early (before the return direction is clear). Poaching too late (after the ball has passed you). Not covering the open court after a missed poach. Poaching on weak serves when the returner can hit aggressive passes. Poor communication with your partner about poach intentions. Hitting the poach volley too hard (trying for an unnecessary winner).

## Tactical Application

Use poaching to: (1) put immediate pressure on the returner, (2) produce easy volley winners on weak returns, (3) disrupt the returner's rhythm with unpredictability, (4) exploit predictable cross-court returners, and (5) create openings for your partner by drawing the returner's attention. The psychological pressure of a poaching net player often causes returners to make errors even when not poached.

## Poaching Drills

The "serve-and-poach" drill has the server serve and the net player poach on the return. The "poach coverage" drill practices the partner's movement to cover the vacated side. The "fake poach" drill practices starting to poach and returning. The "competitive poaching" game rewards successful poaches and penalizes uncovered courts.

## Summary

Poaching is the essence of aggressive doubles net play. It requires anticipation, quick movement, compact volley technique, and flawless communication with your partner. A well-executed poach wins points outright; a poorly executed poach loses them. Drill the poach and its coverage until both players move as one unit.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Poach volley misses or is weak | Late movement, long backswing on the move | Easy return, lost advantage | Start movement earlier, shorten backswing |
| Court left open after poach | Partner doesn't cover vacated side | Easy passing shot | Drill poach coverage, establish communication |
| Returner anticipates poach | Predictable poach timing, no deception | Returner hits down the line | Vary poach timing, use fake poaches |
| Poach volley goes for winner unnecessarily | Over-hitting on poach volley | Unforced error, lost point | Poach for placement, not power |
| Both players poach | Poor communication | Entire court open | Establish clear signals, drill coordination |

## Practical Application: "Read and React"

The poach begins with reading the returner's racket face at contact. The moment you see cross-court direction, explode laterally. Trust your read — hesitation is the enemy of a successful poach. If you read it, commit fully to the movement.
