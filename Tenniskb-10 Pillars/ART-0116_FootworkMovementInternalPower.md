# ART-0116: Micro-Adjustments at Landing — Adapting Base Width to Inbound Ball Trajectory

## Executive Summary

The split step landing is almost always taught as a fixed template — feet hip-width apart, knees bent 20-30°, weight on the balls of the feet. But elite movers do not land the same way every time. They read the inbound ball's trajectory during the airborne phase and adjust their landing geometry — base width, foot angle, lateral weight bias — to prepare for the specific demands of the next shot. A narrow landing (feet at or inside hip width) optimizes for central balls that require explosive starts in either direction; a wide landing (feet at 1.5-2 x hip width) sacrifices initial acceleration for stability against extreme-angle shots. This article maps the relationship between ball trajectory and landing geometry, quantifies the trade-offs, and provides a decision framework for training adaptive landings.

## The Landing as Decision Point

Between the moment the opponent's racket contacts the ball (t = 0) and the moment the returner's feet touch the ground (t ≈ +30 to +80 ms), the visual system has already extracted three critical pieces of information about the inbound ball:

1. **Horizontal direction** — left, right, or center (available within roughly 80-100 ms of contact).
2. **Depth trajectory** — is the ball landing short, deep, or at the feet? (Available within roughly 120-150 ms from contact, based on the ball's vertical angle and spin-induced curvature.)
3. **Lateral angle** — how extreme is the angle? A ball traveling 5 m laterally from center requires a very different landing preparation than one traveling only 1 m.

This information arrives while the player is still airborne during the split step. Elite movers use the final 50-100 ms of flight to adjust their landing posture in response — a process that happens below conscious awareness but is trainable with deliberate practice.

## Narrow Landing: The Central-Ball Optimized Stance

**When to use it.** When the ball is traveling within roughly 1.5 m of the court's center line — the majority of groundstrokes in baseline rallies.

**Geometry.** Feet at or slightly inside hip width (roughly 20-25 cm apart), toes pointed forward or slightly outward (10-15°), weight distributed 50/50 across both feet, knees flexed to roughly 25-35°.

**Advantages.** A narrow base minimizes the moment of inertia around the vertical axis, allowing rapid acceleration in either direction. The first step can be taken with either foot as the "push" foot, and the lateral shift of the center of mass is small — typically 10-15 cm to reach the optimal push-off position.

**Disadvantages.** A narrow base provides less lateral stability against extreme-angle shots. If the ball is wider than anticipated, the player must widen the base after landing, which adds 30-50 ms to the first step. This is why the narrow landing is a gamble against wide serves or extreme cross-court angles.

## Wide Landing: The Extreme-Angle Stance

**When to use it.** When the ball is traveling more than 2.5-3 m from center — wide serves, sharp cross-court forehands, or down-the-line approaches that pull the returner off the court.

**Geometry.** Feet at 1.5-2 x hip width (roughly 35-50 cm apart), toes pointed outward 20-30°, weight shifted 60-70% toward the anticipated side, knees flexed to 30-40°.

**Advantages.** A wide base provides immediate lateral stability — the player is already in a position to push off toward the wide ball without needing to adjust foot placement. The eccentric load on the outside leg is pre-positioned, so the first step is purely concentric. Against extreme angles, a wide landing can save 40-80 ms compared to a narrow landing that must be widened after the fact.

**Disadvantages.** A wide base increases the moment of inertia around the vertical axis, making it harder to accelerate in the opposite direction. If the ball goes to the unexpected side, the recovery cost is 100-150 ms — even worse than the recovery cost from a narrow landing. The wide landing is a commitment.

## Foot Angle Adjustments

Beyond base width, the angle of the feet at landing encodes directional intent:

- **Toes pointed forward (0-10° outward).** Neutral — optimizes for acceleration in either direction. Used for central balls or when the direction is still uncertain.

- **Toes pointed 15-25° outward on one side.** Pre-commits the hip rotators to lateral push-off on that side. The hip is pre-rotated into the position it will need for the push, reducing the first step's rotational component by roughly 20-30 ms. Risk: if the ball goes the other way, the hip must internally rotate before it can push — adding 40-60 ms.

- **One foot slightly ahead of the other (10-15 cm stagger).** Pre-loads the trailing leg for a forward push or the leading leg for a backward push. Used when the depth trajectory is clear but the lateral direction is still uncertain.

## Weight Distribution Adjustments

Even within a fixed base width, the distribution of weight between the feet can be adjusted mid-flight:

- **50/50.** Neutral — either foot can push off.
- **60/40 toward one side.** Mild bias — the weighted leg is pre-loaded for push-off toward the opposite side (the "drive" leg). This is the most common form of anticipatory adjustment.
- **70/30 or more.** Strong commitment — the unweighted leg is essentially a "dead" foot that must be repositioned before it can push off in the opposite direction. Only justified when the directional certainty exceeds 80%.

## The Calibration Error

Most recreational players land with the same geometry regardless of ball trajectory — a fixed habit that is optimal for central balls but suboptimal for everything else. The result is that they are systematically slow on wide balls (because they land narrow and must widen after the fact) and unnecessarily committed on central balls (because they land wide when a narrow base would have been faster).

The fix is not to land wide for every ball — that would sacrifice the speed advantage on central shots. The fix is to land with the geometry that matches the specific trajectory of the incoming ball. This requires:

1. **Reading the trajectory early.** The directional read must occur during the airborne phase, not after landing. This is a visual-motor skill that develops with thousands of repetitions (see ART-0121 on the recognition window).
2. **Having a repertoire.** The player must have practiced both narrow and wide landings so that the motor program is available when the read demands it.
3. **Making the adjustment without thinking.** The landing geometry should be set by the visual input, not by a conscious decision. This is an advanced skill that requires deliberate, feedback-rich training.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player lands with identical base width for all balls | No trajectory-adaptive landing | Slow on wide balls; over-committed on central balls | Trajectory-sorting drill: coach calls "wide" or "central" during the player's hop; player must adjust landing width before touchdown |
| Player's feet are always parallel (no stagger) | Missing depth-trajectory information | Cannot pre-load for forward or backward push | Depth-aware drill: coach varies ball depth; player practices forward-stagger landings for short balls, backward-stagger for deep balls |
| Player lands with toes always forward | No foot-angle pre-rotation | Hip rotation adds 20-30 ms to first step on wide balls | Foot-angle drill: player practices landing with 20° outward toe angle on the side of the anticipated push |
| Player always lands 60/40 weighted | Fixed bias regardless of read | Exposed to the unweighted side; predictable to opponents | Neutral-landing drill: 50/50 weight distribution on central balls; only bias on wide balls |
| Player lands wide on central balls | Over-committing unnecessarily | Slower first step on the majority of shots | Narrow-landing constraint: place two markers 25 cm apart; player must land between them on central balls |
| Player cannot adjust landing mid-flight | Insufficient airborne processing time | Landing is always a fixed template; no adaptive benefit | Slow-motion landing drill: coach tosses a ball during the player's hop; player must call the direction and adjust landing width — video feedback |
| Player's landing width correlates with ball trajectory but is always too narrow | Under-reading lateral angle | Wide balls arrive before the first step can push off | Angle-calibration drill: player practices against a protractor-marked court; learns the visual angle that corresponds to "wide" vs. "central" |

## Practical Application: "Land for the Ball You See, Not the Ball You Expect"

Implement a landing-adaptation drill:

1. **Phase 1 (Central).** The coach feeds 10 balls to the center of the court. The player practices narrow landings (feet inside hip width) and neutral weight distribution. First-step time is the metric.

2. **Phase 2 (Wide).** The coach feeds 10 balls to the corners (2.5+ m from center). The player practices wide landings (feet 1.5-2 x hip width) with weight biased toward the ball side. First-step time and court coverage are the metrics.

3. **Phase 3 (Random).** The coach randomly alternates central and wide feeds. The player must read the trajectory during the hop and adjust the landing geometry accordingly. The coach scores landing accuracy (correct width for the trajectory) independent of shot outcome.

Over weeks, the player builds an automatic mapping between visual trajectory and landing geometry. The landing stops being a habit and becomes a response — calibrated to the specific ball that is about to arrive.
