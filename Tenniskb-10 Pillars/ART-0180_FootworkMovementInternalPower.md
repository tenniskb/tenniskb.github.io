# ART-0180: Biomechanical Footwork Audits — Mapping Movement Patterns via Video Analysis

## Executive Summary

A footwork audit turns match video into a movement map. Three camera angles, a handful of calibrated court dimensions, and a 30-minute coding session convert "he moves late" into "his split lands 180 ms after contact on 7 of 10 backhand rallies" — a measurable fault with a measurable fix. The audit's product is a ranked fault-priority list: three items, ordered by frequency times cost, attacked one at a time and re-measured on the same angles four to six weeks later. This article specifies what to film, how to calibrate pixels into meters, which frames and metrics to extract, and how to build the list that drives the next training block.

## The Three-Angle Minimum

Each angle answers different questions; a serious audit needs all three.

- **Side-on** (camera 1.2-1.5 m high, level with the player, 8-10 m off-court, dominant side visible): split timing, knee angles at landing, stride lengths, backswing onset relative to movement, stance depth.
- **Behind** (from the far baseline, centered, court width filling the frame): first-step type, hip orientation at contact for stance classification, lateral distances, recovery paths.
- **Wide or elevated** (high tripod, balcony, or a 4-5 m raised position behind): court geometry — depth at contact, recovery aiming points, coverage patterns, position relative to the bounce point.

Frame rate is the hard constraint: 60 fps is the minimum, but split timing needs 120-240 fps, where each frame spans 8.3-4.2 ms. At 60 fps a three-frame judgment error is ±50 ms — enough to misclassify a split entirely.

## Calibration: The Court as Ruler

Every court ships with its own measurement scale. The known dimensions — baseline to service line 5.49 m, service line to net 6.40 m, singles width 8.23 m, center mark to sideline 4.115 m — turn any frame containing those lines into a pixels-per-meter conversion. Measure one known horizontal distance in the frame, scale it, and every player position becomes a coordinate: depth behind the baseline at contact in meters, lateral offset from center, recovery distance covered. With a static, well-framed phone, errors of ±5-10 cm are realistic — more than accurate enough to flag a player drifting from 1 m to 3 m deep across a set.

## The Coding Session

Sample 8-12 rallies or 30-50 strokes per situation (forehand rally, backhand rally, return, wide defense) — enough to reveal patterns, small enough to code in one sitting. Seven key frames per stroke:

1. Opponent contact (t = 0).
2. Player's split landing, recorded as an offset in ms.
3. First-step initiation and type — jab, crossover, or gravity step, classified per ART-0158, ART-0157, and ART-0169.
4. Unit-turn onset.
5. Player contact — stance zone by hip line (see ART-0174), depth, contact height.
6. Position 0.5 s after contact.
7. Recovery completion position.

From those frames, five metrics do most of the diagnostic work:

- **Split offset** — ms from opponent contact, judged against the ART-0101 landing window.
- **Preparation lead time** — ms between unit-turn onset and arrival.
- **Contact depth** — m behind the baseline, tracked across the set for drift.
- **Recovery path ratio** — actual path length divided by straight-line distance to the aiming point; under ~1.2 is efficient, above ~1.4 indicates wandering (aiming points per ART-0171).
- **Stance distribution** — the split of open, semi-open, and neutral contacts by wing.

## Building the Fault-Priority List

Rank faults by **frequency × cost**. A split landing 150 ms late on 70% of backhand rallies outranks a spectacular but rare stutter-step, however ugly the latter looks in isolation. Rules for the list:

- **Three items maximum.** A ten-item list trains nothing.
- **One fault at a time**, attacked with a single corrective block.
- **Separate technical from physical.** Splits that are timely in set one and late in set three are not a timing fault — they are a conditioning fault, and no cue will fix them.
- **Re-audit on the same angles, same metrics, 4-6 weeks later.** The comparison, not the first audit, is what proves the block worked or did not.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Split offset above +150 ms on returns only | Return split keyed to server contact, not the forward swing | Rushed return contact | Re-cue to the server's forward acceleration (ART-0101 window) |
| Recovery ratio above 1.4 on the forehand side | Recovery path curves instead of running to the aiming point | 1-2 m extra travel per recovery | Aiming-point targets on court; re-code after two weeks |
| Contact depth drifts from 1 m to 3 m within a set | Fatigue- or spin-driven retreat (see ART-0175) | Court progressively surrendered | Conditioning block plus a depth re-claim cue |
| First step is a crossover on 80% of short-wide balls | Default pattern applied regardless of distance | Slow first 50 cm on short balls | Situational first-step selection drills |
| Unit turn begins under 300 ms before contact | Preparation back-loaded under pace | Rushed swings in fast rallies | Turn-on-first-step programming (ART-0179) |
| Only behind-court footage exists | Audit filmed from a single angle | Split offsets and knee angles unmeasurable | Add a side-on 120 fps angle before drawing conclusions |
| Fault list arrives with ten items | Everything flagged as a fault | Nothing trained | Re-rank by frequency × cost; keep the top three only |

## Practical Application: "The 45-Minute Audit"

Film 15 minutes of points from side-on and behind at 120 fps, elevated if available. Code ten strokes per situation — seven frames, five metrics — into a spreadsheet. Produce exactly three ranked faults with their numbers attached ("split +180 ms on 7/10 backhands" beats "moves late"). Assign one corrective block to the top fault, schedule the re-audit for week five on the same angles, and file the other two faults untouched. The discipline of the audit is not the filming — it is refusing to fix everything at once and insisting that the second measurement prove the first block right or wrong.
