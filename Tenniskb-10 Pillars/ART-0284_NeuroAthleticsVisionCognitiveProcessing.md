# ART-0284: Self-Talk and Internal Dialogue in Tennis

## Executive Summary
Self-talk is the internal dialogue that accompanies performance. In tennis, self-talk can be instructional ("watch the ball"), motivational ("come on"), or evaluative ("I always miss this"). Instructional and motivational self-talk enhance performance; evaluative self-talk degrades it. Understanding how to optimize self-talk allows players to use their internal dialogue as a performance tool.

## Types of Self-Talk

### Instructional Self-Talk
Instructional self-talk directs attention to task-relevant cues: "watch the ball," "bend your knees," "smooth." This type of self-talk is most effective during execution, as it directs attention to the process.

### Motivational Self-Talk
Motivational self-talk increases arousal and confidence: "come on," "let's go," "I can do this." This type of self-talk is most effective before and during competition, particularly when energy is low.

### Evaluative Self-Talk
Evaluative self-talk assesses performance: "that was terrible," "I always miss this," "I'm playing great." Negative evaluative self-talk degrades performance; positive evaluative self-talk can enhance confidence but may also create pressure.

## The Effects of Self-Talk

### Attention
Self-talk directs attention. Instructional self-talk directs attention to the task; evaluative self-talk directs attention to the self, which disrupts automatic processing.

### Confidence
Motivational and positive evaluative self-talk can increase confidence, particularly when the player is doubting themselves.

### Arousal
Motivational self-talk can increase arousal, which is useful when energy is low but counterproductive when arousal is already high.

### Performance
Research shows that instructional self-talk improves performance in precision tasks (e.g., serving), while motivational self-talk improves performance in strength and endurance tasks (e.g., sustained rallies).

## Optimizing Self-Talk

### Identify Your Self-Talk
Record your self-talk during practice and matches. Identify whether it is instructional, motivational, or evaluative.

### Replace Negative with Positive
Replace negative evaluative self-talk ("I always miss this") with positive instructional self-talk ("watch the ball").

### Use First Person or Second Person
Some players respond better to first-person self-talk ("I can do this"); others respond better to second-person ("you can do this"). Experiment to find what works for you.

### Keep It Short
Effective self-talk is short and simple: "watch the ball," "smooth," "come on." Long, complex self-talk consumes working memory and disrupts automatic processing.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | 
| Negative self-talk after errors | Self-criticism | Tilt and errors | Replace with instructional cues |
| Cannot maintain confidence | Low self-efficacy | Passive play | Motivational self-talk |
| Overthinks technique | Excessive internal dialogue | Paralysis by analysis | Short, external focus cues |
| Self-talk varies with mood | Emotional reactivity | Inconsistent performance | Develop consistent self-talk habits |

## Practical Application: "The Self-Talk Script"
Develop a personal self-talk script for different situations: (1) before points: "smooth and aggressive," (2) during points: "watch the ball," (3) after errors: "next point, reset," (4) during changeovers: "I can do this." Practice this script during training until it becomes automatic. During matches, use these scripted self-talk phrases to maintain optimal internal dialogue, regardless of the situation. This scripted approach to self-talk prevents the negative, unproductive internal dialogue that undermines performance and ensures that your mind is always working for you, not against you.
