# ART-919: Heat Illness in Tennis — Exertional Heat Stroke and Hydration Science

## Executive Summary
Heat illness is a potentially life-threatening risk in tennis, particularly during tournaments in hot and humid conditions. Exertional heat stroke (EHS) is the most severe form and requires immediate recognition and treatment. Heat acclimatization, proper hydration, and monitoring of environmental conditions are essential prevention strategies. Understanding the Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) index and its implications for competition scheduling helps protect players.

## The Physiology of Heat Illness
During tennis, muscle activity generates significant heat, and the body must dissipate this heat to maintain core temperature within safe limits. The primary mechanisms of heat loss are radiation, convection, conduction, and evaporation (sweating). In hot and humid conditions, evaporation becomes less effective because the air is already saturated with moisture, reducing the body's ability to cool itself. As core temperature rises, heart rate increases, cardiac output is redirected to the skin for cooling, and blood flow to working muscles decreases. This creates a cascade of physiological strain that can progress from heat cramps to heat exhaustion to heat exhaustion and ultimately to exertional heat stroke.

## Heat Cramps and Heat Exhaustion
Heat cramps are painful involuntary muscle contractions that occur during heavy exercise in hot conditions, typically related to fluid and electrolyte loss. Heat exhaustion is characterized by an elevated core temperature (below 40 degrees C / 104 degrees F), heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache, and rapid pulse. The player is still sweating, and the thermoregulatory system is still functioning, albeit overwhelmed. Treatment involves moving the player to a cool environment, removing excess clothing, applying cold towels or ice packs, and providing fluids. Heat exhaustion can progress to heat stroke if not treated promptly.

## Exertional Heat Stroke
Exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency defined by a core body temperature above 40 degrees C (104 degrees F) combined with central nervous system dysfunction, which may include confusion, collapse, aggression, seizures, or loss of consciousness. EHS can occur even in well-conditioned athletes and is not limited to unfit individuals. The key differentiator between heat exhaustion and heat stroke is the presence of CNS dysfunction and the cessation of sweating (though sweating may still be present in EHS). EHS causes multi-organ damage and can be fatal if not treated immediately. The gold standard treatment is cold water immersion (35-38 degrees C / 95-100 degrees F), which should be initiated within minutes of recognition. The phrase "cool first, transport second" emphasizes that immediate cooling takes priority over transporting to a hospital.

## The 15% Rule for Heat Acclimatization
Heat acclimatization is the process by which the body adapts to exercise in heat, resulting in improved sweating efficiency, expanded plasma volume, reduced heart rate at a given workload, and lower core temperature. The 15% rule provides a guideline for progressively exposing players to heat. For the first day of heat exposure, training volume should be reduced by approximately 15% compared to normal. Each subsequent day, volume can be increased by approximately 10-15% until the player is training at full volume, typically after 10-14 days. The rule applies to both the duration and intensity of training. During acclimatization, players should train during the cooler parts of the day when possible and ensure adequate hydration and rest between sessions.

## WBGT Monitoring
The Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) index is the standard measure of environmental heat stress. It combines temperature, humidity, solar radiation, and wind speed into a single value. WBGT is calculated as: WBGT = 0.7 x wet bulb temperature + 0.2 x globe temperature + 0.1 x dry bulb temperature. Tennis organizations use WBGT thresholds to modify competition: below 18 degrees C (64 degrees F) is low risk; 18-23 degrees C (64-73 degrees F) is moderate risk; 23-28 degrees C (73-82 degrees F) is high risk; above 28 degrees C (82 degrees F) is very high risk. At high and very high risk levels, match formats should be modified (shorter sets, longer breaks), and at extreme levels, play should be suspended.

## Hydration Science
Proper hydration before, during, and after tennis is critical for performance and safety. Pre-exercise hydration should aim for a pale yellow urine color and adequate fluid intake in the 24 hours before play. During exercise, players should drink to replace sweat losses, typically 150-250 ml every 15-20 minutes, adjusted for individual sweat rates and environmental conditions. Post-exercise hydration should replace 125-150% of body weight lost during exercise, with sodium-containing fluids to promote fluid retention. Sports drinks containing 6-8% carbohydrates and electrolytes are beneficial for matches lasting longer than 60 minutes. Individual sweat rate testing, where body weight is measured before and after exercise, helps players determine their personal fluid replacement needs.

## Cooling Strategies
Cooling strategies reduce heat strain during competition. Pre-cooling, such as cold water immersion or wearing cooling vests before play, can improve performance in heat. During play, players should use ice towels on the neck and head, wear light-colored and loose-fitting clothing, use sunscreen to prevent sunburn (which impairs sweating), and seek shade during changeovers. Cold fluids and ice slurry consumption during breaks can help reduce core temperature. The most effective cooling method during play is the application of cold towels to the neck, armpits, and groin, where major blood vessels are close to the skin surface.

## Practical Application: "Beat the Heat Before It Beats You"
If you are traveling to a tournament in a hot climate, arrive at least 7-10 days early to allow for heat acclimatization. During the first two days, reduce your training volume by 20-30% and train during cooler hours. Gradually increase volume by 10-15% per day. Drink a minimum of 500 ml of fluid 2 hours before match play and continue drinking during changeovers. Monitor your urine color and body weight to assess hydration status. If you experience headache, dizziness, or unusual fatigue during play, stop immediately, seek shade, and begin cooling. Heat stroke kills — respect the heat and listen to your body.
