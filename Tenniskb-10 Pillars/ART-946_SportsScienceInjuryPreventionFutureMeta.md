# ART-946: Prehabilitation for Tennis — Preventing Injury Before It Happens

## Executive Summary

Prehabilitation (prehab) is the proactive identification and correction of movement dysfunctions, strength deficits, and mobility limitations before they cause injury. In a sport as physically demanding as tennis — with its repetitive overhead motions, explosive direction changes, and year-round schedule — prehab is not optional; it is essential. Research consistently shows that structured prehab programs reduce injury rates by 30-50% in sports with physical demands similar to tennis. A comprehensive prehab program includes pre-season screening (movement assessment, strength testing, and injury history review), targeted corrective exercises for individual deficits, and ongoing maintenance work integrated into the regular training routine.

## The Most Common Tennis Injuries

To design an effective prehab program, you must first understand what you are preventing. The most common tennis injuries, by body region:

### Shoulder
- **Rotator cuff tendinopathy**: Degeneration of the rotator cuff tendons from repetitive overhead loading (serves, overheads).
- **Shoulder impingement**: Compression of the rotator cuff tendons between the humeral head and the acromion during overhead movements.
- **Labral tears**: Damage to the cartilage rim of the shoulder socket, often from repetitive overhead motion or acute trauma.

### Elbow
- **Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow)**: Degeneration of the extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon at the lateral epicondyle, caused by repetitive wrist extension (backhand, serve).
- **Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow)**: Degeneration of the flexor-pronator tendon at the medial epicondyle, caused by repetitive wrist flexion (serve, forehand).

### Wrist and Hand
- **Wrist tendinopathy**: Inflammation of the wrist tendons from repetitive gripping and wrist motion.
- **Triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) tears**: Damage to the cartilage and ligaments on the ulnar side of the wrist, common in players with excessive wrist snap on serves.

### Spine
- **Lower back pain**: The most common tennis injury, caused by repetitive hyperextension (serve), rotation (groundstrokes), and the combination of both.
- **Disc injuries**: Herniation or degeneration of the intervertebral discs, often related to poor movement patterns and repetitive loading.

### Hip and Groin
- **Hip impingement (FAI)**: Abnormal contact between the femoral head and the acetabulum, limiting hip rotation and causing pain.
- **Groin strains**: Adductor muscle tears from the lateral movements and split steps of tennis.

### Knee
- **Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee)**: Degeneration of the patellar tendon from repetitive jumping and landing.
- **Meniscus tears**: Damage to the knee cartilage from twisting or pivoting movements.
- **ACL tears**: Anterior cruciate ligament rupture, typically from a sudden deceleration or change of direction.

### Ankle
- **Ankle sprains**: The most common acute tennis injury, typically inversion sprains from lateral movements or landing on an opponent's foot.
- **Achilles tendinopathy**: Degeneration of the Achilles tendon from repetitive push-off and acceleration.

## Pre-Season Screening

Pre-season screening is the foundation of prehab. It identifies the individual risk factors that each player brings to the season. A comprehensive screening includes:

### Movement Screening
- **Functional Movement Screen (FMS)**: The 7-test movement assessment described in Article 939. Identifies mobility limitations, stability deficits, and asymmetries.
- **Overhead squat assessment**: Evaluates bilateral mobility and stability in a movement pattern fundamental to tennis.
- **Single-leg squat assessment**: Identifies hip and knee stability deficits on each leg.

### Strength Testing
- **Isokinetic or manual muscle testing**: Measures strength of the shoulder rotators, scapular stabilizers, hip abductors, and core muscles. Identifies left-right asymmetries and absolute strength deficits.
- **Grip strength**: A marker of overall upper-body strength and a risk factor for elbow and wrist injuries.

### Mobility Assessment
- **Shoulder range of motion**: Internal and external rotation, flexion, and abduction. Identifies the internal rotation deficit common in overhead athletes.
- **Hip range of motion**: Internal and external rotation, flexion, and extension. Identifies hip mobility limitations that contribute to lower back and groin injuries.
- **Ankle dorsiflexion**: Measured by the knee-to-wall test. Limited dorsiflexion is a risk factor for ankle sprains and Achilles tendinopathy.

### Injury History Review
- **Previous injuries**: A history of injury is the strongest predictor of future injury. Players with a history of ankle sprain, for example, are 3-5 times more likely to sprain again.
- **Training load history**: Sudden increases in training volume or intensity are a major risk factor for overuse injuries.

## Corrective Exercise Selection Based on Screening

The screening results guide the selection of corrective exercises. The general principle: address the most significant deficit first, and prioritize mobility before stability, and stability before strength.

### Shoulder Mobility Deficit
- **Corrective exercises**: Sleeper stretches, cross-body shoulder stretches, thoracic spine mobility work (foam rolling, rotational stretches), and internal rotation stretches with a band.
- **Frequency**: Daily, 10-15 minutes.

### Scapular Stability Deficit
- **Corrective exercises**: Scapular setting exercises (retraction and depression), band external rotations, prone Y-T-W-L exercises, and serratus anterior punches.
- **Frequency**: 3-4 times per week, 2-3 sets of 10-15 repetitions.

### Hip Mobility Deficit
- **Corrective exercises**: 90/90 hip stretches, pigeon pose, hip flexor stretches, and hip internal rotation mobilizations.
- **Frequency**: Daily, 10-15 minutes.

### Ankle Dorsiflexion Deficit
- **Corrective exercises**: Knee-to-wall mobilizations, banded ankle mobilizations, and calf stretching (gastrocnemius and soleus).
- **Frequency**: Daily, 5-10 minutes.

### Core Stability Deficit
- **Corrective exercises**: Dead bugs, bird dogs, Pallof presses, and plank variations.
- **Frequency**: 3-4 times per week, 2-3 sets of 10-15 repetitions.

## The Prehab Maintenance Program

Once the screening deficits are addressed, a maintenance program prevents recurrence and addresses the ongoing demands of tennis. This program should be integrated into the regular training routine (15-20 minutes, 3-4 times per week):

### Shoulder Prehab (5 minutes)
- Band external rotations: 2 × 15 per side
- Prone Y-T-W: 2 × 8 each position
- Scapular push-ups: 2 × 12

### Core Prehab (5 minutes)
- Dead bug: 2 × 10 per side
- Pallof press: 2 × 10 per side
- Side plank: 2 × 30 seconds per side

### Lower Body Prehab (5 minutes)
- Single-leg glute bridges: 2 × 12 per side
- Lateral band walks: 2 × 15 steps per direction
- Single-leg balance with reach: 2 × 8 per side

### Ankle Prehab (5 minutes)
- Single-leg balance: 2 × 30 seconds per side
- Banded ankle dorsiflexion: 2 × 15 per side
- Calf raises (eccentric emphasis): 2 × 12 per side

## Integrating Prehab into the Training Week

Prehab should not be an afterthought; it should be a scheduled component of the training week. A sample integration:

| Day | Pre-Training | Main Session | Post-Training |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Monday | Shoulder prehab (5 min) | Strength training | Core prehab (5 min) |
| Tuesday | Lower body prehab (5 min) | On-court (match play) | Ankle prehab (5 min) |
| Wednesday | Shoulder prehab (5 min) | Active recovery | Foam rolling (10 min) |
| Thursday | Core prehab (5 min) | On-court (technique) | Lower body prehab (5 min) |
| Friday | Shoulder prehab (5 min) | Strength training | Core prehab (5 min) |
| Saturday | Lower body prehab (5 min) | On-court (match play) | Ankle prehab (5 min) |
| Sunday | Complete rest | Complete rest | Complete rest |

## The Cost-Benefit of Prehab

Prehab requires an investment of time (15-20 minutes per day) and attention. The return on that investment is substantial:

- **Reduced injury rates**: 30-50% reduction in injuries in sports with similar demands to tennis.
- **Reduced time lost from training and competition**: Fewer injuries mean more consistent training and more matches played.
- **Improved performance**: Correcting movement dysfunctions and strength deficits improves movement quality, power output, and efficiency.
- **Longevity**: Players who stay healthy longer have longer careers. Prehab is an investment in career duration.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player has recurring shoulder pain | Unaddressed scapular instability or mobility deficit | Chronic tendinopathy, potential labral tear | Add scapular stabilization and shoulder mobility work to daily routine |
| Player sprains the same ankle repeatedly | Inadequate proprioceptive training and ankle stability | Chronic ankle instability, recurrent sprains | Add single-leg balance and ankle strengthening 3-4x per week |
| Player's lower back pain flares during serving | Poor hip mobility and core stability | Chronic back pain, reduced serve velocity | Add hip mobility work and anti-extension core exercises |
| Player's elbow pain worsens with volume | Insufficient forearm and wrist conditioning | Chronic tennis elbow, time away from training | Add eccentric wrist extension exercises; review equipment and technique |
| Player's training load increases suddenly | No prehab base to handle increased load | Overuse injuries (tendinopathy, stress fractures) | Build prehab base before increasing load; progress gradually |
| Player's left-right asymmetry is significant | Unilateral sport without bilateral correction | Overuse on dominant side, underuse injuries on non-dominant side | Add unilateral exercises for the non-dominant side; address strength asymmetries |

## Practical Application: "The 15-Minute Daily Prehab Circuit"

Perform this circuit 3-4 times per week, before training or as a standalone session. It targets the most common injury sites in tennis and takes approximately 15 minutes.

1. **Band external rotations** — 2 × 15 per side (shoulder)
2. **Prone Y-T-W** — 2 × 8 each position (scapular stability)
3. **Dead bug** — 2 × 10 per side (core stability)
4. **Pallof press** — 2 × 10 per side (anti-rotation)
5. **Single-leg glute bridge** — 2 × 12 per side (hip stability)
6. **Lateral band walk** — 2 × 15 steps per direction (hip abductor strength)
7. **Single-leg balance with reach** — 2 × 8 per side (ankle proprioception)
8. **Eccentric calf raise** — 2 × 12 per side (Achilles and ankle)

This circuit requires minimal equipment (a resistance band and a mat) and can be performed anywhere. It is the most cost-effective injury prevention tool available. The players who perform it consistently are the ones who stay on court, week after week, month after month, year after year. Prehab is not glamorous, but it is the foundation of a long and successful tennis career.
