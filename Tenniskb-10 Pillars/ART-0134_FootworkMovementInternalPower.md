# ART-0134: Spiral Torque Generation in the Lower Legs — Internal Hip Screw Mechanics

## Executive Summary

ART-0127 traced the silk-reeling spiral through the torso — the hip-shoulder separation that winds and unwinds the trunk. This article follows the spiral one joint lower, into the hip itself. The "internal hip screw" is the relative rotation between pelvis and femur across a planted foot: as the unit turn rotates the pelvis away from the target while the foot stays fixed to the court, the hip joint winds like a bolt turning in a nut. The winding pre-loads the deep rotators, gluteal complex, and posterior hip capsule with elastic strain; the unwinding fires the pelvis into rotation with torque the waist muscles alone could not supply. The traditional cue — "screw the foot into the ground" — describes a real event, but it must be coached precisely: the screw lives in the hip, not in a twisting foot.

## Anatomy of the Screw

The hip is a ball-and-socket with roughly 30-45° of internal and 40-60° of external rotation available in most athletes, with wide individual variation. Take the open-stance forehand of a right-hander:

- **The foot locks.** The right (outside) foot is planted, forefoot-loaded, held by court friction (see ART-0133 on grip). It does not twist. The foot is the nut that holds still.
- **The pelvis rotates away.** The unit turn swings the pelvis 30-45° away from the target. Because the femur follows the fixed foot rather than the turning pelvis, the stance hip is wound into relative internal rotation.
- **The winding stretches the rotator bank.** Relative internal rotation loads the hip's external rotators — gluteus maximus, piriformis, the obturators, the gemelli, quadratus femoris — plus the posterior capsule, in eccentric pre-tension.
- **The unwinding fires.** As the stroke begins, the pelvis reverses toward the target, driving the stance hip through relative external rotation. The pre-loaded rotators contract onto a stretch, adding their torque to pelvic rotation, which reaches 300-500°/s in elite forehands and feeds the chain upward (see ART-0131).

The knee and ankle complete the mechanism as distributors: a fixed foot means the rotation between pelvis and ground is shared — most by the hip, a small amount by tibial rotation at the flexed knee, a trace by the subtalar joint. The screw is a three-joint spiral, which is why winding it through the wrong joint is dangerous.

## Winding Before Unwinding

The screw's value is entirely in its sequencing:

- **Wind during the unit turn.** The load phase of the stroke should leave the stance hip at its comfortable end of internal rotation — wound, not strained. A hip wound only halfway delivers half the pre-load; a hip forced past its range delivers pain.
- **Release within the first 100 ms of the forward swing.** The rotator bank unloads early in the stroke, because pelvic rotation must lead trunk rotation by 30-60 ms for the chain to sequence (see ART-0126). A screw that releases late arrives after the trunk has already turned — torque delivered to a train that has left the station.
- **Re-ground on the follow-through.** After release, the stance hip re-winds slightly — why elite finishes show the outside foot still gripping while the pelvis has rotated past it.

## The Screw in Open Stances

- **Open-stance forehand:** the biggest screw. The wider and more open the stance, the more the pelvis rotates over the fixed outside foot and the more the hip contributes to the shot.
- **Two-handed backhand:** the mirror. For a right-hander, the left (outside) hip winds and fires identically; the two-hander is a left-hander's forehip in disguise.
- **Serve:** the coil flexes and internally rotates the back hip against a roughly fixed rear foot; the screw releases upward into extension and rotation as the legs drive.
- **Neutral and closed stances:** the screw is smaller because the pelvis rotates less over the planted foot; these stances trade hip torque for linear weight transfer.

## Cueing the Screw Safely

"Screw the foot into the ground" is an effort cue, not a literal instruction. Taken literally, it plants a fixed foot and twists it — driving rotation through the knee's menisci and ligaments, a classic mechanism of knee injury. The correct coaching language separates the parts:

- **The foot stays still.** Grip the court; the toes do not pivot.
- **The pelvis does the turning.** Cue "rotate your belt buckle away from the target over the planted foot."
- **The knee tracks the toes.** From the front, the planted kneecap stays stacked over the shoelaces through wind and unwind. Any inward drift means the screw is shearing the knee instead of loading the hip.
- **Range is screened, not forced.** Screen supine — hip flexed 90°, rotate the shin to estimate internal and external range. A stiff hip rotates the pelvis less, and the missing rotation migrates into the lumbar spine: this is how stiff-hipped players acquire bad backs.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Outside foot visibly pivots on court during the swing | Cue taken literally; foot twisting against friction | Knee shear; grip lost mid-stroke | Re-cue: "foot still, belt buckle turns"; film the shoe — it must not rotate |
| Planted knee drifts inward on the unwind | Screw routed through the knee, not the hip | Knee strain; medial collapse at contact | Slow-motion shadows at 20% speed with knee-over-toes check; stop and reset each rep |
| No visible wind before the swing | Player rushes from split step straight to forward swing | Rotators unloaded; pelvis starts from slack | Partner-resisted pelvis wind: partner holds the hips, resists the coil 3 s, releases for the fire |
| Lower back sore after heavy forehand sessions | Stiff hips pushing rotation into the lumbar spine | Gradual back overload; truncated coil | Rotation-range screen; mobility work before loading the screw deeper |
| Groin pinch at full coil | Hip wound past its available internal rotation | Impingement symptoms; player shortens the turn | Wind to 80-90% of range; widen the stance slightly to change the hip's loading angle |
| Open stance no stronger than neutral stance | Screw not contributing; stroke is all weight shift | Missing 10-15% of available pelvic torque | Isometric wind holds: rotate pelvis over the fixed foot to the wound position, hold 5-8 s, 6-8 per side |
| Deep buttock stretch never felt on the coil | Winding phase skipped or too small | Elastic pre-load absent; concentric-only rotation | Winding wall drill: outside foot 20 cm from a wall, hand on wall, slow pelvis rotations away, 10-15 per side |

## Practical Application: "Wind the Hip, Not the Foot"

Give the player one sentence: **the foot is the nut, the pelvis is the bolt — turn the bolt.** At the next session, spend five minutes on the winding wall drill: outside foot planted near a wall, one hand on the wall for balance, ten slow pelvis rotations away from the planted foot until the deep buttock stretch is unmistakable. Then take the racket: ten open-stance shadow forehands at half speed with the coach watching only two things — the shoe (still) and the kneecap (stacked over the toes). Only then hit fed balls, feeling for the sensation that the hip is wound before every swing. Within a single session, most players report the forehand "goes by itself" — the signature of a screw that is finally being wound before it is fired.
