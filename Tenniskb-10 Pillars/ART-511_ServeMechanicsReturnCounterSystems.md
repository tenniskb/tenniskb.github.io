# ART-511: Underhand Serve: Tactical Use and Mechanical Execution
## Executive Summary

The underhand serve is one of the most debated shots in tennis — often considered unsportsmanlike by traditionalists yet perfectly legal under ITF rules. This article examines the tactical situations where the underhand serve is a high-percentage play, the mechanical keys to executing it effectively, and the etiquette considerations surrounding its use.

## Tactical Justification

The underhand serve is tactically sound in several specific situations:

### When the Returner Stands Deep
If the returner positions themselves 10-15 feet behind the baseline, a well-executed underhand serve forces them to sprint forward, converting their deep positioning from an advantage to a liability.

### Against Players Who Struggle with Low Balls
Some players have difficulty handling balls at their feet. A short, low-bouncing underhand serve forces them to hit up on the ball, producing a weak return.

### In Windy Conditions
A strong headwind can turn a hard-hit flat serve into a short ball. An underhand serve with backspin stays low and controlled in the wind, making it a more reliable option.

### As a Surprise Weapon
If the returner has settled into a rhythm anticipating a hard serve, an occasional underhand serve disrupts their timing and forces them to adjust their positioning.

### When Protecting a Lead
At 40-0 or match point, an underhand serve can be a high-percentage option that reduces the chance of a double fault while still forcing the returner to generate their own pace.

## Mechanical Execution

### Grip and Stance
Use a continental or eastern forehand grip. Stand at the baseline in a standard serving stance, facing slightly sideways.

### The Motion
The underhand serve motion is fundamentally a drop shot from the service line:
1. Hold the ball in your non-dominant hand at waist height
2. Swing the racket from high to low in a smooth, underhand motion
3. Contact the ball at or below waist height, hitting it with a slight downward or backward motion
4. The racket face should be open (angled upward) to create backspin or a flat trajectory

### Key Mechanical Points
- **Contact at or below waist height**: The rules require the ball be hit before it reaches waist level
- **Smooth motion**: No jerky or deceptive movements that could be confused with a lets
- **Follow through forward**: The racket continues in the direction of the target after contact
- **Backspin or flat**: A slight backspin keeps the ball low after the bounce; a flat trajectory keeps it shallow

## Variations of the Underhand Serve

### Topspin Underhand
Brush up on the ball to create topspin that causes it to kick up after the bounce. This is harder to execute but creates a more aggressive return for the returner to handle.

### Slice Underhand
Brush across the ball to create sidespin that curves away from the returner. Effective for pulling wide on the deuce court.

### Backspin/Chop Underhand
Hit down and under the ball to create heavy backspin that stays low after the bounce. This is the most common variation because it is the most reliable.

## Rules and Etiquette

### ITF Rules
The underhand serve is entirely legal under ITF rules. The server must:
- Toss or release the ball by hand
- Strike the ball before it hits the ground
- The serve must land in the correct service box

### Etiquette Considerations
While legal, the underhand serve can be seen as disrespectful in certain contexts:
- **Junior tennis**: Many coaches discourage underhand serves in junior competition to encourage development of the overhand serve
- **Recreational play**: The serve may be perceived as gamesmanship if used excessively
- **Professional tennis**: More accepted at the professional level; players like Nick Kyrgios and Daniil Medvedev have used it effectively

## Common Underhand Serve Faults

### Tossing Too High
If the ball is tossed above waist height before contact, it is a fault. The ball should be held low and released with minimal upward motion.

### Hitting Into the Net
Without sufficient pace or the correct trajectory, the underhand serve may not clear the net. Practice the required trajectory before using in competition.

### Telegraphing the Serve
If the server changes their stance or motion noticeably before an underhand serve, the returner has time to adjust. The setup should look identical to the overhand serve until the last moment.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Serve lands too far into court | Excessive pace or trajectory | Returner can easily attack | Reduce swing speed; practice trajectory control |
| Serve hits net | Insufficient height at contact | Fault or weak serve | Height target drills; open racket face adjustment |
| Returner easily attacks underhand serve | Predictable timing or placement | Lost advantage | Vary placement; improve disguise of serve |
| Server telegraphs underhand intent | Visible change in stance or motion | Returner adjusts positioning | Disguise drills; identical setup practice |
| Inconsistent contact point | Variable toss height | Faults or weak serves | Toss consistency drills; contact point exercises |

## Practical Application: "The Shallow Target"

Place a target towel 6 feet inside the service line in the service box. Practice 20 underhand serves aiming to land the ball on or just beyond the towel. This develops the feel for pace and trajectory needed to keep the underhand serve shallow enough to pull the returner forward without giving them an easy attack ball.
