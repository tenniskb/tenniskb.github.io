# ART-950: Ankle Injury Prevention — Balance Training and Bracing

## Executive Summary

Ankle sprains are the most common acute injury in tennis, accounting for 15-30% of all tennis injuries. The sport's demands — rapid direction changes, lateral movements, split steps, and the occasional misstep on an opponent's foot or an uneven court surface — create a perfect storm for ankle inversion sprains. Once a player has sprained their ankle, the risk of re-injury increases by 2-5 times, creating a cycle of chronic instability and recurrent sprains. Breaking this cycle requires a multi-faceted approach: proprioceptive training to restore the neuromuscular control that is disrupted by injury, appropriate external support (bracing or taping) when indicated, and footwear optimization to reduce the risk of initial injury. This article provides a complete, evidence-based protocol for ankle injury prevention in tennis.

## Understanding Ankle Sprains in Tennis

The ankle is a complex joint that relies on both passive structures (ligaments, joint capsule) and active structures (muscles, tendons, and the proprioceptive system) to maintain stability during dynamic movement. An ankle sprain occurs when the foot inverts (rolls inward) beyond its normal range of motion, stretching or tearing the lateral ligaments — most commonly the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL).

In tennis, the most common mechanisms of ankle sprain include:

- **Lateral movement**: The foot inverts during a push-off for a wide ball, particularly when the player is off-balance or fatigued.
- **Landing on an opponent's foot**: When approaching the net, a player may land on an opponent's foot, causing the ankle to invert suddenly.
- **Uneven court surface**: A slight irregularity in the court surface can cause the foot to roll.
- **Split step misstep**: The split step, performed as the opponent strikes the ball, can place the ankle in a vulnerable position if the landing is awkward.

### The Proprioceptive Deficit

After an ankle sprain, the proprioceptive system — the sensory receptors in the ligaments, tendons, and muscles that provide the brain with information about joint position — is damaged. This creates a deficit in neuromuscular control: the muscles that should activate to prevent inversion (the peroneals) are slower and less effective, increasing the risk of re-injury. This proprioceptive deficit is the primary reason that previous ankle sprain is the strongest predictor of future sprain.

## Proprioceptive Training

Proprioceptive training (also called balance training or neuromuscular training) is the most evidence-based intervention for preventing ankle sprains. Multiple meta-analyses have shown that proprioceptive training reduces the risk of ankle sprains by 30-50% in sports with high ankle injury rates.

### The Progression of Proprioceptive Training

Proprioceptive training should progress from simple to complex, from stable to unstable, and from predictable to unpredictable:

**Level 1: Static Balance (Weeks 1-2)**
- **Single-leg stance**: Stand on one leg for 30-60 seconds. Focus on maintaining a level pelvis and a stable ankle.
- **Single-leg stance with eyes closed**: Close the eyes to remove visual input, forcing the proprioceptive system to work harder.
- **Single-leg stance on a folded towel**: Stand on a folded towel or a foam pad to create an unstable surface.

**Level 2: Dynamic Balance (Weeks 3-4)**
- **Single-leg reach**: Stand on one leg and reach the non-standing leg in multiple directions (forward, backward, lateral, diagonal), touching the floor and returning to center.
- **Single-leg balance with arm movement**: Stand on one leg while performing arm movements (e.g., throwing a ball against a wall) to add a cognitive and upper-body challenge.
- **Tandem stance**: Stand with one foot directly in front of the other (heel to toe), holding for 30 seconds. Progress to tandem walking.

**Level 3: Reactive Balance (Weeks 5-6)**
- **Single-leg balance with perturbation**: Stand on one leg while a partner gently pushes you in random directions, forcing the ankle to react and stabilize.
- **Single-leg hop and hold**: Hop on one leg and land, holding the landing for 3-5 seconds. Focus on a soft, controlled landing.
- **Star excursion balance test**: Stand on one leg and reach the non-standing leg to touch the floor at multiple points around the body (anterior, posterior, lateral, medial, and diagonals).

**Level 4: Sport-Specific Balance (Weeks 7+)**
- **Single-leg balance during tennis movements**: Perform shadow swings, split steps, and lateral movements while maintaining balance on one leg.
- **Reactive agility drills**: React to a partner's direction or a ball drop, performing a movement that challenges ankle stability.
- **On-court balance drills**: Perform tennis-specific movements (split steps, lateral shuffles, direction changes) on an unstable surface (e.g., a balance pad or a Bosu ball).

### Programming Proprioceptive Training

- **Frequency**: 3-5 times per week.
- **Duration**: 10-15 minutes per session.
- **Volume**: 2-3 sets of 30-60 seconds per exercise, or 2-3 sets of 8-12 repetitions for dynamic exercises.
- **Progression**: Move to the next level when the current level can be performed with good control and no pain.

## Bracing vs. Taping

External support (bracing or taping) is commonly used to prevent ankle sprains, particularly in players with a history of injury. The evidence for both approaches:

### Ankle Bracing

- **Evidence**: Multiple studies have shown that ankle braces reduce the risk of ankle sprains by 50-70% in athletes with a history of injury. The effect is most pronounced in sports with high ankle injury rates.
- **Types**: Semi-rigid braces (e.g., ASO, Aircast) provide the most support and are the most effective for prevention. Lace-up braces provide moderate support. Elastic sleeves provide minimal support.
- **Advantages**: Easy to apply, consistent support, reusable, and does not require a trained professional to apply.
- **Disadvantages**: Can be bulky, may affect comfort and movement, and may lose effectiveness over time as the brace wears out.

### Ankle Taping

- **Evidence**: Taping also reduces the risk of ankle sprains, but the evidence is slightly less consistent than for bracing. The effectiveness of taping depends heavily on the skill of the person applying it and the type of tape used.
- **Advantages**: Customizable to the individual, less bulky than a brace, and can be combined with other techniques (e.g., heel locks, figure-eights).
- **Disadvantages**: Requires a trained professional to apply, loses effectiveness after 20-30 minutes of activity, can cause skin irritation, and is more expensive over time than bracing.

### Recommendation

For players with a history of ankle sprain, semi-rigid bracing is the preferred option for prevention because it is more consistent, more cost-effective, and easier to apply than taping. Taping may be preferred for players who find braces uncomfortable or who need a more customized fit. For players without a history of ankle injury, bracing or taping is generally not necessary if proprioceptive training is performed consistently.

## Footwear Considerations

Footwear plays a critical role in ankle injury prevention. The right shoe can reduce the risk of ankle sprains by providing stability, traction, and support.

### Key Features of Tennis Shoes for Ankle Stability

- **Lateral support**: Tennis involves frequent lateral movements, so the shoe must provide adequate lateral support to prevent the foot from rolling outward (which can stress the ankle). Look for shoes with a firm heel counter and a stable midsole.
- **Outsole pattern**: A herringbone or modified herringbone outsole provides the best traction on hard courts, reducing the risk of slipping and rolling the ankle. On clay, a full herringbone pattern is preferred.
- **Ankle collar**: A padded ankle collar provides additional support and comfort, particularly for players with a history of ankle injury.
- **Fit**: The shoe should fit snugly in the midfoot and heel, with enough room in the toe box to prevent the foot from sliding forward. A shoe that is too loose allows the foot to move inside the shoe, increasing the risk of ankle sprains.
- **Cushioning**: Adequate cushioning in the heel and forefoot reduces the impact forces transmitted to the ankle during landing and direction changes.

### When to Replace Tennis Shoes

Tennis shoes should be replaced every 3-6 months for regular players (3-4 times per week) or when the outsole shows significant wear. Worn-out shoes lose their traction and cushioning, increasing the risk of ankle sprains and other injuries.

## The Complete Ankle Prevention Program

A comprehensive ankle injury prevention program for tennis players includes:

1. **Proprioceptive training** (10-15 minutes, 3-5 times per week): Progress through the four levels of balance training.
2. **Strengthening** (10 minutes, 2-3 times per week): Eccentric calf raises, resistance band dorsiflexion and eversion, and toe curls.
3. **Stretching** (5 minutes, daily): Calf stretches (gastrocnemius and soleus) and ankle dorsiflexion stretches.
4. **Footwear optimization**: Choose shoes with adequate lateral support, traction, and cushioning. Replace shoes regularly.
5. **External support** (if indicated): Use a semi-rigid ankle brace for players with a history of ankle sprain.
6. **Court awareness**: Be aware of court conditions (wet spots, loose clay, uneven surfaces) and adjust movement accordingly.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player has sprained the same ankle multiple times | Chronic ankle instability, proprioceptive deficit | Recurring sprains, reduced performance | Implement progressive proprioceptive training program; consider semi-rigid bracing |
| Player's ankle feels unstable during lateral movements | Insufficient peroneal strength and proprioceptive control | Increased sprain risk, compensatory movement patterns | Add resistance band eversion exercises and single-leg balance training |
| Player's shoes are worn out | Loss of traction and cushioning | Increased slip and roll risk | Replace shoes every 3-6 months; choose shoes with adequate lateral support |
| Player's calf muscles are tight | Limited ankle dorsiflexion | Increased Achilles and ankle injury risk | Add daily calf stretching (gastrocnemius and soleus) and ankle dorsiflexion mobilizations |
| Player's balance is poor on the previously injured ankle | Proprioceptive deficit from previous injury | Increased re-injury risk | Progress through the four levels of proprioceptive training; emphasize the injured side |
| Player experiences ankle pain during play | Tendinopathy or impingement | Chronic pain, reduced performance | Reduce playing volume; consult physical therapist for assessment and treatment |

## Practical Application: "The 10-Minute Ankle Prevention Routine"

Perform this routine 3-5 times per week to maintain ankle stability and prevent sprains:

1. **Single-leg stance** — 3 × 30 seconds per leg (progress to eyes closed)
2. **Single-leg reach** — 2 × 8 reaches per leg (forward, backward, lateral, diagonal)
3. **Resistance band eversion** — 2 × 15 per side (strengthens the peroneals)
4. **Eccentric calf raise** — 2 × 12 per side (3-5 second lowering phase)
5. **Tandem stance** — 2 × 30 seconds per leg (heel to toe)
6. **Calf stretch** — 30 seconds per side (gastrocnemius and soleus)

This routine takes approximately 10 minutes and requires only a resistance band. It is the most cost-effective ankle injury prevention tool available. Combined with proper footwear and, when indicated, external support, it can break the cycle of recurrent ankle sprains and keep players on court, moving confidently, and competing at their best. The ankle is the foundation of every movement in tennis — protect it, and it will carry you through a long and successful career.
