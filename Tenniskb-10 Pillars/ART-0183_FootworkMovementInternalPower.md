# ART-0183: Center of Mass Vector Alignment During Lateral Recovery Movement

## Executive Summary

A recovery is not complete when the feet arrive at center — it is complete when the center of mass (COM) arrives there under control. Players who generate aggressive lateral velocity without aligning the COM's deceleration curve to the target position routinely overshoot or arrive with residual momentum that must be killed in a last-millisecond braking step, adding 100-250 ms before the next stroke can begin. This article maps the COM trajectory during lateral recovery, defines the lean-angle window that prevents overshoot, and explains why the goal is not maximum speed but controlled arrival.

## The COM Path During Lateral Recovery

During a lateral recovery, the COM follows a roughly parabolic trajectory in the frontal plane: it rises slightly as the player loads the push leg, moves laterally during the push-and-glide phase, and must settle back to its neutral height over the ready position at the end of the recovery. The shape of this trajectory determines whether the recovery ends in a controlled ready position or an uncontrolled drift.

The critical variable is the **COM lateral velocity at the moment the feet reach the target position**. Ideally, this velocity should be near zero — the COM arrives and stops in the same instant. In practice, most recreational players arrive with 0.5-1.5 m/s of residual lateral velocity, which they dissipate through a braking step, a stutter, or a momentary loss of balance before the next stroke. Each of these corrections costs time:

- **Braking step:** 80-150 ms (an extra foot plant to absorb momentum).
- **Stutter:** 50-100 ms (a rapid micro-shuffle to arrest drift).
- **Balance correction:** 100-250 ms (reaching for stability with an arm or a wider base).

Over a match, players who consistently arrive with residual velocity lose roughly 2-4 seconds per set in cumulative braking time — equivalent to gifting the opponent a game.

## The Lean-Angle Window

The primary control mechanism for COM alignment is the **trunk lean angle** during the recovery's middle phase. The trunk lean shifts the COM in the recovery direction, generating lateral velocity. But the lean must be reversed (or at least neutralized) before the COM reaches the target, or the player will overshoot.

The effective lean angle during recovery is roughly 5-15° from vertical, depending on the recovery distance:

- **Short recovery (under 1.5 m):** 3-8° lean. The push is gentle and brief; the COM does not need much acceleration. Over-leaning here produces an immediate overshoot.
- **Medium recovery (1.5-3.0 m):** 8-15° lean. The push is sustained over 2-3 steps; the lean must be maintained through the first half of the recovery and actively reversed (trunk moves back toward vertical or even slightly past vertical) in the second half.
- **Long recovery (beyond 3.0 m):** 10-15° lean with active deceleration. On extreme-width recoveries, the player may use 3-4 steps, and the lean must be managed in three phases: acceleration (lean in), cruise (lean constant), and deceleration (lean reversed).

The **deceleration phase** is the most commonly neglected. Players initiate the lean, accelerate laterally, and then "let go" — allowing the COM to coast to a stop. Coasting is unreliable: small variations in court friction, shoe grip, or fatigue level mean the coasting distance varies by 20-50 cm per repetition. Active deceleration — deliberately shifting the trunk back toward vertical starting roughly 0.5-1.0 m before the target — makes the arrival position repeatable within 10-15 cm.

## Overshoot and Undershoot Geometries

**Overshoot** occurs when the COM arrives at the target position with positive residual velocity — the player has pushed too hard or decelerated too late. The visible signature is a "stutter-step" or a visible arm reach for balance at the end of the recovery. Overshoot is more common in players who associate recovery urgency with maximum effort: they push as hard as possible and arrive late (in position, not in time) because they must brake.

**Undershoot** occurs when the COM velocity reaches zero before the target position — the player decelerates too early or pushes too softly. The visible signature is a "hurry-up" step at the end: a short, fast shuffle to cover the remaining 20-50 cm. Undershoot is more common in cautious players or players with poor push-off power who are afraid of overshooting.

Both errors are equally costly in time. Overshoot requires braking time; undershoot requires a catch-up step. The ideal recovery brings the COM to zero velocity within 15 cm of the target position, with no visible correction.

## The Kình Principle: Rooted Arrival

In Tai Chi and internal martial arts, the concept of "rootedness" (zhōng dìng) describes a state in which the body's mass is settled over its base of support with no residual motion — the COM is still, the joints are loaded but not locked, and the next movement can begin instantly from this stable platform. This is exactly the recovery ideal: not "arrive and then get ready," but "arrive already ready."

The rooted arrival requires three conditions:

1. **COM velocity near zero at the target.** Achieved through active deceleration, not coasting.
2. **COM height at or below the neutral ready-position height.** If the COM is still elevated (from the push phase's vertical component), the player must lower it, adding 50-100 ms.
3. **Base of support established.** Both feet must be on the ground and roughly shoulder-width apart before the COM settles. A player who arrives on one foot must still load the second foot, adding 80-120 ms.

When all three conditions are met, the player is ready to move again within 30-50 ms of arrival — effectively, the recovery and the next preparation are merged, just as the hit and recovery are merged in ART-0181.

## Training the COM Path

1. **Wall-lean calibration.** Stand 30 cm from a wall. Lean toward it at 5°, 10°, 15°, and 20° and feel the lateral force at each angle. This builds the proprioceptive map for lean-angle selection during recovery.

2. **Target-zone landings.** Mark a 30 cm × 30 cm target zone on the court with tape. After a recovery, the player must stop with both feet inside the zone. If they overshoot or undershoot, the lean angle or deceleration timing was wrong. Run 20 recoveries and track the hit rate.

3. **Deceleration cue drill.** Coach feeds a ball wide; the player recovers. The coach calls "NOW" at the midpoint of the recovery — the player must begin active deceleration (trunk back toward vertical) at that cue. Early deceleration produces undershoot; late deceleration produces overshoot. The coach adjusts the cue timing to find the player's optimal deceleration point.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Stutter-step at end of recovery | Overshoot; residual COM velocity at target | 80-150 ms braking delay before next stroke | Target-zone landing drill; cue "soften one step earlier" |
| Short shuffle step to reach center | Undershoot; deceleration initiated too early | 50-100 ms catch-up delay | Wall-lean calibration; practice maintaining lean 1-2 steps longer |
| Visible arm reach for balance at arrival | COM displaced outside base of support | 100-250 ms balance correction | Narrow the lean angle by 2-3°; practice "still hands" during recovery |
| COM visibly elevated at arrival | Push-off too vertical; residual upward velocity | 50-100 ms settling time before next movement | Cue "push sideways, not up"; monitor COM height on side-on video |
| Recovery rhythm is two-phase (push, then stop) | Coasting rather than active deceleration | Arrival point varies 20-50 cm per repetition | Deceleration cue drill; practice "brake with the trunk" |
| Player consistently lands on one foot | Base of support not established at arrival | 80-120 ms to load second foot | "Two-feet-settled" cue; static balance holds on two-feet arrival position |

## Practical Application: "Arrive Rooted"

Use the target-zone landing drill at the start of the next session. Mark a 30 cm zone at the recovery target. Hit 10 balls with moderate-width feeds and score how many recoveries end with both feet in the zone on the first landing. If the score is below 5/10, the player's COM control is the limiting factor — not their speed, not their fitness, but their ability to arrive still. Run the deceleration cue drill for 10 minutes, then re-score. Players who improve to 7/10 or higher within a session feel the immediate benefit: more time, less chaos, and the sense that recovery is under control rather than a scramble.
