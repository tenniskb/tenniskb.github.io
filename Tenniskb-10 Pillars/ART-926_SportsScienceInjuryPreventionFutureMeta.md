# ART-926: Asthma and Tennis — Exercise-Induced Bronchoconstriction

## Executive Summary
Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) affects approximately 20% of athletes and up to 90% of individuals with asthma. In tennis, the intermittent high-intensity nature of the sport can trigger bronchoconstriction, particularly in cold, dry, or polluted environments. Proper management, including pre-exercise bronchodilator use, warm-up protocols, and environmental awareness, allows players with asthma to compete at the highest levels without limitation.

## Exercise-Induced Bronchoconstriction
EIB is a narrowing of the airways that occurs during or after exercise, typically beginning 5-10 minutes after the onset of exercise and peaking at 10-15 minutes after exercise cessation. The mechanism involves the inhalation of large volumes of cool, dry air during exercise, which causes osmotic changes in the airway lining and triggers the release of inflammatory mediators from mast cells. This leads to bronchial smooth muscle contraction, airway narrowing, and symptoms including wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and decreased performance. In tennis, the intermittent nature of the sport, with periods of high-intensity movement followed by rest, can make EIB particularly noticeable during changeovers and between points.

## The Refractory Period
A well-established phenomenon in EIB is the refractory period, during which the airways are less responsive to further exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. This period typically lasts 2-4 hours after an initial bout of exercise. The refractory period can be induced by a warm-up protocol consisting of 6-8 high-intensity intervals (85-90% of maximum heart rate) separated by short rest periods. This protocol depletes mast cell mediators, reducing the subsequent bronchoconstrictive response. For tennis players, a warm-up that includes 8-10 minutes of high-intensity activity (such as sprint intervals or jumping jacks) before match play can induce the refractory period and reduce EIB symptoms during competition.

## Pre-Exercise Bronchodilator Use
Short-acting beta-2 agonists (SABAs), such as albuterol (salbutamol), are the most commonly used pre-exercise medications for EIB. These medications relax bronchial smooth muscle within 15-20 minutes of inhalation and provide protection for 2-4 hours. The standard dose is 2 puffs (90 mcg per puff) administered 15-20 minutes before exercise. Players with persistent EIB despite SABA use may benefit from inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), which reduce airway inflammation and improve overall asthma control. Leukotriene receptor antagonists, such as montelukast, provide an alternative for players who do not respond adequately to SABAs. All medications should be used under medical supervision, and players should carry their inhaler courtside at all times.

## Environmental Triggers
Environmental conditions significantly affect EIB in tennis. Cold, dry air is the most potent trigger, as it increases the osmotic stress on the airways. Players competing in cold climates should consider wearing a face mask or scarf to warm and humidify inhaled air. Air pollution, including ozone and particulate matter, can trigger bronchoconstriction and should be monitored on competition days. High pollen counts can trigger allergic asthma in sensitized individuals. Indoor courts with poor ventilation may expose players to respiratory irritants from cleaning chemicals or floor surfaces. Players should be aware of their specific triggers and take appropriate precautions.

## Asthma Action Plan
Every tennis player with asthma should have a written asthma action plan developed with their physician. The plan should include: daily controller medication (if prescribed), pre-exercise medication protocol, recognition of worsening symptoms, and steps to take during an acute asthma exacerbation. The plan should be shared with coaches and training partners so they can assist during an emergency. Players should also carry a medical identification card or wear a medical alert bracelet.

## Asthma and Elite Tennis
Asthma does not preclude elite tennis performance. Numerous professional players have competed at the highest levels while managing asthma, including players who have won Grand Slam titles. The key is proper medication management, adherence to the asthma action plan, and awareness of environmental triggers. Players should work with their physician to optimize their medication regimen and ensure that all medications comply with World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) regulations. SABAs are permitted in competition with a Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) in some cases.

## Warm-Up Protocol for EIB
An effective warm-up protocol for EIB consists of three components. First, a general warm-up of 5-10 minutes of light aerobic activity increases body temperature and prepares the cardiovascular system. Second, a series of 6-8 high-intensity intervals (30 seconds at 85-90% maximum heart rate) with 30-60 seconds of rest between intervals induces the refractory period. Third, dynamic stretching and sport-specific movements prepare the tennis-specific muscle groups. The entire warm-up should last 15-20 minutes and be completed within 30 minutes of match play. Players should time their SABA inhalation 15-20 minutes before the warm-up begins.

## Recognizing an Acute Exacerbation
Despite preventive measures, acute asthma exacerbations can occur during tennis. Signs include persistent coughing, wheezing, chest tightness, shortness of breath that does not resolve with rest, and difficulty speaking in full sentences. The player should stop playing immediately, use their SABA inhaler (2 puffs), and sit upright. If symptoms do not improve within 15-20 minutes, a second dose may be administered. If symptoms persist despite two doses, the player should seek emergency medical attention. Coaches and training partners should be trained to recognize the signs of an acute exacerbation and know how to assist.

## Practical Application: "Breathe Easy, Play Strong"
If you have asthma, develop a written asthma action plan with your physician and share it with your coach and training partners. Always carry your inhaler courtside and use it 15-20 minutes before match play as prescribed. Incorporate a high-intensity interval warm-up to induce the refractory period before competition. Be aware of environmental triggers and take precautions on cold, dry, or high-pollution days. Remember: asthma is a manageable condition, not a limitation. With proper management, you can compete at any level and achieve your full potential.
