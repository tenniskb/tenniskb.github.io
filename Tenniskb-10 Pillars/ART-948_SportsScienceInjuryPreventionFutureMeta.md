# ART-948: Shoulder Prehabilitation for Tennis — Rotator Cuff and Scapular Program

## Executive Summary

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and this mobility comes at the cost of stability. In tennis, where the shoulder is subjected to extreme ranges of motion at high velocities (the serve can reach internal rotation velocities of 7,000 degrees per second), the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers must work overtime to keep the joint centered and functional. Shoulder injuries — including rotator cuff tendinopathy, impingement, and labral tears — are among the most common and most debilitating injuries in tennis. A structured prehabilitation program targeting the rotator cuff, scapular stabilizers, and shoulder mobility can reduce the risk of these injuries and extend a player's career. The program described here adapts the classic "Thrower's Ten" program (developed for baseball pitchers) for the specific demands of tennis.

## Shoulder Biomechanics in Tennis

The tennis serve is one of the most demanding movements in all of sports. The shoulder goes through the following phases:

1. **Wind-up**: The shoulder externally rotates to approximately 150-170 degrees, placing the anterior capsule and the rotator cuff under significant stress.
2. **Cocking**: The shoulder reaches maximum external rotation, and the scapula retracts and rotates upward.
3. **Acceleration**: The shoulder internally rotates at velocities of 6,000-7,000 degrees per second, generating the force that accelerates the racket head.
4. **Deceleration**: After ball contact, the shoulder decelerates rapidly, with the posterior rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers absorbing enormous eccentric loads.
5. **Follow-through**: The shoulder continues to internally rotate and adduct, with the arm finishing across the body.

The repetitive nature of this motion (a professional player serves 50-100 times per match, plus additional overheads and high forehands) creates cumulative stress on the rotator cuff, labrum, and scapular stabilizers. Over time, this stress can lead to tendinopathy, impingement, and labral tears.

## The Thrower's Ten Program Adapted for Tennis

The Thrower's Ten program, developed by Kevin Wilk and colleagues, is a set of 10 exercises designed to strengthen the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers in overhead athletes. The program has been adapted here for tennis players, with modifications to address the specific demands of the serve, overhead, and groundstrokes.

### Exercise 1: External Rotation at 0 Degrees Abduction
- **Position**: Standing, elbow at the side, bent at 90 degrees, holding a resistance band or light dumbbell.
- **Movement**: Rotate the forearm outward (external rotation) against the resistance.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 15 per side.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the infraspinatus and teres minor, the primary external rotators.

### Exercise 2: Internal Rotation at 0 Degrees Abduction
- **Position**: Same as above.
- **Movement**: Rotate the forearm inward (internal rotation) against the resistance.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 15 per side.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the subscapularis, the primary internal rotator.

### Exercise 3: External Rotation at 90 Degrees Abduction
- **Position**: Standing, arm abducted to 90 degrees (shoulder height), elbow bent at 90 degrees.
- **Movement**: Rotate the forearm upward (external rotation) against the resistance.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 15 per side.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the external rotators in the position of maximum stress during the serve.

### Exercise 4: Internal Rotation at 90 Degrees Abduction
- **Position**: Same as above.
- **Movement**: Rotate the forearm downward (internal rotation) against the resistance.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 15 per side.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the internal rotators in the position of maximum stress during the serve.

### Exercise 5: Scapular Setting (Retraction and Depression)
- **Position**: Standing, arms at the sides.
- **Movement**: Squeeze the shoulder blades together (retraction) and downward (depression), holding for 5 seconds.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 10.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the rhomboids and lower trapezius, which stabilize the scapula during the serve.

### Exercise 6: Prone Y
- **Position**: Lying face-down on a bench, arm hanging off the side, thumb pointing up.
- **Movement**: Lift the arm upward and outward at a 45-degree angle (forming a "Y" with the body).
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 10.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the lower trapezius and serratus anterior, which control scapular upward rotation.

### Exercise 7: Prone T
- **Position**: Lying face-down, arm hanging off the side, thumb pointing up.
- **Movement**: Lift the arm straight out to the side (forming a "T" with the body).
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 10.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the middle trapezius and rhomboids, which control scapular retraction.

### Exercise 8: Prone W
- **Position**: Lying face-down, arm hanging off the side, elbow bent at 90 degrees.
- **Movement**: Lift the arm and rotate the forearm upward (forming a "W" with the arm).
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 10.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the posterior rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers in a functional position.

### Exercise 9: Prone L (External Rotation at 90/90)
- **Position**: Lying face-down, arm abducted to 90 degrees, elbow bent at 90 degrees, hanging off the side of the bench.
- **Movement**: Rotate the forearm upward (external rotation) against gravity or a light dumbbell.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 10.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the external rotators in the 90/90 position, the most vulnerable position for the shoulder during the serve.

### Exercise 10: Serratus Anterior Punch
- **Position**: Standing, holding a resistance band or light dumbbell, arm extended forward at shoulder height.
- **Movement**: Punch the arm forward, protracting the scapula (moving the shoulder blade away from the spine).
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 × 15.
- **Purpose**: Strengthens the serratus anterior, which controls scapular protraction and upward rotation.

## Shoulder Mobility Work

Strength without mobility is a recipe for injury. The shoulder prehabilitation program must include mobility work to maintain the range of motion required for the serve and overhead:

### Sleeper Stretch
- **Position**: Lying on the side, shoulder abducted to 90 degrees, elbow bent at 90 degrees.
- **Movement**: Use the opposite hand to gently push the forearm toward the floor, stretching the posterior shoulder.
- **Duration**: 30 seconds, 2-3 repetitions per side.
- **Purpose**: Improves internal rotation range of motion, which is often limited in overhead athletes.

### Cross-Body Shoulder Stretch
- **Position**: Standing, arm across the chest.
- **Movement**: Use the opposite hand to pull the arm closer to the chest, stretching the posterior shoulder.
- **Duration**: 30 seconds, 2-3 repetitions per side.
- **Purpose**: Improves horizontal adduction range of motion.

### Thoracic Spine Mobility
- **Position**: Foam roller placed horizontally across the upper back.
- **Movement**: Roll from the mid-back to the upper back, pausing at tight spots.
- **Duration**: 60 seconds.
- **Purpose**: Improves thoracic extension and rotation, which reduce the compensatory stress on the shoulder.

### Wall Slides
- **Position**: Standing with the back against a wall, arms in a "goalpost" position (elbows bent at 90 degrees, upper arms at shoulder height).
- **Movement**: Slide the arms up the wall, keeping the back of the hands and elbows in contact with the wall.
- **Sets/Reps**: 2 × 10.
- **Purpose**: Improves shoulder flexion range of motion and scapular upward rotation.

## Programming the Shoulder Prehab Program

The shoulder prehabilitation program should be performed 3-4 times per week, with sessions of 15-20 minutes. The program can be performed as a standalone session or integrated into the warm-up before tennis practice or strength training.

**Progression**:
- **Weeks 1-2**: Use a light resistance band (yellow or red) for all band exercises. Focus on proper form and muscle activation.
- **Weeks 3-4**: Progress to a medium resistance band (green or blue). Add a light dumbbell (0.5-1 kg) for prone exercises.
- **Weeks 5+**: Progress to a heavier band or dumbbell as strength improves. Continue the program indefinitely as maintenance.

**Rest**: 30-60 seconds between sets. The exercises should be performed with controlled, deliberate movements — not momentum.

## Common Shoulder Prehab Mistakes

- **Neglecting the scapula**: Many players focus exclusively on the rotator cuff and ignore the scapular stabilizers. Scapular dysfunction is a primary contributor to shoulder impingement and rotator cuff tendinopathy.
- **Overloading too quickly**: The rotator cuff muscles are small and fatigue quickly. Using too much resistance too soon leads to compensatory movement patterns and reduces the effectiveness of the program.
- **Ignoring mobility**: Strength without mobility creates a shoulder that is strong but restricted, increasing the risk of impingement.
- **Inconsistency**: The shoulder prehab program must be performed consistently (3-4 times per week) to be effective. Sporadic effort produces sporadic results.
- **Pain during exercises**: Some discomfort is acceptable, but sharp or increasing pain is a sign to reduce the load or consult a professional.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Player's shoulder pain during serving | Rotator cuff tendinopathy or impingement | Reduced serve velocity, potential labral tear | Implement Thrower's Ten program; reduce serving volume; consult physical therapist |
| Player's shoulder internal rotation is limited | Posterior capsule tightness | Increased impingement risk, altered serve mechanics | Add sleeper stretches and cross-body stretches daily |
| Player's scapula wings during arm elevation | Serratus anterior or lower trapezius weakness | Scapular dyskinesis, impingement risk | Add prone Y-T-W exercises and serratus anterior punches |
| Player's serve velocity has dropped | Shoulder pain or weakness | Reduced serve effectiveness, compensatory patterns | Implement full shoulder prehab program; review serving technique |
| Player's shoulder feels unstable | Labral tear or rotator cuff insufficiency | Apprehension, reduced performance | Refer to orthopedic specialist for evaluation; modify training load |
| Player's shoulder prehab program is inconsistent | Lack of adherence | No improvement in shoulder health | Schedule prehab sessions; integrate into warm-up routine; track adherence |

## Practical Application: "The 15-Minute Shoulder Prehab Session"

Perform this session 3-4 times per week. It combines the Thrower's Ten exercises with mobility work for a comprehensive shoulder prehab program.

**Warm-up (3 minutes)**:
- Arm circles (small to large): 10 each direction
- Wall slides: 2 × 10
- Sleeper stretch: 30 seconds per side

**Thrower's Ten (10 minutes)**:
- External rotation at 0°: 2 × 15 per side
- Internal rotation at 0°: 2 × 15 per side
- External rotation at 90°: 2 × 15 per side
- Prone Y-T-W: 2 × 8 each position
- Prone L: 2 × 10 per side
- Serratus anterior punch: 2 × 15

**Cool-down (2 minutes)**:
- Cross-body shoulder stretch: 30 seconds per side
- Thoracic spine foam rolling: 60 seconds

This session takes approximately 15 minutes and requires only a resistance band and a mat. It is the most important investment a tennis player can make in their long-term shoulder health. The players who serve freely at 35 are the ones who took care of their shoulders at 20.
