# ART-0623: The Serve-and-Volley Game — Complete Net-Rushing System

## Executive Summary

The serve-and-volley is an aggressive strategy where the server follows their serve to the net, looking to finish the point with a volley or overhead. It requires a strong serve, quick transition footwork, reliable volley technique, and the confidence to handle passing shots and lobs. While less common in the modern baseline-dominated game, the serve-and-volley remains a devastating weapon on fast surfaces and for players who master its demands.

## The Serve Component

A successful serve-and-volley begins with the serve itself. The serve must be deep and directed to the opponent's weaker side (typically the backhand) to force a weak return. Slice serves that pull the opponent wide and kick serves that push them behind the baseline are particularly effective. A weak serve that lands short invites an aggressive return that makes the net approach dangerous. The serve is the foundation — without it, the volley game cannot function.

## The Transition: From Serve to Net

After serving, the server must transition to the net with maximum speed while maintaining balance. The first step is a "split-push" — pushing off the serve's landing foot in the direction of the net. The feet take 3-4 quick steps forward, with the final step being a split step at the service line. The racket stays in front of the body, held with both hands, ready for the return. The transition must be continuous — no hesitation between the serve and the net rush.

## The First Volley

The first volley is hit from the service line or slightly inside it, on the return of serve. It is typically a punch volley or slice volley directed deep to the opponent's backhand corner. The goal is to force a weak reply that can be put away at the net. Do not attempt a winner on the first volley — focus on depth and placement. The first volley is a "setup" shot that creates the opportunity for the second volley (the put-away).

## The Second Volley

The second volley is the put-away. After the first volley forces a weak reply, close to the net (3-6 feet) and hit an aggressive punch volley to the open court. This is where points are won. The second volley must be decisive — hesitation allows the opponent to recover. Aim for the open court, the opponent's body, or a sharp angle. The second volley is the reward for a successful serve-and-volley sequence.

## Handling the Passing Shot

When the opponent hits a passing shot, the server must be ready to volley defensively. The key is to get the racket on the ball early — do not wait for it to come to you. Use a block volley or punch volley to redirect the passing shot deep, buying time to recover. If the passing shot is too hot to handle, play a defensive lob to reset the point. The goal is to stay alive in the point until you can hit an offensive volley.

## Handling the Lob

When the opponent lobs over your head, use the backward movement techniques described in ART-0618. Turn sideways, use the drop-step, and retreat for the overhead. If the lob is too deep, play a recovery lob of your own. A common error is staying at the net too long — retreat the moment you recognize the lob. Practice serve-and-volley followed by overheads until the sequence becomes automatic.

## Tactical Application

Use the serve-and-volley: (1) on fast surfaces (grass, fast hard courts) where the ball skids and stays low, (2) against baseline players who struggle with net-rushing, (3) to take time away from opponents who like to set up, (4) in pressure situations where you want to seize control, and (5) to change the rhythm of a baseline-dominated match. Do not use it: (1) on slow surfaces (clay) where the high bounce makes volleying difficult, (2) when your serve is weak, (3) against strong passers and lobbers, or (4) when you are tired (serve-and-volley demands explosive energy).

## The Chip-and-Charge

A variation of the serve-and-volley is the chip-and-charge — used when receiving serve. The returner hits a short backspin chip to the server's feet, then rushes the net. This is particularly effective against second serves. The chip must be low and short, and the charge must be rapid. The chip-and-charge turns the receiver from a passive retriever into an aggressive net rusher.

## Serve-and-Volley Drills

The "serve-volley-putaway" drill practices the full serve-and-volley sequence: serve, transition, first volley, second volley. The "serve-volley-overhead" drill adds overhead practice after the opponent lobs. The "serve-volley vs. passer" drill pits the server against a player hitting passing shots. The "serve-volley competition" game rewards points won with the serve-and-volley.

## Summary

The serve-and-volley is a complete aggressive system: strong serve, quick transition, deep first volley, and decisive second volley. It demands confidence, athleticism, and reliable technique. While the modern game has shifted toward baseline play, the serve-and-volley remains a devastating weapon for players who commit to mastering it.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| First volley lands short | Poor contact, weak punch | Attacking passing shot for opponent | Deepen first volley, aim for service line or deeper |
| Caught in no man's land | Stopping at service line | Easy passing shot | Continue to net after first volley |
| Overhead misses after lob | Poor tracking, rushing | Unforced errors | Point non-dominant arm at ball, take your time |
| Serve is too weak to approach | Short serve, no pace/kick | Aggressive return, dangerous approach | Improve serve quality before approaching |
| Cannot handle fast passing shots | Slow reaction, long backswing | Frame shots, errors | Shorten backswing, use block volley |

## Practical Application: "Serve, Rush, Volley, Win"

The serve-and-volley sequence is four steps: (1) hit a deep serve to the weakness, (2) rush the net with quick steps, (3) hit a deep first volley, (4) put away the second volley. Commit to the entire sequence — half-measures (approaching but not closing) leave you stranded in no man's land.
