# ART-403: Continental Grip for Backhand Slice

## Executive Summary
The Continental grip is the most effective grip for the backhand slice, naturally presenting an open racket face that skids through the ball to produce backspin. Its geometry aligns the racket face with the swing path, making slice production instinctive rather than forced. Mastery of this grip expands a player's defensive and transitional capabilities.

## Grip Geometry for Slice
The Continental grip places the base knuckle of the index finger on bevel 2, the same grip used for serves and volleys. This positions the racket face slightly tilted upward when the arm hangs naturally. The palm sits under the handle more than behind it, creating a natural open face angle.

For the two-handed slice, the bottom hand takes the Continental grip while the top hand may stay in an Eastern forehand grip or shift toward Continental as well. The top hand's position influences how much the slice penetrates versus how much it stays high.

## The Slice Swing Path
The backhand slice uses a high-to-low swing path, fundamentally different from the low-to-high path of a topspin drive. The racket starts above the contact point and descends through the ball, brushing the back and underneath. This descending path is what creates backspin.

The angle of descent controls slice character. A steeper descent (more vertical) produces more backspin but less forward momentum. A shallower descent (more horizontal) produces less spin but more pace and penetration. Players adjust the descent angle based on tactical needs: defensive slices use steeper angles for height and spin, offensive slices use shallower angles for drive and penetration.

## Contact Point and Racket Face
Contact for the slice occurs in front of the body, similar to a drive, but the racket face is tilted open approximately 30-45 degrees. The exact angle depends on how much height is needed over the net. More open face produces more height and spin; less open produces a flatter, more driving slice.

The ball contacts the strings on the upper portion of the string bed when producing an underspin slice. This is because the racket is descending through contact, so the upper strings meet the ball first. The follow-through continues downward and slightly forward, finishing around waist height for a standard slice or lower for a dropping slice.

## Slice Trajectory Control
The Continental grip allows the player to control slice trajectory through three variables: racket face angle at contact, swing path descent angle, and swing speed. A wide open face with a steep descent produces a high, looping slice that drops sharply. A slightly closed face with a shallow descent produces a flat, skidding slice that stays low.

The height of the ball at contact also affects trajectory. Slicing a ball at waist height produces a different trajectory than slicing a ball at shoulder height. Players must adjust their swing to account for incoming ball height, using body positioning (bending knees, stepping in) to bring the ball into the ideal contact zone.

## Defensive vs Offensive Slice
Defensive slice prioritizes height and spin to buy time for recovery. The swing path is steep, the racket face is wide open, and the follow-through is long and upward. The resulting shot clears the net with margin, bounces high, and forces the opponent to generate their own pace.

Offensive slice, sometimes called a "drive slice" or "cutting slice," uses a shallower path and less open face. The ball stays low, skids through the court, and takes time away from the opponent. This shot is effective on low balls and as a change of pace during rallies.

## Combining Slice with Other Shots
The Continental grip enables quick transitions to volleys and overheads, since these shots use the same grip. This makes it valuable at the net, where a player may face a drive, then a half-volley, then a volley, all requiring different grips if using a dedicated drive grip. The Continental grip eliminates grip changes in transitional situations.

On the baseline, the Continental grip for slice pairs well with a drive grip for topspin. Players who use different grips for each shot must practice the transition between them. Many recreational players keep the Continental grip for both slice and drive, accepting less topspin on drives in exchange for faster transitions.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix
| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Slice sails too long | Racket face too closed or path too flat | Ball goes out or is attackable | Open face more; steepen descent angle |
| Slice pops up too high | Racket face too open or path too steep | Easy overhead for opponent | Reduce open face; shallow the descent |
| No backspin, ball floats | Swing path level or ascending | Floaty, attackable ball | Emphasize high-to-low path; brush under ball |
| Slice lacks penetration | Swing speed too slow or contact too far back | Ball dies at opponent's feet | Accelerate through contact; move contact point forward |
| Wrist pain on slice | Wrist too loose or bent at contact | Inconsistent contact, potential injury | Stabilize wrist; check grip pressure |

## Practical Application: "The Skid Test"
Hit ten slice backhands over the net focusing on producing a low, skidding bounce. After each shot, note whether the ball stays below net height after the bounce. If it bounces high, the racket face is too open or the path is too steep. Adjust until at least seven of ten shots skid low, building the feel for an offensive slice.
