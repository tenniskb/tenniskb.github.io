# ART-0186: Energy Economy in Footwork — Minimizing Unnecessary Stride Count Per Point

## Executive Summary

Every stride a player takes that does not contribute to court positioning is energy borrowed from the later games of the match. Stride economy — the ratio of effective movement to total movement — separates players who finish strong from those who fade in the third set. This article quantifies stride counts for common rally patterns, identifies the redundant-step patterns that inflate the count, establishes efficiency benchmarks, and links stride economy to match-length endurance. The central principle: it is not how fast you move, but how few unnecessary movements you make.

## The Stride Budget

A tennis point lasting 8-12 shots requires roughly 25-50 strides of meaningful movement (sprint steps, crossover steps, split steps, and shuffle steps) plus an additional 5-15 strides of preparatory adjustment (small shuffles, stance resets, positional micro-corrections). The total stride budget for a point is therefore roughly 30-65 strides, depending on rally length and court coverage required.

Elite players operate near the lower end of this range — roughly 30-40 strides for a typical 8-shot rally — because their movement is efficient: each stride covers meaningful distance, and redundant steps are eliminated. Recreational players often operate near the upper end — 50-65 strides for the same rally — because they incorporate redundant steps (double stutters, over-shuffling, unnecessary directional reversals) that add 20-40% to the stride count without improving positioning.

The energy cost is substantial. Each redundant stride costs roughly 0.5-1.5 kcal of metabolic energy (depending on intensity and player mass), and an extra 20 redundant strides per point accumulates to roughly 10-30 kcal per point, or 300-900 kcal over a 30-point set. Over three sets, this is the energy equivalent of an entire extra set of tennis — energy that is not available for the decisive third set.

## Redundant-Step Patterns

Five specific patterns account for the majority of unnecessary strides:

**1. The double stutter.** The player initiates a push in one direction, hesitates (a micro-stutter of 5-15 cm), then pushes again in the same direction. The hesitation adds 1-2 strides without adding distance. It is caused by uncertainty — the player is not committed to the direction and briefly pauses to re-confirm. The fix: commit to the first push; the split-step and visual read (ART-0101) should provide enough information that no mid-push hesitation is needed.

**2. The over-shuffle.** After arriving at the recovery target, the player continues shuffling — small lateral adjustments that add 2-5 strides without improving the position. This is caused by anxiety about being "not quite right" at arrival. The fix: use the target-zone landing protocol (ART-0183) to establish a clear arrival point, and cue "stop on the target" to eliminate post-arrival shuffling.

**3. The directional reversal.** The player pushes in one direction, reverses, and pushes back — covering the same ground twice. This occurs when the player misreads the ball early (pushes the wrong way) or over-commits to a direction and then corrects. The fix: improve split-step timing and directional accuracy (ART-0101, ART-0104) so the first push is correct; the reversal is a symptom of a read error, not a movement error.

**4. The stance-reset shuffle.** Between shots during a static rally (both players roughly centered), the player performs 2-4 small shuffles to "reset" their stance. These add no positioning value — the player is already where they need to be. The fix: eliminate the reset; if the player is within 30 cm of the ideal ready position, no shuffle is needed.

**5. The split-step double-tap.** The player performs a split-step, lands, and then performs a second small split-step before the first directional push. This is a timing error — the second split-step is the body's attempt to re-synchronize when the first split-step was mistimed. The fix: single, committed split-step with a clear directional intent (ART-0101); eliminate the second tap.

## Efficiency Benchmarks

Stride counting is the measurement tool. Film a player from the side during a rally and count the total number of ground contacts (foot plants) per point. Subtract the strokes (each stroke involves a ground contact for the hitting stance). The remainder is the movement stride count.

**Baseline benchmarks for an 8-shot rally (singles, moderate court coverage):**

| Level | Total Movement Strides | Redundant Strides (estimated) | Efficiency Rating |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Elite professional | 28-36 | 2-6 | Excellent |
| Advanced competitive | 36-45 | 6-15 | Good |
| Intermediate recreational | 45-58 | 15-28 | Fair |
| Beginner | 58-75 | 28-45 | Poor |

The goal for an advanced competitive player is to reduce the redundant stride count from 6-15 to 2-6 per rally — a reduction of roughly 10-20% in total stride count. This is achievable within 4-6 weeks of targeted drill work.

## How Stride Economy Extends Into Third Sets

Fatigue in tennis is not merely cardiovascular — it is neuromuscular. Each stride requires eccentric and concentric loading of the quadriceps, calves, hip stabilizers, and core. Players who run redundant strides in the first set deplete their eccentric reserves, and by the third set, the same movement patterns produce slower times, lower jump heights, and wider landing scatter (see ART-0120 and ART-0187).

The energy saved through stride economy is not just metabolic — it is mechanical. Eccentric loading of the braking muscles (quads, calves, hip extensors) is the primary source of delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and of the "heavy legs" feeling in later sets. Players who brake 10-20% less often (because they arrive with less residual velocity, thanks to better COM control and less over-shuffling) preserve their eccentric capacity for the later sets.

Practical implication: a player who eliminates 10 redundant strides per point across a 12-game set saves roughly 120 strides of eccentric loading — equivalent to roughly 1.5 km of unnecessary running. This is the difference between a player who moves well in the third set and one who is visibly laboring.

## Training Stride Economy

1. **Stride counting with feedback.** Film a player from the side during a rally. Count the total strides and the estimated redundant strides. Give the player their number after every 5-point block. Players who see their count (e.g., "42 strides in that rally") are motivated to reduce it. Over 3-4 sessions, most players reduce their count by 10-15% through awareness alone.

2. **The "one-step" rule.** In practice rallies, the player is allowed only one adjustment shuffle after arriving at the ready position. Any additional shuffles result in a point penalty (the coach calls "extra step" and the point is restarted). This creates immediate accountability for over-shuffling.

3. **Constraint-based rallies.** Reduce the available court width by placing cones 1 m inside each singles sideline. The player must cover a narrower court, which reduces the total movement demand and forces more efficient path selection. As the player's economy improves, gradually widen the cones to the full court width.

4. **Economy sprints.** Time a player over a standard 5-point rally at moderate pace. The goal is not maximum speed but minimum time with minimum stride count. Time and stride count are recorded; the player is challenged to reduce the stride count while maintaining or improving the time.

## Diagnostic & Training Matrix

| Observation | Failure | Result | Corrective Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 3+ small shuffles after arriving at ready position | Over-shuffling; anxiety about exact position | 2-5 redundant strides per rally; energy waste | "One-step" rule; target-zone landing with "stop on target" cue |
| Player hesitates mid-push, then re-pushes same direction | Double stutter; directional uncertainty | 1-2 redundant strides per hesitation; rhythm break | Commitment cue: "decide and go"; split-step timing drill to improve first-read accuracy |
| Player covers same ground twice in one recovery | Directional reversal; misread | 2-6 redundant strides per reversal | Improve split-step timing; pre-commit to the most likely direction based on court position |
| Stride count increases 15-25% in second set | Fatigue amplifies redundant patterns | Accelerated energy depletion; third-set fade | Stride counting with feedback in the first set; awareness reduces second-set creep |
| Player performs a second split-step before directional push | Split-step double-tap; timing desynchronization | 1 redundant stride; 80-150 ms delay | Single-split drill; eliminate the second tap; cue "land and go, no bounce" |
| Stride count varies >30% between similar-length rallies | Inconsistent movement patterns | Unpredictable energy expenditure; fatigue management failure | Film and compare high-count vs. low-count rallies; identify the specific redundant pattern in the high-count version |

## Practical Application: "Count Your Strides"

At the next session, film a player from the side during a 10-point rally at moderate pace. Count the total movement strides (excluding stroke contacts) and the estimated redundant strides. Give the player the numbers: "You took 48 strides to play those 10 points, and roughly 14 of them were redundant." Then run the "one-step" rule drill for 10 minutes — any extra shuffle after arrival restarts the point. Re-film another 10-point block. The stride count typically drops by 10-20% within the session. This is the most direct and measurable way to improve footwork economy, and players can feel the difference immediately — the same work output, less fatigue.
