Pendulums, Gravity, and Arcs: Part 2

The very best writer on this topic is Rolf Clark in his wonderful book "Breakthrough Tennis". Chapter 10 is entitled "Timing and Skinny Twelve-Year Olds" and contains the best description of timing I have come across. He starts out by saying that timing is really about letting things move in their most natural way. He uses the example of a child on a playground vung vợt: "You wouldn't push when the vung vợt is still coming towards you, nor stop it at the top of its arc and then giao lại it go. Those disrupt the rhythm. Instead, you help the vung vợt move its own natural way" (75).

I mentioned on the previous page that the cú đánh actually begins as you start to lift the vợt and arm upward and backward in a gradual arc. This gradual motion upward and backwards begins the continuous flow that peaks at the top of the đưa vợt ra sau before falling with the aid of gravity. Rolf uses Newton's Law of Motion to explain why top players do this, rather than "take the vợt back right away" as all the instructors tell us to do.

"Recall Newton's Law of Motion: For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. To roll a bowling ball down the khu vực đôi, you first vung vợt it back behind you, then vung vợt it forward and then giao lại it go. There is no delay between the action and reaction. The action causes the reaction. A delay would interfere with the smoothness of the chuỗi. In tennis you vung vợt the vợt back so you can vung vợt it forward, neither rushing nor delaying. That's action and reaction at work. If you bring your vợt back too early, you end up delaying the forward vung vợt while you wait for the ball. If you phản ứng late to the ball you compensate by pulling the vợt back suddenly and then forcing it forward. The result is poor timing and a poorly hit ball. You wouldn't and couldn't do that with the bowling ball. The ball is too heavy. You vung vợt it back and giao lại it vung vợt forward when it's ready. If you don't -- if you delay it at the back of your vung vợt, or if you jerk it back too fast -- you'll hurt your vai.

If the vợt is to meet the ball at the right time, then the forward vung vợt must phản ứng naturally to the đưa vợt ra sau as Netwon says. It should follow the đưa vợt ra sau without delay. That, in turn, means the đưa vợt ra sau must start at the right time -- not too late, nor too early. Practice the action/reaction chuỗi at home. Hold the vợt in front of you and imagine a ball coming at you. Bring the vợt back and then vung vợt it forward to meet the mental ball with natural vung vợt timing." (82)

-Rolf Clark
Breakthrough Tennis
