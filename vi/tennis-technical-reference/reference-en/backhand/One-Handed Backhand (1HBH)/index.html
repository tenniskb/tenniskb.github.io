The One-Handed Backhand — Technical Reference

The one-handed backhand is arguably the most demanding groundstroke in tennis to execute well — an open kinetic chain that needs a highly specific upper-body coil, a precise release of your hitting arm, and careful sideways positioning all the way through your contact zone. It's declined steadily at the elite level since 2000, as the two-hander's structural advantages against heavy, high-bouncing topspin became decisive. But the players still using it — in Federer's lineage: Musetti, Tsitsipas, Dimitrov, Thiem — have adapted it to survive in a game it wasn't originally built for.

Why It's Still Worth Having

The one-hander keeps three real advantages the two-hander can't fully replicate. First, slice integration — the same grip and preparation can produce either a topspin drive or an attacking slice, giving you a disguise the two-hander simply can't match. Elite one-handers are genuinely bilingual, switching between topspin and slice from identical-looking preparation. Second, net game flexibility — the transition from a one-handed backhand into a Continental-grip volley is seamless, which makes one-handed players more naturally suited to attacking the net. Third, swing shape variety — the one-hander can produce heavy topspin, flat drives, and slices all from visually similar setups, creating a genuine anticipation problem for your opponent that the two-hander's more uniform mechanics can't create.

Stage 1: The Extreme Coil

Your setup here needs to go further than a forehand. Most modern one-handers use an Extreme Eastern or Semi-Western backhand grip — the heel of your hand sitting on top of the handle for natural support behind the ball. Your turn should rotate roughly 30% further than your forehand turn — your back nearly faces the net, and your chin rests on or over your front hitting shoulder. Your non-dominant hand holds the throat of the racket securely, pulling it back precisely and keeping the racket head high through the whole loading phase.

This deeper coil buys you real range — 90 to 120 degrees of shoulder-hip separation, significantly more than the two-hander's 45 to 60, because your open kinetic chain allows a much bigger range of motion. One serious caution: try to hit a one-handed backhand from an open stance, facing the net, and you lose access to your upper back muscles entirely — your forearm and elbow end up absorbing all the incoming force alone.

Stage 2: The Upper Back Is Your Engine

Your one-hander is driven primarily by your upper back and abdominals — not your arm. Elite players coil their torso during preparation and unwind it explosively; Federer used almost pure upper-back rotation with minimal arm action, his arm simply following the already-rotating racket. Don't lead with your elbow, and don't rely on triceps extension to drive the shot — pulling forward with your arm muscles, elbow leading, is the central technical error here, and it's the direct pathway to tennis elbow. Hip rotation actually plays a secondary role on this stroke; Federer barely used hip rotation on his backhand at all — the torque came from his upper back.

Your contact point needs to happen well in front of your body — roughly a foot and a half ahead of your hips, arm nearly extended. This stroke is far less forgiving of late contact than any other groundstroke you hit. Late contact here means a blocked, popped-up ball, full stop. The cue worth internalizing: hit the ball when it feels a touch early.

Your Wrist and Elbow at Contact

Your wrist stays firm through contact — only slightly laid back, never sharply flexed. The angle between your forearm and racket stays around 90 degrees through the shot. Your racket head shouldn't "snap" at contact; keeping a steady wrist is what keeps your string face consistent and your contact fault-tolerant. Your elbow sits moderately bent at contact, around 90 degrees or less, and begins extending only as your racket passes the ball — that extension happens after impact, unfolding through your follow-through rather than firing toward the ball.

Your Finish Needs a Counterbalance

At the finish, your non-dominant arm extends backward as your hitting arm swings forward — this isn't just for looks, it's a real counterweight mechanism. Your non-dominant shoulder blade should pull visibly backward as your hitting arm swings forward. This controlled opposition is what keeps your body stable and prevents over-rotation. If both shoulder blades move in the same direction during your stroke, that counterweight has failed, and your shot will lack depth no matter how good your swing path looked.

Choosing Your Grip

Grip Best For Weakness
Eastern backhand Flat drives plus moderate topspin, easy net transition Less natural topspin on very high bounces
Semi-Western backhand Extra topspin, high-ball contact Sacrifices flat power, more arm load
Continental Slice, underspin, defensive chops Makes aggressive topspin very difficult

No player currently on tour uses Continental for a topspin one-hander. Eastern backhand is the right starting point.

Thiem's Outlier Approach, Worth Knowing About

Dominic Thiem's one-hander solved the stroke's two classic weaknesses — generating power off the back foot, and vulnerability to high balls — through extreme pre-stretch. Rather than keeping a bent elbow in preparation, he extended his hitting arm almost completely straight, a setup more associated with a two-hander's leverage. Combined with extreme core torque on his forward swing, this created a stretch-shortening cycle localized in his posterior shoulder that produced a violent racket-head snap through contact.

Where It Goes Wrong

An open stance eliminates your access to your upper back, leaving your elbow and forearm to absorb everything alone. Leading with your elbow instead of unwinding your torso produces weak contact and is the primary path to tennis elbow. Late contact — letting the ball pass your optimal zone in front of your hips before impact — causes a blocked, structural collapse. And over-rotating your shoulder into the shot, instead of letting your torso carry your arm, can produce real rotator cuff strain.
