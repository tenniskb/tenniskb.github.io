Roland Garros 1989. Stefan Edberg vs. Michael Chang. Semifinal.

Edberg là serve-and-volley player trên clay — surface không phải natural home của net game. Chang là baseline counter-puncher 17 tuổi.

Edberg không chỉ serve-and-volley. Anh ta approach-and-volley, transition-and-volley, chip-and-charge — bất cứ cơ hội nào để đến lưới với position tốt.

CHƯƠNG 24

24.1 Net Game Philosophy — Tại Sao Và Khi Nào

The Modern Net Game Mindset

Net game hiện đại không phải về "serve and volley every point" như 1980s. Đó là về selective, tactical net approach — lên lưới khi math works, khi conditions favor, khi opponent is off-balance.

Win percentage when reaching net (professional level): 65-75% Win percentage from baseline (neutral): 45-55%

The math is clear. Problem is most players don't approach net ENOUGH — or approach from wrong position at wrong time.

Three Reasons Players Avoid Net

Reason 1 — Fear of passing shots:

"Nếu tôi lên lưới, họ sẽ pass tôi."

Reality: Yes, sometimes. But win rate at net (65-75%) still significantly higher than baseline (45-55%). Even getting passed 30% of time, you win more overall by approaching more.

Reason 2 — Fear of lobs:

"Họ sẽ lob tôi."

Reality: Lobs are often weak when player is under pressure. Your overhead should handle most lobs. Move back appropriately, not too close to net.

Reason 3 — Poor volley confidence:

"Tôi không giỏi volley."

Reality: Most volley failures come from poor approach shot selection (leaving opponent comfortable) rather than volley technique itself. Better approach shot = easier volley.

When Net Approach Is Correct

Situation 1 — Opponent's ball is short:

Ball landing inside service line = approach territory. Shorter ball = stronger approach position = more difficult passing shot for opponent.

Situation 2 — Opponent is out of position:

Opponent behind baseline, wide, or off-balance = reduced passing shot quality = safe to approach.

Situation 3 — Opponent hits defensive ball:

High, slow, floating ball = approach time = net position available.

Situation 4 — You hit strong approach position:

Your approach shot forces opponent to hit from defensive position = approaching makes sense.

When NOT to approach:
  • Opponent is in good position with comfortable ball
  • You're off-balance approaching from weak position
  • Your approach shot is itself defensive (going to opponent's strength)

24.2 Approach Shot — The Foundation Of Everything

Why Approach Shot Determines Volley Quality

This is the most misunderstood concept in net game:

Most players focus on: Improving volley technique They should focus on: Improving approach shot quality

A great approach shot:

  • Lands deep (pushes opponent back)
  • Has pace or spin (opponent must generate own pace on return)
  • Is directed to opponent's weakness
  • Creates awkward contact height for opponent

A great approach shot makes the volley simple. A poor approach shot makes even excellent volley technique not enough.

Approach Shot Mechanics

General rule: Approach shot while moving forward (not from static position).

Why? Moving forward:

  • Closes distance to net faster
  • Creates forward momentum into volley position
  • Generates pace through body weight transfer
The approach shot swing:

Modified groundstroke — same mechanics but abbreviated follow-through (you need to move forward, not complete full swing and recover).

Contact: In front of body (same as normal groundstroke). Follow-through: Shortened. Punch through contact, follow through toward target, then immediate forward movement toward net.

Approach Shot Direction

The classic rule: Approach down the line.

Why:

  • You follow your shot to the net — covering the down-the-line angle you just created
  • Crosscourt return from opponent travels longer distance → more time for you to reach volley position
  • Crosscourt attempt from opponent is the harder shot from their position
When approach crosscourt:
  • Only when ball is so short and opponent so out of position that crosscourt is simply easier
  • When setting up specific pattern (approach crosscourt → move center → anticipate opposite)

Approach Depth And Spin

Deep is non-negotiable:

Approach landing within 1m of baseline = opponent pinned deep = difficult passing angle.

Approach landing at service line = opponent attacks from comfortable mid-court = easy passing shot.

Topspin approach:

Kicks high after bounce → opponent must hit from uncomfortable height → weaker pass.

Slice approach:

Stays low → opponent must bend and lift → difficult driving pass → often goes up in the air → easy volley.

Which to use:
  • Against opponent who struggles with high balls → topspin approach
  • Against opponent who struggles with low balls → slice approach
  • General: slice is safer for approach (lower error rate, harder for opponent)

24.3 Transition — Moving From Baseline To Net

The Critical Split Step At Service Line

When approaching net, many players run all the way to net without stopping. This is wrong.

The split step rule:

As you move toward net after approach shot, split step approximately AT SERVICE LINE as opponent begins to swing.

This split step:

  • Gives you moment to read direction of passing shot
  • Allows explosive first step to either side
  • Prevents running past ball while opponent changes direction

Without split step: You're committed to moving forward when opponent hits → if they change direction, you cannot react.

Closing Speed And Position

After approach shot: Walk? No. Jog? No. Sprint to volley position. Optimal volley position: 2-3m inside service line (not touching net). This position:
  • Eliminates wide passing angles
  • Lets you cover lob (can back up to hit overhead)
  • Gives you overhead capability
Too close to net (touching net area): Can't reach lobs. Wide passes go past you. Too far back (service line area): Passing angles wide open. Low balls difficult.

Reading The Approach Situation

As you close to net, read:

Opponent's position: Are they comfortable or scrambling? Opponent's preparation: Full unit turn (likely passing drive) or abbreviated (likely short ball or lob)? Ball trajectory: Coming fast (block volley) or slow (drive volley)? Open court: Where is the space?

All this reading happens in 1-2 seconds as you close. With practice, it becomes automatic.

24.4 Volley Decisions At The Net

The Volley Decision Tree

Not every volley is the same. Decision at net:

Ball above net level:

→ Attack. Put away. Aim for open court (usually deep crosscourt or sharp angle). Win the point.

Ball at net level:

→ Redirect. Cross-court to open space. Maintain net position. Set up next volley.

Ball below net level:

→ Defensive. Lift over net. Go deep and central. Reset to safer position.

Punch vs. Touch Volley

Punch volley (most common):
  • Ball coming fast
  • Need to redirect pace
  • Short backswing, forward punch through contact
  • Firm wrist, body weight into shot
Touch/drop volley (specialty shot):
  • Opponent deep behind baseline
  • Ball is comfortable, slow
  • Soft hands absorb pace
  • Aim just over net, minimal pace
  • Makes opponent sprint forward
Drive volley (aggressive):
  • High ball at comfortable height
  • Treat like groundstroke but abbreviated
  • Full unit turn possible
  • Attack with pace and direction

Volley Placement Logic

Aim for open court: If opponent is on left side of court, open court is right side. Aim at feet when opponent at net (doubles especially): Difficult half-volley. Change direction vs. continue direction:
  • If approaching down the line → volley crosscourt (changes direction → opens court)
  • If approach was crosscourt → volley down the line OR deep center
Deep vs. short:
  • Opponent moving backward → drop volley short draws them forward (they run twice)
  • Opponent moving forward → volley deep (behind them)

24.5 The Overhead — Weapon And Safety Net

Overhead Is Non-Negotiable At Net

When opponent lobs, you MUST be able to:

1. Read the lob early

2. Move back correctly

3. Execute overhead

Failure at any of these = lost point from what should be your advantage position.

Reading The Lob

Visual cues opponent will lob:
  • Open racket face preparation (face tilted upward)
  • Slower preparation (not full groundstroke coil)
  • Looking at net player position
  • Body under ball (not leaning into ball for drive)
Early reading allows:
  • Time to move back properly
  • Setting up good trophy position
  • Controlled, confident overhead

Overhead Movement

For lob over your left shoulder (right-handed):
  • Turn immediately (full body pivot)
  • Backpedal or side-skip back
  • Get underneath ball before it passes you
For lob directly over your head:
  • Back-pedal directly
  • Keep eyes on ball throughout
For very deep lob:
  • If can't reach for overhead, let it bounce
  • Hit off-bounce overhead (same mechanics, different setup)
  • Better than jumping and missing
The scissor kick:

For deep, moving lobes, use scissor kick — jump off outside leg, kick legs in air to create rotation, land on opposite leg. Allows hitting overhead while moving backward.

Overhead Mechanics Revisited

Contact point: In front of body, high. NOT behind head (causes net errors). Swing path: Up and FORWARD — not just down. Ball must clear net. Pronation: Same as serve. Snap through contact. Follow-through: Natural — don't stop at contact. Aim: Most effective overhead targets:
  • Deep crosscourt (longest distance from opponent)
  • Sharp angle (opponent cannot reach)
  • At opponent's feet if they're rushing net
Common mistake: Hitting overhead straight down (like smashing the ball). Ball hits net. Hit overhead with ANGLE — forward trajectory, like a hard flat serve.

24.6 Defending The Net — Lob Recovery And Passing Shot Prevention

Positioning To Prevent Passing Shots

Correct net position prevents the most common passing shots:

Service line = too far back:

Wide angles open. Opponent can pass cleanly without much effort.

Too close to net:

Lob over you easily. Wide angles still possible from extreme angles.

Optimal (2-3m inside service line):
  • Wide passes require excellent shots from opponent
  • Lob can be covered by retreating for overhead
  • You can cover both T and wide with one step
The bisection principle:

After approach shot, position yourself on the imaginary line bisecting the widest angles opponent can attempt. This minimizes maximum passing distance you must cover.

When Opponent Has You In Trouble

Opponent hits great passing shot → you can barely reach it:

→ Lob over opponent if they're at net

→ If no one at net, just get it back crosscourt deep

→ Reset point, don't try to win from defensive position

Opponent hits body shot while you're at net:

→ Use backhand volley to protect body

→ Redirect to safest direction (usually central/deep)

→ Recover position

You're caught between baseline and service line (no man's land):

→ Either immediately close to net (with split step)

→ OR immediately retreat to baseline

→ Never stay in no man's land — worst of both worlds

24.7 The Chip And Charge — Return Net Attack

What Is Chip And Charge

Chip and charge = return of serve → immediately rush net.

The chip: Low, sliced return at server's feet (as they come forward for Serve+1). The charge: Immediately move to net after chip return.

Purpose: Force server to hit difficult half-volley from low position → you inherit net position from which to finish.

When To Use Chip And Charge

Optimal situations:
  • Server has predictable serve pattern (you know where it's going)
  • Server rushes forward every serve (they want to get to net first — beat them)
  • Second serve (more time for chip, more predictable serve)
  • Grass or fast hard courts (lower bounce, easier chip)
  • Against server who is uncomfortable at net themselves
Less optimal:
  • First serve at full pace (hard to chip controlled return)
  • Clay (higher bounce, less effective chip)
  • Against net players (they're comfortable at net — you're attacking their strength)

Chip And Charge Execution

Step 1 — The chip:
  • Position: Move 1 step inside baseline before serve
  • Swing: Abbreviated slice return — short backswing, continental grip
  • Target: Low to server's feet (between service line and baseline)
  • NOT trying for winner — trying to force difficult low ball
Step 2 — The charge:
  • Move IMMEDIATELY as chip leaves racket
  • Sprint forward to split step position (service line)
  • Split step as server contacts chip return
  • React to half-volley/low return
Step 3 — The finish:
  • Server usually hits defensive, upward shot (from below net)
  • You receive comfortable volley at or above net level
  • Put away to open court

24.8 The Sneak Attack (S.A.B.R.)

Federer's Innovation

In 2015, Federer introduced a shot that revolutionized net game: S.A.B.R. (Sneak Attack By Roger).

What it is:

On second serve, returner waits behind baseline, then SPRINTS FORWARD as server tosses ball, takes ball aggressively on the rise just inside service line or at service line, and immediately moves to net.

Effect: Server doesn't know returner has moved forward until too late. Return arrives much earlier than expected — before server's Serve+1 position is established.

Why It Works

Timing disruption:

Server prepares for 2+ seconds before ball arrives at returner. Returner moving forward changes arrival time dramatically → server has less time for Serve+1.

Position confusion:

Server sees returner at baseline, plans Serve+1 to open court → returner is already at service line or closer → "open court" is now covered.

Psychological pressure:

Even when S.A.B.R. fails once, server knows it might come again → affects serve decisions.

S.A.B.R. Execution

Read: Watch for short or predictable second serves. Trigger: Begin moving forward as server TOSSES ball (not as ball comes over net — too late). Take on the rise: Contact ball before it reaches peak bounce. This is the key skill — requires practice. Move to net immediately: Don't stop — commit fully. Target: Low to server's feet if possible, or any direction that opens court for volley. This is advanced technique. Practice separately:
  • Practice stepping inside baseline and taking ball aggressively on the rise
  • Practice the sprint forward trigger timing
  • Combine only when both components are solid

24.9 Net Game In Doubles

Doubles Net Game Is Different

In doubles:

  • Net player is involved every point
  • Poaching (intercepting crosscourt balls) is standard
  • Communication with partner is essential
  • Formation adjustments are tactical weapons
The active net player:

In doubles, net player should be in split step as partner serves/returns, looking for balls to poach.

Poaching:

Moving across to intercept return player expects will go to partner.

When to poach:

  • You read return direction
  • Your partner's shot forces crosscourt ball
  • Score allows risk (0-30 down = conservative; 30-0 = can poach)
  • You and partner have signaled it (hand signal behind back — "I'm crossing")

Doubles Net Positions

One up, one back (transition):
  • After serve: Server stays back, partner at net
  • After return: Usually returner stays back, partner at net
Both up (attack):
  • Both players at net after short ball or when controlling point
  • Most difficult formation for opponents — smallest angles available
Both back (defense):
  • When severely under pressure
  • Temporarily — goal is to get one player back to net quickly

Net Player Roles In Doubles

Active intimidation:

Move toward center as serve is hit — forces returner to aim away from you → less return options.

Volley termination:

When ball comes to you, put away. These should be high-percentage puts — don't be fancy.

Communication:

After every point: "Mine" or "yours" signals. Before serve: hand signals for crossing plans.

24.10 Net Game On Different Surfaces

Grass — Natural Net Surface

Why grass favors net game:
  • Low bounce → groundstrokes less effective → passing shots harder
  • Fast surface → less time for returner to prepare passing shot
  • Ball stays low → volleys sit up less
Grass net game approach:
  • Serve and volley is viable (not default, but viable)
  • Chip and charge is particularly effective
  • Slice approach shot is best approach (stays very low on grass)
  • Overhead slightly different — ball doesn't bounce high, so let it bounce for overhead if lob is deep

Clay — Net Game Challenges

Why clay makes net game harder:
  • High bounce → topspin passes kick up at your body (difficult volley)
  • Slow surface → more time for opponent to prepare pass
  • Ball slows down → longer exchanges before short ball opportunity
Clay net game approach:
  • Selective approaches only — must have clear advantage
  • Deep topspin approach → opponent must hit from behind baseline → reduces pass quality
  • Anticipate lob heavily (clay players lob frequently)
  • Be willing to back up for overheads — don't get caught

Hard Courts — Standard Net Game

Moderate pace, moderate bounce. Hard court net approach:
  • Slice approach effective
  • Topspin approach also works
  • Speed of surface makes net position valuable (faster ball = harder pass for opponent)
  • Generally best surface for learning net game (most predictable)

24.11 Eight-Week Net Game Program

Week 1-2: Approach Shot Foundation

Session 1:

Feed short balls (landing inside service line). Player approaches down the line, moves to net, coach feeds volley. 20 repetitions.

Focus: Deep approach shot AND immediate movement to net.

Session 2:

Approach shot targets: Place cones in deep crosscourt and down the line corners. Approach and aim for cones. Track accuracy.

Session 3:

Approach + volley + overhead sequence: Approach → close to net → partner lobs → overhead. 15 sequences.

Week 3-4: Decision Making

Session 1:

Coach feeds balls of varying quality. Player decides: approach or stay back? Discuss decisions afterward.

Session 2:

Chip and charge practice: Against moderate second serves, chip return low → rush net. 20 repetitions.

Session 3:

Situational scenarios: Score 30-30, short ball in middle of court. What do you do? Approach down line? Crosscourt? Stay back? Execute the decision.

Week 5-6: Pressure And Competition

Session 1:

Live points: Server and returner play out points. Returner bonus point for any net approach. Encourages net game.

Session 2:

Passing shot challenge: Player at net, opponent at baseline. Opponent gets 3 attempts to pass. Track: how many passes successful? Target: hold net position 65%+ of time.

Session 3:

Full game practice with net game emphasis: Every short ball MUST be approached. No exceptions. Discuss results.

Week 7-8: Integration

Session 1:

Match play with approach stat tracking. Count: how many approaches made? How many points won at net? Calculate win rate.

Session 2:

Against different player types: Approach against aggressive baseliner, against net rusher, against defensive player. Adjust strategy for each.

Session 3:

Full match with target: minimum 5 net approaches per set. Review outcome.

24.12 Năm Lỗi Net Game Phổ Biến

Lỗi 1: Approaching On The Wrong Ball

Mô tả: Player approaches on deep, comfortable ball — opponent is in good position, can pass cleanly. Fix: Approach only on short balls, weak balls, or when opponent is out of position. Ask: "Is opponent comfortable?" If yes, don't approach.

Lỗi 2: Weak Approach Shot (Ball Too Short Or Too High)

Mô tả: Approach shot lands short (mid-court) or high (slow, floating) → opponent has comfortable mid-court ball → drives past you. Fix: Approach shot must be deep (within 1m of baseline) and low (topspin or slice). If you can't hit quality approach, don't approach.

Lỗi 3: Stopping In No Man's Land

Mô tả: After approach shot, player walks to service line and stops — neither close enough to net to cut angles, nor back enough to handle deep ball. Fix: Split step AT service line as opponent swings → move decisively toward net after split step. Never park at service line — it's worst of both worlds.

Lỗi 4: Volley Goes Too Soft Or Too Short

Mô tả: Player reaches net but volley lands at service line → opponent runs and passes. Fix: Volley target is deep (within 1m of baseline) unless executing drop volley intentionally. Punch through volley with forward motion.

Lỗi 5: Getting Lobbed Repeatedly And Not Adjusting

Mô tả: Player gets lobbed → overhead miss → next point approaches net same way → lobbed again → cycle repeats. Fix: After two successful lobs, adjust: (1) don't close as tight to net, (2) overhead practice needed, OR (3) stop approaching until overhead is reliable.

Tóm Tắt Chương 24

  • Net game philosophy: Selective, tactical, mathematical — approach when conditions favor, not randomly
  • Why players avoid net: Fear of passes, lobs, poor volley confidence — all addressable. Win rate at net (65-75%) justifies approach.
  • Approach shot is most important: Quality approach = easy volley. Poor approach = impossible volley. Focus here first.
  • Approach mechanics: Down the line (default), moving forward, deep and with spin, abbreviated follow-through.
  • Transition: Sprint toward net after approach. Split step at service line as opponent swings. Close to 2-3m inside service line — not touching net, not at service line.
  • Volley decisions: Above net = attack; at net = redirect; below net = defensive reset.
  • Overhead: Critical skill. Read lob early, move correctly, contact in front and above, swing up and forward, not straight down.
  • Chip and charge: Return → immediate net rush. Effective on fast courts, second serves, against serve-and-volleyers.
  • S.A.B.R.: Sneak attack — move forward during toss, take ball on rise, rush net. Advanced but high-reward.
  • Key insight: Net game is about the approach shot, not the volley. Fix approach quality first — volley becomes simple.
