Chương 28: Tennis Của Hai Người

> "Doubles không phải là hai người chơi singles bên cạnh nhau. Đó là một organism với bốn chân, bốn tay, và một bộ não."

> — John McEnroe

Wimbledon 2000. John McEnroe và Pete Sampras. Legends doubles exhibition.

Khán giả expect spectacular singles-style shotmaking — big serves, crushing groundstrokes.

Điều họ thấy khác hơn. McEnroe và Sampras di chuyển như một unit. Khi Sampras serve, McEnroe đã ở vị trí perfect để intercept. Khi một người bị kéo wide, người kia đã cover center. Communication bằng tay signals trước mỗi point.

Đây là điều tách doubles ra khỏi singles: coordination, positioning, và communication quan trọng hơn individual brilliance.

Chapter này là guide hoàn chỉnh cho doubles — từ formation basics đến advanced strategy cho competitive play.

CHƯƠNG 28

28.1 Tại Sao Doubles Là Game Khác Hoàn Toàn

Những Khác Biệt Cốt Lõi

Court width:

Doubles court rộng hơn singles — 10.97m vs. 8.23m. Thêm 2.74m mỗi bên (alley).

Implication: Angles rộng hơn, net coverage quan trọng hơn, và volleys thường xuyên hơn. Net presence:

Trong doubles, thường có 2-3 người tại hoặc gần net ở bất kỳ thời điểm nào. So với singles thường chỉ 0-1.

Implication: Ground rallies ngắn hơn. Points kết thúc nhanh hơn — thường sau 3-5 balls. Serve importance:

Serve trong doubles ít dominant hơn singles vì court rộng hơn, returns thường chơi với less pace vì returner cần target net player.

Implication: Serve placement quan trọng hơn raw speed. Teamwork:

Quyết định tactical không phải của một người — mà của hai người acting as unit.

Implication: Communication, trust, và shared strategy là essential.

The Two-Person Organism Concept

Tốt nhất khi nghĩ doubles partnership như một organism với hai bodies.

The organism's goals:

1. Control the net (together)

2. Create pressure with two people threatening

2. Eliminate "no man's land" — never have one person at net, one at baseline

The organism's movement rules:
  • When one person moves left → other covers right
  • When one person moves forward → other may follow or hold
  • When serving → net partner positions to cut off angles

28.2 Court Positioning — The Foundation

The Four Basic Positions

Both back (2-back):

Both players at baseline. Purely defensive formation.

When to use:

  • Receiving wide serves
  • When opponents are at net and you're in trouble
  • Emergency reset when both caught out of position

Weakness: Opponents at net have full advantage. Don't stay here long.

One up, one back (standard formation):

Server or returner at baseline, partner at net (service line area).

This is the DEFAULT starting formation in doubles.

Both up (2-up):

Both players at or inside service line.

When to use:

  • After approach shot
  • After strong first volley
  • When opponents give you short ball
  • During point when you've moved forward together

Advantage: Angles are devastating from this position. Pressure is maximum.

I-formation:

Both server and net partner aligned on center service line, creating confusion about where net player will move.

When to use:

  • To disrupt returner's rhythm
  • When normal formation has been read by opponents

Ideal Net Position In Doubles

Net player in standard formation should stand:

Laterally: Approximately 1-1.5m from center service line (not against the alley). Depth: Inside the service box — approximately halfway between service line and net. Not pressed against net (lob risk), not at service line (too deep to volley). This position:
  • Allows movement toward alley to cut off wide shots
  • Covers center passing lane
  • Close enough to put away volleys
  • Far enough back to cover lob (take two steps back, jump)
Common mistake: Net player stands too close to net (can't recover lob) OR too close to alley (center is exposed).

28.3 The Server's Team — Tactics And Systems

Serve Placement In Doubles

Serve placement in doubles is different from singles strategy.

Primary serve targets in doubles: Deuce court:
  • T (center): Limits returner's angle. Return must come back to center where net partner can intercept. MOST EFFECTIVE in doubles.
  • Wide: Pulls returner wide, opens center. But net player must cover center.
  • Body: Jams returner. Often produces weak return.
Ad court:
  • T (center): Same benefit — limits angle, sets up net player.
  • Wide to backhand: Many players have weaker backhand return. Produces shorter, softer returns.
  • Body: Same as deuce — jams returner.
In doubles, T serve to center is generally more valuable than wide because it limits the return angle options and sets up net player for easy volley.

The Net Player's Role When Serving

Net player in doubles has TWO jobs when partner serves:

Job 1 — Intercept (poaching):

Move across center to intercept return before it gets to serving partner.

Job 2 — Cover:

If return goes to serving partner (not intercepted), net player adjusts position to cover.

The fundamental rule: Net player must be ACTIVE, not static.

Static net player = wasted resource. They should be threatening, moving, creating doubt in returner's mind.

The Serve-And-Follow System

Advanced doubles teams: Server serves AND follows serve to net.

Sequence:

1. Server hits serve

2. Serve bounces, server immediately moves forward

3. Return is hit

4. Server hits first volley (usually between service line and net)

5. Both players are now at net

This is the most aggressive doubles formation — and most effective at high levels. Requirements:
  • Strong, deep first serve (to get short or weak return)
  • Confident first volley under pressure
  • Good partnership communication (net partner moves to give server room)
When NOT to serve-and-follow:
  • Second serve (usually better to stay back initially)
  • If returner is very aggressive and can drive through your approach
  • If net player is poaching (they cross — you don't follow)

28.4 Poaching — The Art Of Interception

What Is Poaching?

Poaching = net player crosses center and intercepts return before it reaches partner.

When done correctly, poaching:

  • Creates easy put-away volley
  • Surprises returner
  • Changes returner's focus (they now must think about net player, not just server)
  • Psychologically disrupts the returning team

Poaching Mechanics

When to go:
  • As return is being HIT (not before — premature poach telegraphs movement)
  • When you read the return direction based on returner's setup
  • When your partner's serve has pushed returner off-balance
Movement:
  • Two steps diagonal forward-across (not sideways only)
  • End position: In front of opponent's return path
  • Racket ready at shoulder height
The shot:
  • First choice: Angle volley away from opponents
  • Second choice: Drive volley through or at players
  • Third choice: If too low → deep to baseline (reset)

Signaling Systems

High-level doubles teams use signals to coordinate poaching.

Basic signal system (net player signals to server before each point):

Behind back, net player signals:

  • Closed fist (or one finger pointing down): Net player STAYS — no poach
  • Open hand (or one finger pointing across): Net player POACHES — crosses on serve
  • Two fingers or specific signal: I-formation start
Why signals matter:

If net player poaches without server knowing → server takes wrong path → both players converge on same spot → center is exposed.

Signals ensure server knows to cover the side net player is leaving.

Signal sequence:

1. Before point, net player gives signal to server (hand behind back, server looks)

2. Server nods or signals acknowledgment

3. Point starts

This takes 2-3 seconds and completely transforms team coordination.

Planned vs. Spontaneous Poaching

Planned poach (signal based):

Net player commits to crossing BEFORE point starts. When return is hit → goes regardless.

Risk: If return goes down the line (away from poach direction) → open court.

Reward: High success when return is crosscourt (as expected).

Spontaneous poach (read-based):

Net player reads return as it's happening → decides to cross.

Risk: Reaction time limited, may not reach.

Reward: Lower risk than planned poach (only go if clearly correct).

Fake poach:

Net player starts moving as if poaching → stops → returns to position.

Goal: Disrupt returner's aim. If returner saw movement and aimed down the line → they hit into alley (out) or into your partner's zone.

28.5 The Returning Team — Tactics And Systems

Return Targets In Doubles

Returns in doubles have specific optimal targets:

Target 1 — At the net player's feet (low, crosscourt):

Best return. Ball is low, forcing net player to hit UP (weak volley). Returner's partner can then attack the weak volley.

Target 2 — Down the line past the net player:

High risk, high reward. If successful → server's partner open court wide.

Risk: Net player often covering this, may intercept.

Target 3 — At the net player's body:

Effective. Jams net player, making clean volley difficult. Return landing near hip/shoulder area forces awkward volley.

Target 4 — Lob over net player:

Use when net player is pressed close to net. High lob over their head forces them to turn, run back, and gives returner's team time.

Target 5 — Deep to server (crosscourt):

Safe. Gets return in, forces server to play first ball from baseline. Gives returner's team time.

Priority: Target 1 (at feet) should be default. Then Target 5 (deep crosscourt) as safety. Target 2 (down the line) only when net player leaves that side open.

Positioning For The Returner's Partner

Returner's partner at net should:

Start position: Inside service box, halfway between service line and net. Slightly favoring center. During return:
  • If return is quality (low, good target) → move forward aggressively
  • If return is weak → prepare to defend (step back slightly, be ready for put-away volley)
After return:
  • Quality volley by opponent → defend or lob
  • Weak volley by opponent → attack

Return-And-Stay vs. Return-And-Follow

Return-and-stay:

Returner hits return and stays at baseline.

When to use:

  • Second serve that you couldn't attack
  • When server is charging net (don't get caught in no-man's land)
Return-and-follow:

Returner hits aggressive return and moves forward.

When to use:

  • When you can drive return with pace and depth
  • When server is staying back
  • When your partner is already at net (form 2-up quickly)

28.6 Formations — The Tactical Tools

Standard Formation (One Up, One Back)

Most common. Server or returner at baseline, partner at net.

Strengths:
  • Safe and reliable
  • Clear roles
  • Easy coordination
Weaknesses:
  • Predictable
  • Experienced opponents read it easily
  • Net player can be lobbed

The I-Formation

Both server and net partner set up aligned along center service line.

Net partner is in center crouching or kneeling (so returner can't see them clearly).

How it works:

1. Net player and server agree: Net player goes LEFT or goes RIGHT after serve

2. Server serves (usually T or body — wide is dangerous with I-formation)

3. As ball is served, net player moves to their decided side

4. Serve player covers the opposite side

What returner sees:

Confusion. They don't know which direction net player will go. This disrupts their aim.

Effectiveness: Very high when executed cleanly. Difficult to "set up" against. Requirements: Practice together. Poor execution of I-formation = both players on same side.

Australian Formation

Both players set up on the SAME side of the center service line.

Setup:

Deuce court: Server stands normally. Net player stands on deuce side (same side as server, NOT the normal crosscourt position).

Why:

Forces returner to either:

A) Hit down the line (more difficult angle, into net player's side)

B) Hit behind the server (unexpected direction)

Most effective against: Returners with strong crosscourt return. Australian formation takes away their favorite target.

The Return I-Formation

Returner's team can also use I-formation.

Same concept as server's I-formation — returner's partner positions at center, then goes one direction as return is hit.

Used when: Serving team has strong poaching net player. This disrupts the poach by making it unclear where returner's partner will end up.

28.7 Communication Patterns

Between-Point Communication

Effective doubles communication is brief, specific, and positive.

What to say:
  • Tactical adjustment: "Let's take the T serve" / "I'm going to poach next point"
  • Encouragement: "Good point, let's keep going"
  • Reset: "Let's both focus, our best tennis right here"
What NOT to say:
  • Blaming: "Why didn't you poach that?" / "Your serve was too weak"
  • Long explanations: Save analysis for changeover
  • Negative predictions: "They're too good today"

Changeover Communication

90 seconds at changeover allows real tactical discussion.

Structure (60 seconds of the 90 used for this):

First 20 seconds: Partner 1 observes

"Their net player is very aggressive — we should lob them more."

Next 20 seconds: Partner 2 adds

"Also their ad-court serve is always kick wide — let's shade that direction."

Final 20 seconds: Agree on ONE adjustment

"Okay, this game: lob their net player once, and shade wide on ad court. Agreed?"

One adjustment at a time. Two players cannot implement three changes simultaneously. One clear focus is executable.

Mid-Point Communication

Legal communication only: Talking between points is fine. Talking during points is not allowed. Eye contact: Partners should make brief eye contact after difficult exchanges — "I see you, I'm with you." Body language: Positive body language from partner matters. Slumped shoulders and visible frustration drains partner's energy. Stand tall, look forward.

28.8 Targeting The Weaker Player

The Math Of Targeting

In doubles, if one partner is meaningfully weaker than the other, targeting them is standard, legitimate, high-percentage tactics.

Why it works:
  • More errors from weaker player
  • Their partner cannot compensate (different positions)
  • Creates psychological pressure on weaker player
How to target without being obvious about it:

In patterns, not constantly — mix up occasionally so opponent doesn't fully read your strategy.

For example: Serve to stronger player's side BUT direct follow-up rally to weaker player's side.

The "Around The Net Player" Tactic

Against a very dominant net player:

Avoid feeding them. Direct shots AT THE SERVER or AROUND the net player.

Options:
  • Lob over the dominant net player
  • Drive return down the line past them (when they shade crosscourt)
  • Body shot at dominant net player (forces defensive awkward volley)

28.9 Net Play — Finishing Points In Doubles

The Doubles Volley Is Different

In singles, volleys are often first volleys or passing shot exchanges.

In doubles, volleys often occur with:

  • 2-3 people at or near net simultaneously
  • Fast exchange within 5 meters
  • Angles tighter and reactions faster
The key difference: Doubles net exchanges are more about positioning and cut-off angles than purely technical volley quality.

Putting Volleys Away In Doubles

When you have open court put-away volley in doubles:

Best targets:

1. Down at their feet (low, near baseline)

2. Sharp angle past the outside player (into alley)

3. Between both players (splits them)

4. At the closer player's body

Common mistake: Going for winner at extreme angle from close distance. Often clips net or goes wide. High percentage put-away: Drive through the middle of both opponents or down at the closer one's feet. These angles are more reliable than extreme crosscourt when very close to net.

The Overhead In Doubles

Lob goes over you → partner must call "mine" or you call it.

Communication rule: Whoever calls it, plays it. Default: Person who can hit overhead (not backing up away from smash) calls it. Where to hit overhead in doubles:
  • NOT straight at net player
  • Between both opponents (splits them)
  • At the slower-moving opponent
  • Short angle to alley

28.10 Mental Partnership — Trust And Resilience

When One Partner Is Struggling

Doubles is 50% tennis, 50% partnership management.

When your partner is having a difficult match:

What NOT to do:
  • Avoid them (they need partnership, not isolation)
  • Give technical instruction mid-match (they already know)
  • Show disappointment
What TO do:
  • Touch rackets between points (nonverbal "I'm with you")
  • Take more serves, more balls when possible
  • Short positive phrase: "Let's play our game, we've got this"

When YOU Are Struggling

Communicate openly:

"My serve isn't working today — let's think of how to compensate."

Better to acknowledge and adjust together than pretend and continue struggling.

Momentum Management As A Team

When behind:

  • Discuss and agree on one tactical change
  • Play one point at a time — verbalize it: "Just this point"
  • Positive body language even when behind

When ahead:

  • DON'T relax too early
  • Maintain routines
  • Short check-in: "Let's keep executing"
The most common doubles collapse: Team wins first set comfortably → becomes conservative → loses second set → momentum lost.

Fix: When winning → maintain aggression. Don't wait for opponents to get back into it.

28.11 Surface-Specific Doubles Tactics

Fast Courts (Grass, Fast Hard)

Characteristics: Ball stays low, skids. Points shorter. Implications:
  • Serve-and-follow system more effective (less time for returner)
  • First volley is critical
  • Low returns at feet are very effective
  • 2-up formation wins more points
Strategy: Maximize net presence. Serve and follow as much as possible.

Medium Courts (Medium Hard, Carpet)

Characteristics: Balanced. Both baseline and net effective. Implications:
  • Mix of formations viable
  • Both serve-stay and serve-follow work
  • Tactical diversity rewarded
Strategy: Read opponents, adapt. No single dominant formation.

Slow Courts (Clay)

Characteristics: High bounce. Points longer. Baseline dominates. Implications:
  • Lobs more effective (high bounce after lob, harder to smash)
  • Groundstrokes from baseline can stay competitive
  • Poaching less common (more time for returner to redirect)
Strategy: More patience. Net play still important but lobs neutralize aggressive net players. Returns can be driven more since slower surface gives more time.

28.12 Doubles-Specific Drills

Drill 1 — Signal Poaching Practice

Setup: Server + net partner + two opponents. Execution:

Before every serve, net partner signals (stay or poach).

Server serves, net partner executes their signal.

Goal: 20 serves with correct coordination. No confusion about who covers what. Progress: Track how many poaches were successful (interception that led to winning point or weak reply).

Drill 2 — Return At The Feet

Setup: Server serves, net player in position. Returner focuses ONLY on low returns at net player's feet. Goal: 20 serves, count how many returns are low (below net height at net player's position). Scoring:
  • Low return: 2 points
  • Medium height return: 1 point
  • High return or error: 0 points
Progress: Should see improvement in consistency of low returns within 2-3 sessions.

Drill 3 — 2-Up Formation Practice

Setup: Two teams. After any groundstroke that's comfortable → both players move up together. Goal: Comfort in 2-up formation, understanding of angles, volley exchanges at net. Focus: Move together (not one person forward, one back). Communicate "up, up" when moving.

Drill 4 — Communication Drill

Setup: Full doubles point play. Rule: Before each point, both partners must verbalize their intention:

"I'm serving T, you poach."

"I'm staying back after return."

After each point: Both partners briefly confirm: "Good execution" or "Let's adjust: [specific thing]." Goal: Build communication habit. Make talking natural.

28.13 Eight-Week Doubles Development Program

Week 1-2: Positioning Fundamentals

Focus: Master basic positioning for server's team and returning team. Practice:
  • Walk through positions without hitting: Where should each player be for each formation?
  • Shadow movement: Partner serves (no ball), net partner practices moving to poach position and covering.
Goal: Both players automatically move to correct position without thinking.

Week 3-4: Signal System Implementation

Focus: Implement signal system for poaching. Practice:
  • Choose simple 2-signal system (stay/go).
  • Practice signals in every service game during training.
  • Track: How often do you use signals? How often does poach succeed?
Goal: Signal before every serve. No more reactive, uncoordinated poaching.

Week 5-6: Return Systems

Focus: Return targeting and returner's partner movement. Practice:
  • Return-at-feet drill (Drill 2 above) — 3 sessions.
  • Live points focusing on returner's partner movement (move forward on good return, defend on weak return).
Goal: Returns are targeted, not random. Returner's partner is active, not static.

Week 7-8: Full Match Integration

Focus: Put everything together in competitive practice matches. Metrics to track:
  • % of serves preceded by signal
  • # of successful poaches per set
  • % of returns at or below net height at net player
  • Net points won % (both teams at net — who wins more?)
Goal: Conscious execution of all systems. Communication feels natural.

28.14 Năm Lỗi Doubles Phổ Biến

Lỗi 1: Both Players Staying At Baseline

Mô tả: Both partners retreat to baseline whenever under pressure. Never attack net together. Opponents volley freely. Fix: At minimum, after every approach, one player must go to net. After quality return, returner follows to create 2-up. Baseline-only doubles gives away free points.

Lỗi 2: Net Player Is Statue

Mô tả: Net player stands still, doesn't poach, doesn't fake, doesn't move. Returner ignores them completely. Fix: Net player must be active threat every point — even if not poaching, move forward, fake, threaten. Make returner THINK about you.

Lỗi 3: Returning Into The Net Player

Mô tả: Returner hits medium-height return crosscourt, directly to waiting net player. Easy put-away. Fix: Returns must be LOW (at feet) or DOWN THE LINE or LOB. Never medium-height crosscourt to a standing net player.

Lỗi 4: No Communication Between Partners

Mô tả: Two players play like strangers. No signals, no changeover discussion, no encouragement. Each person plays their own singles game in their half. Fix: Implement at minimum: signal before every serve + one communication at changeover. Two minutes of planning per set transforms performance.

Lỗi 5: Targeting The Strong Player

Mô tả: Out of habit or discomfort, players keep hitting to the stronger opponent — feeding them easy balls. Fix: Identify weaker player immediately. Target them relentlessly. When in doubt, go to the weaker side.

CHƯƠNG 28

Tóm Tắt Chương 28

  • Doubles is a different game: Not singles with two people. Court geometry, point duration, positioning, and tactics are fundamentally different.
  • The two-person organism: Move together. No one up, one in no-man's land. When both are at net → maximum pressure.
  • Net player must be active: Signals, poaching, faking. Static net player gives opponent free crosscourt returns.
  • Return targeting: Primarily low at net player's feet. Down-the-line when opportunity. Lob when net player is aggressive. Never medium-height crosscourt to waiting net player.
  • Formations are tools: Standard formation is reliable. I-formation creates confusion. Australian formation disrupts strong crosscourt returners.
  • Communication transforms results: Signal system before every serve. Brief, specific changeover discussion. One adjustment at a time.
  • Target the weaker player: Legitimate, effective, standard doubles tactics.
  • Mental partnership: Support partner, maintain positive body language, communicate openly when struggling.
  • Key insight: A technically average team with excellent communication and tactical coordination will defeat a technically superior team with no coordination. Doubles is the most learnable discipline in tennis.
