Video & Self-Analysis Methodology Coaching Guide¶

A practical companion to Self-Coaching Discipline — that page covers the mindset and habits; this one covers the specific method.

Why This Matters¶

Self-Coaching Discipline establishes that improving without a coach present is possible and valuable. This guide gives the concrete tool that makes it actionable: a repeatable video self-analysis method, rather than vague "watch yourself play" advice.

Core Concepts¶

Break the stroke into phases, not one blur. Effective self-analysis compares your swing to a reference (a pro, or your own best-recorded rep) phase by phase: preparation/takeback, wind-up (unit turn, loading), force-generation (the actual acceleration through contact), and follow-through/recovery. Watching a stroke as one continuous motion makes it very hard to isolate what's actually different; watching phase by phase makes the comparison specific and actionable.

One phase, one session. Trying to fix the whole stroke from one video session produces information overload and no real change. Pick a single phase (say, the unit turn) as the focus for a full practice session or week, film before and after, and only move to the next phase once that one shows a measurable change.

Self vs. pro, not self vs. ideal. Compare your recorded rep against a pro reference at the same phase of the swing, at a similar shot type and situation (a pro's cross-court rally forehand, not their inside-out winner) — an apples-to-apples comparison is far more useful than comparing your rally shot to a highlight-reel winner.

Action Steps¶

  1. Film from two angles: side-on (for the kinetic chain and unit turn) and behind-the-baseline (for racket path and contact point) — a single angle hides too much information.
  2. Pick one phase per week to analyze and drill, and don't move to the next phase until you can see a repeatable difference on video.
  3. Build a small reference library of pro clips at the same phase you're working on, at rally-shot intensity rather than highlight-reel intensity, for a fair comparison.
  4. Re-film the same drill at the end of the session to check whether the change actually happened under fatigue, not just in the first few reps.

Common Mistakes¶

  • Watching full-speed, whole-stroke video and trying to fix everything at once.
  • Comparing your rally-shot form to a pro's highlight-reel winner, which sets an unfair and demotivating bar.
  • Filming once, drawing conclusions, and never re-checking whether the fix held up under match fatigue.

Quick Reference¶

Phase What to Look For
Preparation/takeback Timing relative to opponent's contact
Wind-up (unit turn) Shoulder/hip separation, loading
Force-generation Sequencing legs → hips → trunk → arm
Follow-through/recovery Balance, readiness for next shot
